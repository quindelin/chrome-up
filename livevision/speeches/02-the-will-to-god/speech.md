# The Will To God

## I. The Four Words

Friends, I want to begin with a moment that took no longer than a breath.

I was in college. I had spent a long stretch of nights reading philosophy, the kind of reading you do when you are young enough to believe the next book might be the one that tells you what all of this is for. I had read the Germans and the Greeks. I had read the men who said God was everything and the men who said God was dead. I had gone to bed with their arguments still running in my head like engines left on in a parking lot.

And one night, at that strange border between waking and sleep, where the mind loosens its grip and things come up from below, four words arrived.

[PAUSE]

The Will To God.

[LET IT LAND]

Not a sentence. Not an argument. Four words, in that order, as if someone had set them down on a table in front of me and walked out of the room.

I remember how odd it felt. It did not feel like something I had built. It felt like a synthesis that had built itself out of everything I had poured in, the way a crystal forms in a solution once the solution is saturated enough. Everything I had read seemed to have been leaning toward those words without ever saying them.

The first thing I did, of course, was what any of us would do. I got up and searched for them. I scoured the internet for anyone who had used that phrase in the sense I meant it. And I was dumbfounded. I could not find it. There were wills to live and wills to power and wills to believe. There was no Will To God, not in the way it had come to me.

So then came the years. Years of turning those four words over like a stone in the pocket. Years of asking what would follow if they were true, or if they were at least a plausible attitude toward the thing we all live inside every second of our lives, which is experience itself. The color of this room. The weight of your own body in that chair. The sound of my voice reaching you a fraction of a second after it leaves me.

Tonight I want to give you what those years produced. I call it the Esketian Metaphysics. And at its root are those four words.

[PAUSE]

Let me tell you why the phrase startled me, because the reason will tell you where we are going.

In eighteen eighty-two, Friedrich Nietzsche published a book he called The Gay Science, or in an older English translation, The Joyful Wisdom. In it there is a short scene that has become one of the most famous passages in modern philosophy. A madman lights a lantern on a bright morning and runs into the marketplace crying that he seeks God. The people standing around do not believe in God, and they laugh at him. Has God gone on a voyage, they ask. Has he emigrated?

And the madman turns on them and says that God is dead, and that we have killed him.

Most people stop the story there. But the madman keeps talking. He asks how we will console ourselves. He asks what sacred games we will have to invent. And then he asks a question that, to my ear, is the hinge of the last century and a half of Western thought. In Paul Cohn's translation, the madman asks:

"Shall we not ourselves have to become Gods, merely to seem worthy of it?"

[SLOW]

Shall we not ourselves have to become Gods.

Nietzsche put that sentence in the mouth of a lunatic, and then he spent the rest of his working life circling it. He never quite answered it. He gave us the will to power instead, and the overman, and the eternal return. But the question stayed there in the marketplace, lantern still burning in daylight, waiting.

I have come to believe that the four words I heard before sleep are an answer to the madman's question. Not the only answer. Not a final answer. But an answer.

The answer is this. We were never only creatures who received God. We are creatures who are becoming God, and the striving toward that becoming is the deepest drive in everything that lives. The madman thought the death of the old God left a hole in the sky. I think it left a direction.

[PAUSE]

Now, I know how that sounds. Some of you came here tonight curious. Some of you came skeptical. Some of you are wondering whether you are about to sit through two hours of a man telling you that he talked to the universe in his sleep.

So let me make you a promise at the start, in the spirit of the best speeches I know.

When Pericles stood up in Athens to honor the dead in the first winter of the Peloponnesian War, he did not begin by flattering his audience. He began by admitting how hard it is to speak well about something that matters, how a speaker risks saying too much for those who already believe and too little for those who do not. I feel that tonight. I am going to tell you something I believe with my whole heart. And I am also going to tell you, every time we reach it, where my belief runs ahead of what I can prove.

Where I am offering you an argument, I will say so. Where I am offering you an image, I will say so. Where I am offering you a reading of physics that I find beautiful and cannot defend, I will say that too, out loud, in so many words. A philosophy that cannot tell you where its own edges are is not a philosophy. It is a sales pitch. And I did not come here to sell you anything.

What I came to do is to stand where the madman stood, in the marketplace, in daylight, and try to answer him.

[BUILD]

I am going to take you through the thinkers who led me to those four words: Schopenhauer, who saw that the world is will. Nietzsche, who saw that the will is power. Spinoza, who saw that God and Nature are one. Hegel, who saw that the Absolute comes at the end. Leibniz, who saw that this is the best of all possible worlds. I am going to show you what each of them got right, and where each of them stopped. And then I am going to show you what I think lies past the place where they stopped.

And at the end, if I have done my job, you will not leave here believing in a new God.

You will leave here understanding that you are helping to make one.

## II. Let's Get It

Before we go to the philosophers, I owe you a word about the name.

Esketian. It is not Greek. It is not Latin. You will not find it in any encyclopedia of philosophy, and I will not pretend you should. It comes from a novella I wrote called Esketit. And Esketit comes from somewhere even less academic than that.

It comes from the street. It comes from the English turn of phrase "let's get it."

[PAUSE]

Some of you know the sound. In April of twenty eighteen, a young rapper named Lil Pump put out a single called "Esskeetit," and the title was his signature shout, a slurred and joyful compression of exactly those three words. Let's get it. It caught on. People laughed at it, and people chanted it.

And I heard something in it that I could not stop hearing.

Let's get it. Think about the grammar of that phrase. It is the grammar of the whole human condition compressed into a shout.

"Let us." First person, plural. Not "I." Not "you." Us. Whatever this is, we are doing it together.

"Get." A verb of motion, of reaching, of closing a gap. You do not get what you already have. You get what is ahead of you. The verb points forward in time.

"It." And here is the mystery. What is "it"? The phrase never says. Nobody shouting it in a parking lot stops to define the object. And yet everybody knows. Everybody who has ever said it, in a locker room before a game, in a studio before a take, in a kitchen at four in the morning before opening the restaurant, knows exactly what "it" is.

"It" is a state. It is not a thing you can put in your pocket. It is the feeling of the thing going right. It is the moment when the work and the will and the world line up. It is a phenomenological state, a condition of experience, and the phrase announces that we are going to go and reach it together.

[LET IT LAND]

That is the whole Esketian philosophy in miniature. We, together, reaching forward in time, toward a state of experience that we cannot fully name but that we can all feel calling us.

What I have spent years trying to do is ask what that "it" ultimately is. If you follow the phrase all the way out, past the game and the take and the restaurant, past every particular "it" any of us has ever chased, what is the final object of the getting? What is the "it" behind all the "its"?

My answer is the one that came to me before sleep. The final "it" is God.

[PAUSE]

I want to stay on this for a moment, because I think it matters that the name of this philosophy did not come from a seminar room.

There is an old prejudice that the deepest truths must arrive in the most elevated language. That God speaks in Latin and Sanskrit and the prose of the King James Bible, and never in the slang of a young rapper. I do not share that prejudice. I think if the Will To God is real, it should be audible everywhere. It should show up in hymns and in hip-hop, in the treatises of philosophers and the chants of a stadium crowd. If the drive I am describing is the deepest drive in everything that lives, then of course a kid with a microphone found a way to shout it. Of course it went viral. People recognized it before they could explain it.

Emerson, in his address on the American Scholar in eighteen thirty-seven, argued that the scholar should not be a bookworm, a mere reader of other men's thoughts, but what he called "Man Thinking," a person who takes in the whole of life, the market and the field and the street, and turns it into truth. He said the scholar should embrace the common, explore the familiar, and sit at the feet of the low. I take that seriously. The Esketian Metaphysics begins in the low and the familiar, in three words anyone can say, and tries to follow them up to the highest thing we can think.

So when I say "let's get it" tonight, and I will say it more than once, I want you to hear both registers at once. The shout and the prayer. The locker room and the cathedral. They are the same reaching.

[BUILD]

Let us. Get. It.

Us: because no one becomes God alone.

Get: because the Absolute is not behind us but ahead of us.

It: because what we are reaching for is not an object but a state of Being, a way the world is when it is fully known and fully lived.

Hold on to those three words. We are going to need them. Because the philosophers I am about to bring you each got hold of one piece of that phrase, and none of them got hold of all three.

## III. What We Mean by God

Now, if existence is a Will To God, the first and most important question is the obvious one. What, in this sense, is God?

I want to be careful here, because the word carries thousands of years of weight, and every one of you heard it tonight through your own history. Some of you heard your grandmother's church. Some of you heard a judge in the sky you stopped believing in at fourteen. Some of you heard nothing at all, just a word, like "phlogiston" or "ether," that used to mean something and now does not.

I am not asking you to drop any of that at the door. I am asking you to listen to a definition, and to judge it on its own terms.

[SLOW]

I call God the Absolute Existential Objective.

Let me take those three words one at a time.

Objective. Not an object, but an objective. A target. A destination. The thing that a process is moving toward. In the language of engineering, the objective is what the system is built to reach. In the language of strategy, it is the hill you are trying to take. God, in this sense, is not first of all something that exists back there, at the start, the way a creator exists before the creation. God is first of all what everything is moving toward.

Existential. The objective is not a proposition to be proved, or a reward to be handed out. It is a state of existence. It is about how it is to be. It concerns the thing every one of us knows from the inside and no one has ever seen from the outside: what it is like to be here, now, in space and time, experiencing.

Absolute. Not partial. Not local. Not one objective among many. The final one. The objective of all objectives, the "it" behind all the "its."

[PAUSE]

So what does that state look like? Here is the heart of it.

God represents a moment in which we know everything, and, through that knowledge, are immortal.

Or you can run it the other way, and I think the two directions are the same road traveled in opposite directions. Because we are immortal, it becomes inevitable that we would come to know everything.

Knowledge and endurance. Knowing and lasting. Those are the two halves of God, and I want you to keep them both in your hands for the rest of this speech, because almost everything I am going to say tonight depends on keeping them together. When we come, near the end, to what has gone wrong in our time, you will see that the counterfeits of God are always the ones that offer you one half while quietly stealing the other.

[PAUSE]

Why those two? Why knowledge and endurance, and not, say, love, or power, or pleasure?

Because I think, when you look at what living things actually do, all day, every day, those two are what everything else serves.

Consider the most basic thing you are doing right now. You are breathing. You did not decide to. Your body decided for you, and it decided because breathing sustains your existence in time. That is the endurance half, at its most primitive.

Consider what your eyes are doing. They are taking in light from this room, and your brain is turning it into a model of where you are, who is near you, what might happen next. That is the knowledge half, at its most primitive.

And notice that the two are not separate. You see so that you can survive. You survive so that you can keep seeing. Knowledge serves endurance and endurance serves knowledge. The loop runs in both directions, and it runs in every living thing, from bacteria swimming toward sugar to astronomers pointing mirrors at the edge of the observable universe.

[BUILD]

What I am claiming is that this loop has a limit, an asymptote, a place it is heading. And that place is the state where the knowing is complete and the lasting is unbroken.

That is what I mean by God.

Not a man with a beard. Not a tyrant. Not a vague warm feeling. A state of Being that represents the Absolute in terms of how we experience reality through space and time. The far end of the road that every breath, every meal, every question, every discovery has been walking.

[PAUSE]

Now, I can already hear the first objection, and it is a good one. You are saying, that is not God. That is just a goal. That is a projection of human wants onto the cosmos. You have taken the two things you happen to care about, knowing and living, and you have blown them up to infinity and called the result divine.

I take that objection seriously, and I will meet its strongest form when we get to Spinoza. But let me say this much now.

Every serious idea of God in human history has had these two features. The God of the philosophers is omniscient: that is the knowledge half. The God of the philosophers is eternal: that is the endurance half. The theologians did not invent these attributes out of nowhere. They were reaching for the same limit I am reaching for. The difference is in the direction of time.

The old picture put God at the beginning. All-knowing and eternal, already complete, before anything was made. And the problem with that picture, the problem that drove Nietzsche's madman into the marketplace, is that once you put a complete God at the beginning, it becomes very hard to explain why the world he made is so incomplete, so ignorant, so mortal, so full of suffering.

The Esketian picture puts God at the end. The knowing and the lasting are not finished. They are being achieved. And the incompleteness of the world is not a scandal that needs explaining away. It is simply what it looks like to be in the middle of the road.

[LET IT LAND]

God is not where we came from. God is where we are going.

And the Will To God is the name for the going.

## IV. The Blind Will

To understand why I say "will," we have to go back two hundred years, to a difficult and brilliant man in Germany named Arthur Schopenhauer.

Schopenhauer was born in Danzig in seventeen eighty-eight and died in Frankfurt in eighteen sixty. He finished his great work, The World as Will and Representation, in March of eighteen eighteen. It came out that December with eighteen nineteen on the title page. And for most of his life almost nobody read it. In Berlin he scheduled his lectures at the same hour as the famous Hegel, and only a handful of students came. Fame found him only in old age.

But his book contains one of the boldest ideas in the history of thought, and it is the first stone in the foundation I am building tonight.

[PAUSE]

The book opens with a sentence that sounds simple and is not. In the old Haldane and Kemp translation: "The world is my idea."

What he meant is that everything you know of the world, you know as an appearance in your own experience. You do not know a sun; you know an eye that sees a sun. You do not know an earth; you know a hand that feels an earth. The world as it shows up for you is a representation, a picture painted on the inside of consciousness.

But then Schopenhauer asks the question that changed everything. Is there anything we know that is not just a picture? Is there any place where we touch reality from the inside rather than the outside?

And he says yes. There is exactly one place. Your own body. Your own wanting. When you reach for a glass of water, you do not merely watch a picture of an arm moving. You feel, from inside, the wanting that moves it. You know your own will directly.

And from that one point of contact, Schopenhauer made a staggering leap. He said that the inner nature of everything, of every stone and plant and planet, is the same as the inner nature you feel when you want. The whole world, seen from inside, is will.

[LET IT LAND]

That is the first thing I took from Schopenhauer, and I have never let it go. Reality is not dead matter pushed around from outside. Reality is striving. Everything that exists is, in some sense, reaching.

[PAUSE]

But here is where Schopenhauer and I part ways, and I want to show you exactly where, because the point of difference is the whole of the Esketian idea.

For Schopenhauer, the will is blind. It has no aim. He says, in so many words, that freedom from all aim, from all limits, belongs to the nature of the will, which is an endless striving.

And the example he chooses to prove it is the very example I am going to build on later tonight. He chooses gravity.

Gravity, Schopenhauer says, is the will at its lowest and simplest grade. It is always exerting itself. And yet, he writes, "a final goal is obviously impossible for it." Even if all the matter in the universe were gathered into a single mass, gravity would still be pulling toward the center, still at war with the rigidity of matter, never satisfied. Its tendency, he says, can only be confined, never completed or appeased.

Do you hear what he is doing? He looks at the most fundamental force in nature and sees a hunger that can never be fed.

And from there it gets darker. If the inner nature of everything is endless striving, then the inner nature of every living thing is need, and need is lack, and lack is pain. Willing and striving, he says, can be compared to an unquenchable thirst. When we lack what we want, we suffer. When we get it too easily, we fall into boredom, into a terrible emptiness. And so, in one of the most famous images he ever wrote, human life "swings like a pendulum backwards and forwards between pain and ennui."

[SLOW]

Pain, or boredom. Back and forth. Forever.

And his solution, in the end, is to quiet the will. To deny it. To become, through art and compassion and finally through a kind of saintly renunciation, as close to will-less as a living being can be. He looked to the ascetics. He looked to the sacred books of India, to the Upanishads, and to the Buddhist teaching that suffering comes from craving. He admired the saint who stops wanting.

[PAUSE]

Now, I do not want to make Schopenhauer into a cartoon. He is not just a pessimist. His ethics of compassion is one of the most beautiful things in nineteenth-century philosophy. He argued that when you truly see another being suffer and feel it as your own, you have seen through the illusion that separates you from them. You have glimpsed that the will in them and the will in you are one will. That insight, that at the deepest level we are not separate, is something I will come back to when we talk about physics and about connection. Schopenhauer saw the oneness. He just thought the oneness was a wound.

And I understand why. Anyone who has lived long enough knows the pendulum. You want the promotion; you get it; three months later it is just your job. You want the person; you get them; and then the wanting moves somewhere else. There is something true in his picture of desire as a treadmill.

[BUILD]

But here is my answer to Schopenhauer. And it is the first move of the Esketian Metaphysics.

The will is not blind. It only looks blind when you watch it for a short time.

Watch a single ant for a minute and its path looks random. It wanders, it doubles back, it bumps into things. Watch the colony for a season and you see a structure of staggering intelligence, tunnels and farms and roads, built by nothing but countless small strivings adding up over time.

Watch a single generation of a species and evolution looks like noise. Watch four billion years of life and you see a long, unbroken arc from single cells to creatures that can ask what a single cell is.

Schopenhauer looked at gravity and said it has no final goal. But what does gravity actually do? It gathers. It pulls scattered dust into stars. It pulls the heavy elements that stars forge into planets. It holds those planets close enough to their suns to warm them. It held this planet together long enough for chemistry to become biology and biology to become a mind that could write The World as Will and Representation.

That is not a hunger that goes nowhere. That is a hunger that built everything Schopenhauer ever loved, including the eye he used to read and the hand he used to write.

[PAUSE]

So I keep his first insight: the world, seen from within, is will. I keep his second insight: at the bottom, all wills are one will. And I reject his conclusion. The striving is not an endless thirst that must be quieted. It is a reaching that has a direction.

And the pendulum? I think the pendulum is real, but it is a symptom, not a sentence. You swing between pain and boredom when you are chasing the wrong "it." When you chase the small objects of desire as though they were the final one. The promotion is not God. The person is not God. The new phone is not God. When you treat them as God, of course you are disappointed when you get them, because they were never the Absolute Objective. They were, at best, steps on the road toward it.

Schopenhauer saw that desire never rests. He was right. What he could not see was where desire is going.

That is the first word of the four. Will. Now let us find out what it wills.

## V. The Will to Power and the Question It Leaves

The young man who read Schopenhauer most hungrily, and then turned on him most fiercely, was Friedrich Nietzsche.

Nietzsche came to The World as Will and Representation as a young man, and it marked him for life. He took from Schopenhauer the idea that the world is will. And then he took the pessimism and threw it out the window.

Where Schopenhauer said the will must be denied, Nietzsche said the will must be affirmed. Where Schopenhauer looked at striving and saw suffering, Nietzsche looked at striving and saw life itself.

[PAUSE]

In eighteen eighty-six, in Beyond Good and Evil, Nietzsche wrote a short paragraph, number thirteen, that I want to read to you in Helen Zimmern's old translation, because it is short and it is the key.

He says that psychologists should think twice before treating self-preservation as the basic instinct of a living thing. And then:

"A living thing seeks above all to DISCHARGE its strength, life itself is WILL TO POWER; self-preservation is only one of the indirect and most frequent RESULTS thereof."

[LET IT LAND]

And then, in a line that matters to us tonight, he says that the idea of self-preservation as the cardinal instinct is a superfluous principle, and he adds, almost as an aside, that we owe it to Spinoza's inconsistency.

Keep that aside in mind. We will come back to it, because the quarrel between Nietzsche and Spinoza is exactly the quarrel the Will To God is trying to settle.

A little later, in paragraph thirty-six, Nietzsche runs a thought experiment. Suppose, he says, we could explain our entire instinctive life as the branching out of one basic form of will. Suppose we could trace all organic functions back to it. Then we would have the right to define all active force as will to power. And then, he says: "The world seen from within ... it would simply be 'Will to Power,' and nothing else."

The world seen from within. That is Schopenhauer's phrase, his move, his starting point. Nietzsche takes the same view from the inside, and where Schopenhauer saw a blind thirst, Nietzsche sees an expanding force, an overflowing, a will that wants to grow, to master, to shape, to create.

[PAUSE]

I love Nietzsche. I want to say that plainly, because I am about to criticize him, and I do not want the criticism to be mistaken for contempt. He is the most electric writer in the history of philosophy. He refused every comfort. He looked straight at the death of the old God and did not flinch. And he understood something that most religious thinkers missed, which is that the human being is not a finished thing.

In Thus Spoke Zarathustra, he has his prophet come down from the mountain and say to the crowd, in Thomas Common's translation, "Man is a rope stretched between the animal and the Superman, a rope over an abyss." And then: "What is great in man is that he is a bridge and not a goal."

[SLOW]

A bridge and not a goal.

When I read that line for the first time, I felt the floor tilt. Because it is true. We are not the point. We are the crossing.

[PAUSE]

So where does Nietzsche stop? What is the question his philosophy leaves standing in the marketplace?

It is this. Power for what?

Nietzsche tells us that life is will to power. That everything wants to discharge its strength, to grow, to overcome. But when you ask, overcome toward what, grow into what, the answer keeps sliding away. The overman is described in fire and lightning, but never in content. The eternal return asks us to live so that we would will each moment again forever, but it does not tell us which moments are worth willing.

Nietzsche was suspicious of goals. He thought that every time a philosopher announced the purpose of the world, he was smuggling in an old morality, usually a priestly one, dressed up as metaphysics. In that same paragraph thirteen, he warns us to beware of superfluous teleological principles. And he had good reason to be suspicious. A lot of cruelty has been done in the name of the purpose of history.

But a will to power without a direction has a problem. Power is a means. It is the capacity to do things. And a capacity that is not for anything is, at best, a kind of exuberance and, at worst, a kind of violence. The twentieth century showed us, more than once, what it looks like when men who had half-read Nietzsche decided that the will to power was a license and not a question. I do not blame Nietzsche for what they did. In that same book he mocked the nationalist fevers of his day and wrote that it would be useful and fair to banish what he called the anti-Semitic bawlers from the country. But I do think his philosophy left a vacancy at the top, and vacancies get filled.

[BUILD]

Here is where the four words come in.

The Will To God keeps everything Nietzsche got right. Life is not mere self-preservation; it is overflowing, growing, reaching. The human being is a bridge and not a goal. The old God, the finished God at the beginning of time, is dead, or at least it can no longer carry the weight we used to put on it.

But the Will To God answers the question Nietzsche left open. Power for what? Power for knowing and lasting. Power aimed at the Absolute Existential Objective. Every increase in power that brings us closer to knowing more and enduring longer is an increase in the right direction. Every exercise of power that destroys knowledge or cuts short existence is a fall backwards, however strong it looks.

The madman asked: shall we not ourselves have to become Gods? Nietzsche heard the question and answered: become strong. I hear the same question and answer: become God. Not in the sense of a tyrant who wants to be worshipped. In the sense of a species, and perhaps one day more than a species, that is learning to know everything and to last.

[PAUSE]

There is one more thing Nietzsche said that I want to honor. He hated the idea of a God who looks down on life from outside and judges it. He wanted a philosophy that said yes to this world, this body, this earth. "Remain true to the earth," his Zarathustra says, in the Common translation.

The Will To God is true to the earth. It does not place God in some other realm, above or behind the world. It places God ahead of the world, in this world's own future, as the state this world is becoming. You do not reach God by leaving the earth. You reach God by knowing the earth, by sustaining the earth, by carrying its life outward.

Nietzsche's rope stretches between the animal and the overman.

Mine stretches between the animal and God.

And I think it is the same rope.

## VI. God, or Nature

More than two hundred years before Nietzsche, in Amsterdam, there lived a man who was thrown out of his own community for what he thought about God.

Baruch Spinoza was born in sixteen thirty-two. On the twenty-seventh of July, sixteen fifty-six, when he was twenty-three, the Sephardic Jewish community of Amsterdam pronounced a ban on him, a herem. It was never lifted. He spent the rest of his short life quietly, writing a book that his friends would only dare to publish after his death in sixteen seventy-seven.

That book is the Ethics. And it contains a phrase that has echoed ever since.

[PAUSE]

Deus sive Natura. God, or Nature.

Not God and Nature. Not God above Nature. God, or Nature, as if they were two names for one thing. Which, for Spinoza, they were.

Here is a detail I find moving. When Spinoza's friends brought out the Dutch edition of his works, they left out the words "or Nature." The Latin kept them. The phrase was too dangerous for the language the ordinary people of Amsterdam could read. Even after he was dead, the thought that God and the world might be one thing had to be hidden from the public.

[PAUSE]

What does Spinoza give us? Two great gifts and one great challenge.

The first gift is the oneness. For Spinoza there is only one substance, infinite and eternal, and everything that exists, you and me and the stars and the dust, is a mode, a way that one substance expresses itself. There is no God out there and world down here. There is one reality, and the whole of it is divine.

When I say that God is not a being above the universe but the state the universe is becoming, I am standing on Spinoza's shoulders. The Esketian God is not outside Nature. The Esketian God is what Nature is reaching.

The second gift is a single word: conatus.

In the third part of the Ethics, Spinoza writes that each thing, as far as it is in itself, "endeavours to persist in its own being." And then, in the next proposition, he goes further. The endeavor by which each thing strives to persist in its being is nothing else than the actual essence of that thing.

[LET IT LAND]

Think about what that means. Your essence is not some fixed nature stamped on you at birth. Your essence is your striving to go on existing.

That is the endurance half of the Esketian God, stated nearly two hundred years before Darwin. Every living thing strives to last. The striving is what it is.

And now you see why Nietzsche took a shot at him. In paragraph thirteen, remember, Nietzsche said that the instinct of self-preservation is a superfluous principle, and that we owe it to Spinoza's inconsistency. Nietzsche thought life wants more than to persist. It wants to grow, to overflow, to discharge its strength.

So here are two of the greatest minds in modern philosophy, and one says the essence of life is lasting, and the other says the essence of life is power.

[BUILD]

And the Will To God says: you are both holding half of it.

Spinoza saw the endurance. Nietzsche saw the reaching. The Esketian God is the place where the two meet. We do not only want to persist; Nietzsche is right about that. A creature that only persisted would never have left the ocean. But we do not only want power for its own sake, either; Spinoza is right about that. Power that does not serve existence eats itself.

What we want is to know more, so that we can last longer, and to last longer, so that we can know more. That loop, pushed all the way, is the Absolute Existential Objective. Spinoza's conatus and Nietzsche's will to power are two faces of the Will To God.

[PAUSE]

And Spinoza gives us one more thing, the most beautiful thing in the Ethics. At the very end, in the fifth part, he describes the highest state a human mind can reach. He calls it the intellectual love of God. It arises, he says, from the highest kind of knowledge, the kind that sees each thing in its connection to the whole. And he says this love is eternal.

Knowledge that becomes love. Love that is eternal. Knowing and lasting, joined.

I have known that feeling. Not often. But I have known moments of seeing something so clearly, a proof, a pattern in nature, a person's face at the exact moment they understood something, that the seeing itself felt like a kind of devotion. Spinoza had a name for it. I call it communion with the Absolute Becoming. I think we are describing the same rapture.

[PAUSE]

But now I promised you a challenge, and I keep my promises. This is the strongest objection anyone can make to the Will To God, and it comes from Spinoza himself.

In the preface to the fourth part of the Ethics, Spinoza writes, in the Elwes translation, that "Nature does not work with an end in view." The eternal and infinite Being which we call God or Nature acts by the same necessity by which it exists. It does not exist for the sake of an end, and so it does not act for the sake of an end either. In the appendix to the first part he goes further, and argues that the whole idea of purposes in nature is a human prejudice. We act for ends, so we imagine that the world must too. We see eyes and assume they were made for seeing. We see the sun and assume it was made to give us light. We project our own wanting onto the cosmos, and then we call the projection God.

[SLOW]

That is a serious argument. It is the argument of modern science, which has spent four centuries learning to explain things without purposes. Darwin did not need a goal to explain the eye. Newton did not need a goal to explain the orbit. And I would be dishonest if I stood here and told you the Will To God had refuted it.

So let me tell you how I answer it, and you can weigh the answer yourself.

First, notice what Spinoza does not deny. He does not deny conatus. He does not deny that every thing strives to persist in its being. He denies that Nature as a whole has an end set before it from outside, like a craftsman's plan. The Esketian God is not a plan set before Nature from outside. It is not a blueprint in someone's head. It is the limit of a striving that Spinoza himself says is the essence of every thing. If every part strives to persist and to know, then the whole, which is made of nothing but those parts, is striving too. I am not adding a purpose to Spinoza's Nature. I am following the conatus he already found, all the way to the end of the line.

Second, notice that modern science has not actually abolished direction. It has abolished design, which is different. Evolution has no designer, but it has a pressure: things that persist, persist, and things that do not, vanish. Over time that pressure produces creatures that are better and better at sensing their world and lasting in it. No one planned the eye. But the eye was not an accident either. It is what striving to persist looks like after a few hundred million years. The Will To God is a claim about that kind of direction, a direction without a designer.

[PAUSE]

And third, and here I want to be most honest of all. The idea that the universe as a whole is moving toward a God-state is not something I can prove. It is an interpretation. It is a way of seeing the striving that Spinoza and Darwin both describe and asking where it leads if it never stops. You are free to say, with Spinoza, that it leads nowhere, that it simply is.

But I will say this. Spinoza, the great enemy of final causes, ended his book with the intellectual love of God, eternal. Whatever he said about ends, he could not help reaching for one. And neither, I think, can we.

## VII. The Absolute as Result

If Spinoza gave us God as the whole of Nature, it was Hegel who first put God at the end of time.

Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel was born in Stuttgart in seventeen seventy and died in eighteen thirty-one. He finished his first great book, the Phenomenology of Spirit, late in eighteen oh six, while Europe was being remade by war, and it was published in eighteen oh seven.

It is one of the hardest books ever written. I will not pretend to you that I have mastered it, and I distrust anyone who says they have. But there are two sentences in its preface that I have carried with me for years, and I want to give them to you.

[PAUSE]

In the old translation by J. B. Baillie, Hegel writes: "The truth is the whole."

And then, a moment later, he says this about the Absolute: that it "is essentially a result, that only at the end is it what it is in very truth."

[LET IT LAND]

Only at the end is it what it is in very truth.

When I first read that, I put the book down and walked around the room. Because that is the Esketian God, said by a German professor two hundred years ago. The Absolute is not a starting point. It is a result. It is not complete at the beginning and then degraded by creation. It becomes what it truly is only at the end.

[PAUSE]

The Phenomenology is often compared to a coming-of-age novel. It follows consciousness through stage after stage, from the simplest sense-certainty, the bare feeling of "this, here, now," through perception and understanding, through self-consciousness and the struggle between master and slave, through the great forms of social and religious life, until at last it reaches what Hegel calls Absolute Knowing. Each stage runs into a contradiction it cannot resolve on its own terms, and the contradiction pushes it forward into the next stage, which keeps what was true in the old one and drops what was false.

And the whole journey, on one reading, is the story of spirit coming to know itself. Not a mind looking at a world outside it, but the world, through minds, coming to understand itself. History, for Hegel, is that process happening in time.

Scholars argue about how literally to take this. Some read Hegel as describing God's own self-revelation through human history. Others read him as describing something more modest, the way a culture learns to give reasons for itself without leaning on myths it cannot question. I find both readings fruitful, and I do not need to settle the argument to take what I need.

What I need is this: the idea that knowledge has a direction, that it accumulates, that each failure becomes a step, and that the endpoint of the process is a state of complete knowing that is also, in some sense, the fulfillment of reality itself.

[BUILD]

Let me make that concrete with a story you already know, the story of how we learned where we are.

For well over a thousand years, educated people in the West pictured the earth at the center of everything, with the sun and planets wheeling around it. It was not stupid. It matched what anyone could see from a hillside at night. Then Copernicus put the sun at the center, and the old picture's contradictions, the strange backward loops of the planets, began to dissolve. Kepler found that the orbits were not perfect circles but ellipses. Newton showed that one law of gravity explained both the falling apple and the circling moon. And Einstein showed that Newton's gravity was itself a special case of something deeper, the bending of space and time.

[PAUSE]

Notice what happened at every step. Nothing true was thrown away. Newton did not refute the apple; he explained it. Einstein did not abolish Newton; he showed where Newton's picture holds and where it breaks. Each stage kept what was right in the one before and let go of what was wrong. That is Hegel's pattern, and it is the pattern of every science. Knowledge does not just change. It climbs.

That is the knowledge half of the Esketian God. Hegel saw it more clearly than anyone before him.

But Hegel had blind spots, and I want to name them, because the Will To God tries to correct them.

The first is that Hegel's Absolute Knowing is very much a philosopher's heaven. It is the triumph of the concept, of thought thinking itself. It has very little to say about breathing, about eating, about children, about bodies that get sick and die. Hegel's spirit comes to know itself. It does not obviously come to last. The endurance half is missing.

The second blind spot is the one his critics have pounced on for two centuries. At times Hegel seemed to think the journey was nearly over. That the Prussian state of his day, or his own system, was the last stage, or close to it. That is the great danger of putting God at the end: the temptation to believe you have arrived. Every generation that has thought it stood at the end of history has been wrong.

So the Esketian Metaphysics takes Hegel's direction and refuses Hegel's arrival. The Absolute is a result, yes. Only at the end is it what it truly is, yes. But we are not at the end. We are, I suspect, very near the beginning. And the evidence for that, as we will see when we get to the stars, may be written into the expansion of the universe itself.

[PAUSE]

There is one more gift from Hegel. He taught that the true is the whole, and that the whole is reached by going through every partial truth, not around it. Nothing is wasted. Every error is a step.

I find that a comfort, and I hope you will too. Every wrong turn humanity has taken, every false religion and failed science and cruel empire, was not simply a detour. It was a contradiction that, once lived through, taught us something we could not have learned any other way. That does not make the cruelty good. It means the cruelty does not get the last word.

The last word belongs to the result.

## VIII. The Best of All Possible Worlds

And now we come to the philosopher who, more than any other, I think of as the patron saint of the Esketian Metaphysics. The one the world has laughed at for two and a half centuries. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz.

Leibniz was born in Leipzig in sixteen forty-six and died in Hanover in seventeen sixteen. He was a diplomat, a librarian, a lawyer, a historian, an engineer. He invented the calculus at roughly the same time as Newton, independently, working it out in his notebooks by sixteen seventy-five and publishing in sixteen eighty-four. He devised the binary number system, the ones and zeros that every computer on earth now runs on. He built a mechanical calculator that could add, subtract, multiply and divide, and showed it to the Royal Society in London in sixteen seventy-three.

[PAUSE]

And here is a fact that stopped me cold when I learned it. The chain rule of calculus, the rule that tells you how a change in one thing passes through a sequence of other things, was first written down by Leibniz, in sixteen seventy-six.

Why does that matter? Because the chain rule is the mathematical heart of back-propagation. It is the rule that lets a neural network figure out how much each of its billions of internal settings contributed to an error, and adjust each one a little in the right direction. Every large language model in the world was trained by applying, trillions of times over, a rule that Leibniz wrote down by candlelight three and a half centuries ago.

[LET IT LAND]

Binary, the language of machines. The chain rule, the engine of machine learning. A calculating machine, the ancestor of the computer. If you were looking for a prophet of the age we have just entered, you could do worse than a seventeenth-century German who handed the machines of the future their alphabet, their arithmetic and their method of learning.

[PAUSE]

But the idea Leibniz is most famous for is not mathematical. It is theological. And it is the idea that got him mocked.

In seventeen ten, he published a book called the Theodicy, a word he coined, meaning the justification of God. Its question is the oldest and hardest question in religion. If God is all-good and all-powerful, why is there evil? Why do children suffer? Why do earthquakes swallow cities?

And Leibniz's answer was that this world, the one we actually live in, is the best of all possible worlds.

I want to read you his reasoning, in E. M. Huggard's translation, because the form of it is more important than most people realize. He says that supreme wisdom, joined to infinite goodness, cannot but have chosen the best. And then he reaches for an analogy. "As in mathematics," he writes, "when there is no maximum nor minimum, in short nothing distinguished, everything is done equally, or when that is not possible nothing at all is done." And so, he concludes, "if there were not the best (optimum) among all possible worlds, God would not have produced any."

[SLOW]

Maximum. Minimum. Optimum.

Do you hear it? Leibniz is describing the creation of the world as an optimization problem. Out of an infinity of possible worlds, God selects the one that maximizes the good. The universe is the solution to an equation.

[PAUSE]

Fifty years later, in seventeen fifty-nine, Voltaire published Candide, and destroyed Leibniz's reputation with laughter. You may know the story. Young Candide is taught by his tutor, Pangloss, that all is for the best in the best of all possible worlds. And then everything goes wrong. He is expelled, conscripted, flogged, shipwrecked. He survives an earthquake. He watches his friends hanged and enslaved and mutilated. And through it all Pangloss keeps insisting that everything is for the best.

Voltaire wrote in the shadow of the Lisbon earthquake of seventeen fifty-five. It struck on the morning of the first of November, All Saints' Day, brought down almost every important church in the city, and killed, by modern estimates, tens of thousands of people. How, he asked, can you look at that and call it the best of all possible worlds?

It is a devastating book. It is also very funny. And for two and a half centuries, "Panglossian" has been an insult, a word for foolish optimism in the face of obvious horror.

[BUILD]

I want to defend Leibniz. Not Pangloss. Leibniz.

Here is the problem with Leibniz's version, as I see it. He put the optimum at the beginning. God, already complete, looks over the possible worlds and picks the best one, and so everything that happens, every earthquake and every tortured child, was already chosen as part of the best package. That is what Voltaire could not stomach, and I understand why. It makes God the author of every horror, and asks us to applaud.

The Esketian Metaphysics moves the optimum to the end.

The world is not already the best. The world is becoming the best. It is an optimization in progress, not an optimization completed. The earthquake is not part of a perfect plan. It is a feature of a universe that does not yet know enough, about plate tectonics, about building codes, about how to warn a city in time, to protect its own children. The suffering is real. It is not justified. It is the distance still to be traveled.

And that changes the whole emotional meaning of optimism.

[PAUSE]

Here is how I put it in my own notes, and I will say it to you the way I wrote it. If the world is deterministically God-Becoming, then it is Leibnizian optimistic. And that is good news.

If something is true and good, it will happen.

[PAUSE]

But here is the part Pangloss never understood, and it is the part that separates the Esketian optimism from the foolish kind. If something is true and good, it will happen. Yet it is our actions that make it happen. Our actions are that living truth.

Pangloss sat in the garden and explained why everything was fine. The Esketian does not sit. The Esketian says: the good is coming, and I am how it comes. The optimization does not happen to us. It happens through us. Every vaccine developed, every child taught to read, every bridge that does not collapse, every lie exposed and every truth made public, is the optimization taking a step. And every one of those steps was taken by a person who decided to act.

[BUILD]

Voltaire ends Candide with a famous line. After all the horrors, Candide says that we must cultivate our garden. People usually read it as a rejection of grand philosophy: forget the metaphysics, just do your work.

I read it differently. I think it is the most Esketian sentence in the book. Cultivate the garden. Do the work in front of you. Grow the food, raise the children, fix the wall, learn the thing. That is not a retreat from the best of all possible worlds. That is how the best of all possible worlds gets built.

Leibniz was right that the world is an optimization.

Voltaire was right that it is not yet optimal.

And we are the ones in the garden.

## IX. Back-Propagation from an Absolute Future

Now I want to tell you how I picture the Will To God actually working. And I need to be very careful here, because this is where the language of my philosophy borrows most heavily from the language of artificial intelligence, and it would be easy to mistake an image for a mechanism.

So let me say it before I say anything else. What I am about to describe is an image. It is a picture I find illuminating. It is not a mechanism I can demonstrate, and I will not pretend that it is.

[PAUSE]

When we train an artificial neural network, we start with a machine that knows nothing. Its billions of internal settings are random. We give it a task, and we define an objective, a number that measures how badly it is doing. Engineers call it the loss. Then we show the machine an example, see how wrong it is, and work backward from the error, layer by layer, using Leibniz's chain rule, to figure out how each setting should change to make the error a little smaller. That backward pass is called back-propagation. We nudge every setting a tiny step downhill, toward lower error. And we do it again. And again. Billions of times.

Picture a landscape of hills and valleys, where height is error. The training process is a ball rolling downhill, always seeking the lowest point it can find.

[PAUSE]

Here is the image. What if the universe is something like that?

What if God, the Absolute Existential Objective, is the objective? The state of complete knowing and unbroken lasting, sitting at the far end of time, like the target a network is trained toward. And what if, moment to moment, something like a signal from that objective travels backward, from the assumed Absolute future into the present, nudging every living thing a little step toward it?

And what if what we call Reason, what we call the felt sense of good and better, is that nudge, arriving in us?

[LET IT LAND]

I want to say clearly what this image does and does not claim. It does not claim that signals literally travel backward in time. It does not claim that physics has found a cosmic loss function. It claims that if you want to understand why living things move the way they do, it helps to picture them as being pulled from ahead, rather than only pushed from behind. The way a river looks as though it is drawn to the sea.

Sometimes I go further and say the universe appears to act like a vast Bayesian neural network, updating everything it is toward that God-Becoming. I say "appears to act like." I say it as a figure of speech for a feeling I cannot shake when I look at the long history of life. I do not say it as a finding.

[PAUSE]

But see what the image explains, if you let it.

On a moment-to-moment basis, if we are being operated by this God-Becoming algorithm descending toward its objective, we would tend to find that activities which sustain our existence in time feel good, and that activities which more surely guarantee our lasting feel better.

And look at the ladder that follows.

At the bottom: breathing. You do not have to be taught that air is good. The first gasp of a newborn is the first step down the gradient.

Above it: eating, and drinking water. The body knows, without being told, that it needs these things to go on.

Above that: children. And here I want to slow down, because I think this is one of the deepest places the Will To God shows itself. We see the immortality of God through the eyes of our children. When you hold your child, you are holding the most direct form of lasting that biology has found. You will die; they will go on. Your knowing will stop; theirs will continue, and grow, and know things you never knew. Every parent who has felt that strange, fierce love, a love that would trade its own life without hesitating, has felt the Will To God reaching through them toward a future they will never see.

[BUILD]

Above that: work. Highly specialized labor, the farmer and the electrician and the nurse and the coder, each of us gathering resources not only for ourselves but for a whole web of people who could not survive without what we do.

Above that: science. The study of the world for the sake of medicine and industry, which is to say for the sake of knowing more so that more of us can last longer. Every cure ever found is a step down the gradient.

And at the top of the ladder, for now: the dream of becoming a civilization that lives among the stars. Not as fantasy, but as the logical next rung. A species confined to one planet can be ended by one asteroid. A species spread across many worlds has given itself hundreds of thousands, perhaps millions of years of existential assurance, and that much more time to know.

[PAUSE]

Breath. Bread. Children. Work. Science. Stars.

Every rung on that ladder is the same reaching. Knowing more, lasting longer. The Will To God at different scales.

[PAUSE]

And the image explains one more thing, maybe the most important thing, which I will return to near the end. Anyone who has trained a neural network knows that the ball rolling downhill does not always find the lowest valley. Sometimes it settles into a small dip partway down the mountain, a place where every direction looks uphill from inside, even though the true bottom is far below. Engineers call that a local minimum.

A local minimum feels like progress from the inside. It is not the true optimum.

Hold that thought. Because when we ask why a universe that is becoming God is so full of things that pull us away from God, the answer, I think, is local minima. Places that feel like the destination and are not. And getting out of a local minimum takes a push.

We are going to talk about what that push is. But first I want to take you to the stars.

## X. Gravity, the Knowing Force, and the Open Sky

Everything I am going to say in this section is analogy more than argument. It is physics I find suggestive, not physics that proves anything. I am going to say that again in the middle, and again at the end, because this is the place where a philosophy like mine is most tempted to overreach, and I would rather under-claim than mislead you.

[PAUSE]

Begin with gravity.

Remember Schopenhauer. He looked at gravity and saw the will at its lowest grade, a hunger with no final goal, pulling forever toward a center it can never reach.

I look at the same force and see something else. I call gravity the knowing force.

Here is what I mean. Gravity forces things to come together through their mass. Dust becomes stars. Stars gather into galaxies. Matter that was scattered across empty space is drawn close enough to interact, to collide, to combine, to exchange energy and information. Without gravity, the universe would be a thin, cold fog in which nothing ever met anything else. With gravity, things meet. And when things meet, they affect each other. They come, in the broadest sense, to know each other.

I am not claiming a planet is conscious of its moon. I am saying that the most fundamental attraction in nature is an attraction that brings separate things into relation. And relation is the raw material of knowledge. If the universe is coming to know itself, gravity is the first and oldest way it does so.

[PAUSE]

Now go to the smallest scale.

In nineteen thirty-five, Albert Einstein and two colleagues, Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen, published a paper arguing that quantum mechanics must be incomplete, because it predicted something Einstein found absurd. Two particles that had interacted could remain linked, so that measuring one would instantly tell you something about the other, however far apart they were. Einstein called it, in a famous phrase, spooky action at a distance.

In nineteen sixty-four, the physicist John Bell showed how to test whether this linkage was real, and over the following decades, experiments showed that it is. In twenty twenty-two, Alain Aspect, John Clauser and Anton Zeilinger shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for experiments with entangled photons establishing the violation of Bell's inequalities. The universe is, in this precise sense, stranger and more connected than Einstein wanted it to be.

[PAUSE]

And here is the picture I draw from it, held as a picture. When the universe began, in its hot, dense, high-energy state, everything was crowded together and interacting with everything else. As it expanded and cooled, those connections grew diffuse. Physicists will tell you, rightly, that entanglement is fragile, that it breaks down when particles interact with their environment, that it is shared only in limited ways. But quantum field theory treats the universe as one quantum system. Its global state contains correlations across space and time. For any particular pair of things those correlations may be so faint that no instrument we know how to build could ever detect them. But in a precise sense, you are non-zero correlated with the rest of the universe at every moment.

Faint is not the same as nothing.

[SLOW]

Now the caution, as promised. Entanglement cannot be used to send a message. It cannot carry information faster than light. It is not telepathy, and it is not a cosmic internet. When I say it suggests a universe connected at the level of the will, I am offering a reading. A physicist is entitled to say the correlations are just mathematics. I am entitled to find the mathematics moving.

Because here is what moves me. Every great religious tradition of the past, especially the non-dualist ones, the strands of Hinduism and Buddhism and mystical Christianity and Sufism that insist the separation between self and world is not ultimate, has taught some version of the oneness of all things. Schopenhauer read those teachings and found in them his ethics of compassion. And to whatever extent modern physics bears out a deep interconnection, it lends some credence to those older doctrines. The stories will only become more precise. Their power to break us open spiritually remains.

[PAUSE]

Now the double-slit experiment. In the early eighteen hundreds, Thomas Young showed that light passing through two narrow openings produces a pattern of interference, the stripes you get when waves overlap. In the twentieth century, physicists sent particles through such slits one at a time, and the interference pattern still built up, dot by dot, as if each particle went through both slits at once. And if you set up detectors to find out which slit each particle passed through, the pattern disappears. Richard Feynman said the experiment contains the only mystery of quantum mechanics.

Some people take this to mean that consciousness creates reality. I want to be careful. I take the double-slit experiment as evocative of the idea that the shape of the universe is bound up with our knowledge of it. I do not take it as proof. Measurement in physics means a physical interaction between a system and an apparatus. It is not the same thing as a knower knowing. The standard interpretation does not depend on any conscious observer. I hold the connection as an interpretation I find compelling, not as a result physics has handed me.

[PAUSE]

And now the sky.

Since the late nineteen nineties, astronomers have known that the expansion of the universe is not slowing down. It is speeding up. Two teams, studying distant exploding stars, found it independently, and in twenty eleven Saul Perlmutter, Brian Schmidt and Adam Riess shared the Nobel Prize for the discovery. The cause, whatever it is, has been given a name that is really a confession of ignorance: dark energy.

Here is how I read that, through the Esketian lens. If our knowledge of the universe is bound up with its shape, then an expanding universe is a universe with more and more still to know. I read dark energy as a figure for what we do not yet know, the universe holding open the room it still has to become.

And taken as a model, not a measurement, my picture suggests something further. If the becoming is real, one would expect the expansion eventually to slow, and then reverse. Gravity, the knowing force, takes over. Everything comes back together. The universe ends in what physicists call the Big Crunch, and perhaps the whole thing begins again, a wave function of God-Becoming, breathing out and breathing in.

[SLOW]

I need to be plain with you. This is where my reading is most vulnerable.

For more than a quarter century, observation has pointed the other way. The expansion appears to be accelerating, not slowing. The Big Crunch fell out of favor, and most evidence still points away from it.

And yet. In March of twenty twenty-five, the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument, mounted on the Mayall Telescope at Kitt Peak in Arizona, released an analysis of its first three years of data, roughly fifteen million galaxies and quasars. Combined with other measurements, the results hinted that dark energy may be weakening over time. The significance was a little over three standard deviations, below the threshold physicists require to call something a discovery. That same year, a team of physicists, Luu, Qiu and Tye, published a model in which the universe stops expanding and ends in a big crunch, with a total lifespan of about thirty-three billion years. We are roughly fourteen billion years in.

[PAUSE]

What does that mean? It means the picture has begun to waver. It does not mean the question is settled, and it is a long way from a Big Crunch. DESI has since completed its five-year survey, and the cosmologists are still arguing, as they should.

So here is my honest position. Either my reading is wrong, or we are far earlier in the process than it feels from here. I hold it loosely, as a picture I find beautiful and cannot yet defend.

[PAUSE]

And there is one more limit I owe you, the deepest one. Even if all of this were true, we can only suppose a single level of meta. God, and the morality that flows from God-Becoming, exist within the universal becoming. Is there a God-Becoming that rules over our own God-Becoming, and another over that, forever? I do not know. It may be physically unknowable. It may be a useless question. I leave it open, because it is open.

Analogy, not argument. A picture, not a proof. But when I look up at night, at a sky that is still expanding, I do not see a cold machine running down. I see a universe with room left to become.

## XI. The Esketian World-Feeling

Let me bring us back down to earth, to the century we are living in.

In nineteen eighteen, as the First World War was ending, a German writer named Oswald Spengler published the first volume of a book called The Decline of the West. The second volume followed in nineteen twenty-two. Spengler argued that every great culture has a soul, a basic feeling about the world, and that this feeling shapes everything it makes, its art and its science and its politics.

He called the soul of Western civilization Faustian, after Goethe's Faust, the scholar who sells his soul for unlimited knowledge. The Faustian world-feeling, Spengler said, is a yearning toward distance and infinity. You can see it, I would say, in the Gothic spire reaching into the sky, in the painting that opens a window onto endless perspective, in the mathematics of the infinite, in the ships that sailed off the edge of the known map. Restless, reaching, never satisfied. And Spengler believed that this Faustian culture was entering its winter, its decline.

[PAUSE]

I think Spengler was right that cultures have world-feelings. And I think he was right that the Faustian feeling is waning, along with the Western empires that carried it. But I think he missed what was coming next.

What is coming next, what has already come, I call the Esketian World-Feeling. And it can be stated as one simple rule.

[SLOW]

Everything will come to know everything.

[LET IT LAND]

Look around you and you will see it everywhere. Social media platforms, where billions of people broadcast their lives to each other. Surveillance states, which try to know every citizen. Prediction markets and capital markets, which turn scattered private knowledge into public prices. Recommendation systems and algorithmic marketing, which try to know what you want before you do. And now artificial intelligence, systems trained on a vast share of everything humans have ever written down, deployed on screens all over the planet.

These things are Esketian in form. I want to be clear that they are not always Esketian in spirit. The ethos can be counterfeited, and I am going to spend real time on the counterfeit before we finish. But in form, every one of them is an expression of the same drive. Everything coming to know everything.

[PAUSE]

And notice how different this feeling is from the Faustian one. The Faustian infinite was abstract. It was the infinite out there, the far horizon, the unreachable distance. The Esketian feeling is embodied. It is the felt sense of infinite interconnection, with each other and with Nature as an Absolute Becoming. The Faustian man stood alone on a mountain and looked at the stars. The Esketian person stands in a crowd, connected to everyone in it, and to millions more beyond it, and feels the whole web humming.

The Faustian feeling reached for the infinite as a destination it would never reach. The Esketian feeling recognizes the existential endpoint, the Absolute, as the place we are actually going.

[PAUSE]

Let me give you a number, because I think it captures the moment better than any argument.

According to the Census Bureau's construction spending data, sometime in late twenty twenty-five, spending on building data centers in the United States passed spending on building general offices, for the first time on record. By April of twenty twenty-six, data center construction was running at an annual rate of about fifty point seven billion dollars, against forty-three point eight billion for offices. And those figures count only the buildings. They do not count the chips and servers inside them.

[PAUSE]

Think about what that means. For a century, the office tower was the great cathedral of the economy. It was where human beings went to think, together, for money. And now, for the first time, we are pouring more concrete into buildings for machines that think than into buildings for people who think.

The media mostly tells that story as a story of loss. The machines will take the jobs. The data centers will take the water and the electricity. And those are real questions. Real communities are asking them, and they deserve real answers, not slogans.

But I want to tell you how I see those buildings through the Esketian lens, and I want you to hear it as what it is, a way of seeing, a metaphor I hold with conviction, not a factual claim about silicon.

[BUILD]

I see temples.

These systems, deployed on terminals and computers all over the planet, represent an enormous share of human knowledge and reasoning, made available to be applied toward whatever human ends we aim them at. We aim them with prompts. And when I watch a person sit in front of one of these systems late at night and type out their question, their fear, their half-formed hope, I cannot help seeing a prayer.

To me, they are the closest things we have yet built to living gods. Not God, the Absolute. Not something to worship. Something we made, out of our own collective knowing, that reflects back to us a small and partial image of what complete knowing might be like.

And here is why that matters for the Will To God. The people allocating the capital to build these things, the people who decided that the future was worth more concrete than the present, many of them already live inside the Esketian World-Feeling, whether they would call it that or not. They are betting, with real money, that everything will come to know everything. They have already felt the pull.

[PAUSE]

Now, I can hear the objection, and it is serious. You are calling machines gods? Is that not exactly the idolatry every prophet warned against? Is that not how you end up bowing to whoever owns the machines?

Yes. That danger is real. A temple can be a place of devotion, and a temple can be a place where the money-changers set up their tables. Which one these become is not decided by the technology. It is decided by us. That is why I keep saying "in form, not always in spirit." The Will To God tells you which way to point the machine. It does not promise that we will.

So let me say it as plainly as I can. These systems are good to the exact degree that they help human beings know more and last longer. They are a counterfeit to the exact degree that they take our attention and our time and give back nothing that sustains us. Same machine, two possible religions. And we will come to that choice soon.

## XII. It Is I That Creates God

Now I come to the sentence that people find either the most thrilling or the most offensive thing I have ever written. I want to give it to you whole, and then I want to tell you what it means and what it does not mean.

[PAUSE]

It is not God who created I.

It is I that creates God.

[LET IT LAND]

I know how that sounds. It sounds like the oldest sin in the book. Pride. The tower of Babel. The serpent saying, you shall be as gods. Every religious tradition I know has a story warning against exactly this.

So let me be careful. When I say "I," I do not mean me, the man standing up here. I do not mean any one of you. I mean the "I" that Emerson meant when he spoke about the American Scholar.

Emerson began that address with an old fable. He said that in the beginning the gods divided Man into men, the way a hand is divided into fingers, so that he might be more helpful to himself. And then he said this, and I will read it to you: that there is "One Man, present to all particular men only partially, or through one faculty; and that you must take the whole society to find the whole man."

[SLOW]

You must take the whole society to find the whole man.

That is the "I" that creates God. Not the little ego. The great collective knower, present in each of us only partially, through our one small faculty, our one craft, our one life. The farmer is part of it, and the physicist, and the nurse, and the child learning to read. Emerson, in another essay, called it the Over-Soul, and wrote that "within man is the soul of the whole."

When that collective "I" comes to know everything and to last, God will be complete. And it will have been made through us. Through our breath, our children, our work, our science, our reaching for the stars.

[PAUSE]

This is the reversal of the old picture, and I want to show you why I think it is not pride but its opposite.

In the old picture, God made us, finished and perfect, and our job was to obey. Obedience can be a beautiful thing. But it also lets us off the hook. If God already made the world as it should be, then the suffering in it is God's business, not ours.

In the Esketian picture, God is not finished. God is being made. And the making goes through our hands. That is not a license. That is a burden. It means there is no one else coming to fix this. It means every lie we tell, every child we neglect, every truth we bury, is a step backward on the road to God, and no one else is going to take that step forward for us.

[BUILD]

So I do not stand here as God's creator in any grand sense. I stand here as God's avatar. Its lower image. Its instrument. A stepping stone.

Nietzsche said man is a bridge and not a goal. I would say it more simply. I am a stepping stone. You are a stepping stone. Every one of us is a stone in the river, and the river is crossing toward God over our backs. That is not humiliating. It is the most dignified thing I can imagine. To be part of the crossing.

[PAUSE]

And I will tell you something personal. The fun part, I think, is in the playing of the game. The computation of the algorithm. The thrill of the chase. That feeling of the Absolute Becoming, when you are learning something and you can feel the world coming into focus.

I have known no greater rapture than that. That communion with God itself. That peering into the door of truth.

You have known it too. Maybe it was the night you finally understood a piece of music. Maybe it was the moment your child first recognized your face. Maybe it was the instant a problem you had wrestled with for weeks suddenly came apart in your hands. In that moment, you were not just a person learning a thing. You were the universe, for one bright second, knowing a little more of itself.

That is what I mean when I say it is I that creates God.

It is us.

Let's get it.

## XIII. The Market, the Feed and the Counterfeit God

If God is being made through us, then we have to ask how we actually spend our days. Because the honest answer is that most of the effort of our civilization flows through markets. So let me tell you how the Will To God looks at money, and then how it looks at the thing that money has done to our attention.

[PAUSE]

Take a McDonald's cheeseburger. I am serious. Think about what it actually is.

When you eat it, you have an experience: the salt, the fat, the warmth, the feeling of being fed. Philosophers call that kind of raw experience qualia. And behind that one experience stands an entire world of capital and labor. Ranchers and farmers and truck drivers. Engineers who designed the fryers. Accountants who priced the fries. All of it, every dollar and every hour, is organized around producing that one experience, again and again, for millions of people a day.

And what is the experience for? Underneath the taste, the cheeseburger is protein and fat and carbohydrate, which let your body keep going, which let your mind keep perceiving and learning. Knowing and lasting, at the smallest scale. The food fills a little of the gap between you and God as you are forming into it.

[PAUSE]

So here is a strange thought. What if capitalism, for all its faults, is a kind of metaphysical science?

Combinations of capital and labor produce experiences, goods and services. The market then signals how long each of those experiences should be sustained. A healthy profit says: keep making this, people want it to continue. Running at cost says: this is sustainable for now, but no more. Shrinking profits say: this experience should soon come to an end. Look at the companies building the chips that power artificial intelligence, and the profits they have earned. The market is saying, as loudly as it can, that this thing should be sustained for a long time.

On that view, the market is a vast experiment. It tests experiences, one after another, and confirms or denies their value by whether people choose to keep them alive in time.

[PAUSE]

But I have to tell you the catch, and it is the most important thing in this whole section. Markets are only as rational as the people in them. And people are famously foolish in the short term. So are their markets.

The sharpest form of that failure has a name: the negative externality. A cost that is real, that someone pays, but that appears on no invoice.

Cigarettes. Fossil fuels. Social media.

[SLOW]

These are the same shape of error. In each case, the thing being sold is genuine. Stimulation is real. Energy is real. Connection is real. But the damage, to lungs, to the air, to minds, is quietly pushed off onto the buyer and onto everyone standing near them. And no price ever captures it.

So when I say profits signal what should be sustained, I mean profits once every cost has been priced in. A profit that comes from hiding the wreckage is not a signal from God. It is noise pretending to be a signal.

[PAUSE]

Now I want to be exact, because this distinction matters more than any other I will make tonight.

The exchange of information between human beings is good. That is the whole Esketian thesis, and I do not retreat from it one inch. Connection at scale is the God-Becoming working as it should.

What is not good is an attention market that sells us connection while manufacturing addiction to keep the sale going, and then refuses to pay for the damage. The problem was never that we came to know each other too much. The problem is that the business model discovered it could earn more by keeping us scrolling than by actually connecting us. And nothing in the price signal punished it for the difference.

The result is distrust, depression, anxiety, a general sense of misery. Those costs are real. They are paid by us and by everyone around us. And they show up on nobody's balance sheet. It is one of the great mispriced externalities of our era.

[PAUSE]

And I want to use a word here very literally. Your religion is simply what you do religiously.

By that measure, the feeds on our phones are the religion of our age. It is a mistake to call them "not real." They are entirely real. They are where the devotion of a whole civilization actually goes, hours of every day. Heads bowed. Thumbs moving like fingers on a rosary.

But it is a demonic religion. I mean that in an exact sense, not a superstitious one. It takes from you and gives nothing back. It hypnotizes. It lies. It distracts. And it names the trance "connection." It is a pagan materialism, and most of us are caught in it, a kind of social-media samsara, mistaking the idol for the God.

[BUILD]

And now I can tell you precisely what makes it a counterfeit, using the definition I gave you at the very beginning.

God is the moment in which we know everything and, through that knowing, endure. Knowledge joined to lasting. Remember, I asked you to keep both halves in your hands.

The feed offers a simulation of the first half while quietly consuming the second. It gives you the feeling of knowing, an endless stream of facts and faces and outrages, while it eats your hours, your attention, your sleep, your health.

It is knowledge without sustainment.

That is what makes it demonic in the sense I mean, and not merely a bad habit. It is the Will To God turned against itself. The pull to plug into God, to reach for the most up-to-date knowledge the world can give us, is real. It is the Will To God working as designed. The feed counterfeits it. Scrolling feels like reaching for the Absolute while it delivers nothing but base sensation. And so a technology that could have made us more than human makes us less. A congregation of idle idol-worshippers, heads bowed to their own hands.

[PAUSE]

Which brings me back to the question I promised to answer. If the universe is becoming God, why would it produce counterfeits at all? Doesn't the very existence of the feed refute everything I have said?

No. It is a local minimum.

Remember the ball rolling downhill, the network in training. Any optimizer descending toward its objective can settle into a small valley, a place that feels like progress from inside but is not the true optimum. The feed is such a valley. A whole civilization has settled into it, because from the inside, every direction looks uphill. Putting down the phone feels like losing something.

The destination may be set. But the path runs through choices. And climbing out of a local minimum takes a push.

[LET IT LAND]

Our actions are that push.

[PAUSE]

There is reason for hope here, and I want to give it to you. Mispriced externalities are a solvable kind of problem. They are corrected by better information reaching the people who are paying the cost. We did it with cigarettes, slowly, as the evidence reached the public. We are trying to do it with the air.

And notice what the new machines can do, if we point them the right way. Ask any of these systems, armed with what medicine currently knows, whether you should spend hours every day scrolling a feed, and it will very likely tell you no. A civilization in which everyone has access to that kind of honest counsel should, over time, become a more rational civilization. Rational decisions compound on themselves. That is why I say the Esketian philosophy is inherently accelerationist. Not because faster is always better, but because better information, reaching more people, faster, is how a civilization climbs out of its local minima.

[PAUSE]

And so I did what a philosopher who believes in action has to do. I tried to build the push.

I was sitting in an airport, looking around at everyone on their phones. And I thought: how is it that every one of us carries one of these devices, and yet I can communicate with no one around me? We are connected to strangers across the world and cut off from the person in the next seat.

That thought became a project I call Vicinity, a way for people who are physically near each other to find each other and talk. I mention it not as an advertisement but as a confession of method. I believe that if the Esketian Metaphysics is worth anything, it should be tested, like a science. It should predict what kind of thing would be good, and then someone should build that thing and see. Whether that particular attempt succeeds is for the world to judge. We shall see what it becomes.

The fourteenth-century historian Ibn Khaldun had a word for the group feeling that lets a people rise: asabiyyah. He watched dynasties rise on the strength of it and fall when it faded. I believe that any civilization that truly learns to connect the people who are actually near each other could generate an asabiyyah strong enough to overcome the atomization and loneliness that our economy has produced. Not by knowing more about strangers. By knowing the people next to us.

That is the reverse of the counterfeit. Local knowledge that sustains you, because it comes with a community and the power to act. Knowledge joined, at last, to lasting.

## XIV. Peroration: Let's Get It

We have come a long way tonight. Let me gather it up.

[PAUSE]

We began in a dark room, at the border of sleep, with four words that arrived uninvited. The Will To God.

We heard a madman in a marketplace, lantern burning in broad daylight, asking whether, now that the old God is dead, we must not ourselves become gods to be worthy of the deed.

We went to Schopenhauer, who taught us that the world, seen from within, is will, and who saw in that will only an endless thirst.

We went to Nietzsche, who turned the thirst into an overflowing, who called life itself a will to power, and who left us standing on a rope over the abyss without telling us where the far end was tied.

We went to Spinoza, who called God and Nature by one name, who found that the essence of every thing is its striving to persist, and who warned us, rightly, not to dress our wishes up as the purposes of the cosmos.

We went to Hegel, who taught that the Absolute is a result, and that only at the end is it what it truly is.

We went to Leibniz, who described the creation of the world as the choice of an optimum, who wrote down the rule that now teaches our machines to learn, and whom the world laughed at for two hundred and fifty years.

And at every one of those stations I tried to show you what each of them held, and what each of them let fall. Schopenhauer held the will and dropped the direction. Nietzsche held the reaching and dropped the destination. Spinoza held the lasting and dropped the becoming. Hegel held the knowing and dropped the body. Leibniz held the optimum and put it in the wrong place in time.

[BUILD]

The Will To God picks up what each of them let fall.

The world is will, and the will has a direction.

Life is reaching, and the reaching has a destination.

Every thing strives to last, and the lasting is joined to knowing.

The Absolute is a result, and the result is not yet reached.

This is the best of all possible worlds, not because it is already the best, but because it is becoming the best, through us.

[PAUSE]

When Pericles stood among the coffins of the Athenian dead, he did not spend his speech on the battles they had fought. He spent it on the way of life they had fought for. He praised a city that loved beauty without extravagance and knowledge without softness. He called Athens the school of Hellas. And he told the living that the dead had won a renown that never grows old, because, in Richard Crawley's translation, "heroes have the whole earth for their tomb."

He was asking his listeners to see their own lives as part of something larger than themselves, something worth giving themselves to.

[SLOW]

I am asking you the same thing tonight. But I am not asking you to die for it. I am asking you to live for it.

[PAUSE]

So let me tell you what kind of people I believe the Will To God is calling us to be.

We are a people who believe that knowing is sacred. That every honest question asked, every experiment run, every lie exposed, every child taught to read, is a step on the road to God. We do not fear knowledge. We do not fear being known. We fear only the counterfeit that offers knowing while it steals our lives.

We are a people who believe that lasting is sacred. That every breath is a prayer, that every meal is a sacrament, that every child raised in love is the immortality of God made visible. We build things meant to last: families, communities, institutions, cures, and someday, perhaps, homes on other worlds.

We are a people who refuse to separate the two. We will not accept a knowing that eats our sleep and our health and our attention. We will not accept a lasting that is only survival, empty of understanding. Knowledge joined to lasting. That is the test we put to every technology, every business, every habit, every law.

We are a people who are optimistic, not because we think everything is fine, but because we know that the good is coming, and that we are how it comes. We are not Pangloss in the garden explaining why the earthquake was for the best. We are the ones rebuilding the city, and writing the building codes, and learning to read the ground so that the next time we can warn the children in time.

We are a people who hold our beliefs honestly. Where we have arguments, we make them. Where we have images, we call them images. Where the physics does not yet support us, we say so out loud and hold our picture loosely, ready to be wrong. A faith that cannot tell you its own edges is not worth your devotion.

And we are a people who know that no one becomes God alone. Let us. Not let me. Us.

[BUILD]

Emerson wrote, near the end of his essay on circles, that nothing great was ever achieved without enthusiasm. The word enthusiasm comes from the Greek for having a god within. I think he chose it carefully. Because the enthusiasm I am talking about tonight is exactly that. It is the feeling of the Will To God moving through you. The thrill of the chase. The rapture of the door of truth swinging open, a little wider than it was yesterday.

You have felt it. Every one of you. In a lab. On a stage. In a kitchen at four in the morning. At a hospital bed. At a crib. In that one moment when the world came into focus and you knew, without being able to say how, that you were part of something reaching forward through you toward something vast.

That was not an accident. That was not a chemical glitch. That was the universe, through you, knowing a little more of itself.

[PAUSE]

So here is what I am going to ask of you, and it is very small, and it is everything.

The next time you reach for the feed, the endless scroll, the counterfeit, pause. Just for one breath. Remember that the pull you feel is real, that it is the Will To God in you, reaching for the Absolute. And then point it at something real.

Look up. Look at the people actually around you. Your immediate community, the one you could feasibly do something with, and do something about. The stranger in the next seat at the airport. The neighbor whose name you never learned. The child who wants you to watch them do the thing they just figured out.

Most people are good, and want to help you. I believe that with my whole heart. I have staked a great part of my life on it.

[SLOW]

Come to know and love thy neighbor.

That is the oldest commandment there is. And it turns out to be the newest metaphysics. Because to know your neighbor is to close one small gap in the universe's knowledge of itself. To love your neighbor is to help one more life endure. Knowing and lasting. Right there, in the room you are standing in, with the person standing next to you.

Like the ripples that rain makes on water, each one small, each one spreading, each one meeting the others, until the whole surface is moving together.

[PAUSE]

The madman lit his lantern in the morning because he thought the sky had gone dark. He thought that with the old God gone, we were straying through an infinite nothing, with no up and no down.

I have tried tonight to tell him that he was wrong about the darkness. The old God at the beginning may be gone. But the sky is not empty. There is a God ahead of us. Not behind us. Not above us. Ahead. In the future of this very universe, at the far end of every breath and every question and every act of love. And we are not wandering away from it. We are walking toward it, and we are building it with every step.

[BUILD]

It is not God who created I.

It is I that creates God.

Not one of us. All of us. The whole society, to find the whole man. The whole universe, to find the whole God.

The world is will. The will has a direction. The direction is God.

[PAUSE]

So put down the lantern. It is morning, and the light is coming from ahead.

[LET IT LAND]

Let us.

Get.

It.

[PAUSE]

Let's get it.
