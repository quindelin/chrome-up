# Notes: 24-napoleon, Napoleon Bonaparte: Proclamations to the Army

Kind: transcription. Fifty-eight proclamations and addresses by Napoleon (died 1821), in public-domain English translations published in 1894 to 1897.

## Length and runtime

- `wc -w speech.md`: 12,193 words, counting headings, context lines and cues.
- Spoken text only: 11,356 words.
- Estimated runtime at 140 wpm: about 81 minutes (1 hour 21 minutes).
- **This is shorter than the two-hour target.** Napoleon's proclamations are short by design; most run 70 to 300 words. The Tarbell anthology, the main public-domain English collection found, holds about 10,000 words of proclamations and addresses. Nothing was padded. Tarbell's letters, his will and the Bellerophon protest were left out because they are not proclamations or addresses (see "Left out" below). Adding them (about 5,960 words, not counting the will) would bring the total to about 17,300 words, roughly 2 hours 4 minutes, if the user wants that.

## Sources

1. **Main text (56 of the 58 items):** Ida M. Tarbell, ed., *Napoleon's Addresses: Selections from the Proclamations, Speeches and Correspondence of Napoleon Bonaparte* (Boston: Joseph Knight Company, 1897; copyright 1896). Public domain in the US (published before 1929).
   - Text taken from the proofread Wikisource transcription, fetched through the Wikisource API:
     - https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Napoleon%27s_Addresses/Part_I
     - https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Napoleon%27s_Addresses/Part_II
     - https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Napoleon%27s_Addresses/Part_III
     - https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Napoleon%27s_Addresses/Part_IV
     - https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Napoleon%27s_Addresses/Part_V
   - Checked against the Library of Congress scan of the 1897 edition on the Internet Archive: https://archive.org/details/napoleonsaddress00napo (OCR text: `napoleonsaddress00napo_djvu.txt`). A word-level script compared every selected item with the scan's OCR. The only differences were OCR misreads ("lias" for "has", "Mojiiteur" for "Moniteur" and so on), page headers, and accents that Wikisource restored (Kléber, Lainé, écrasé). No wording differences were found.
   - Translator: the book credits no translator. Tarbell is named only as editor, and her introduction says the texts come from the *Correspondance de Napoléon Ier*. So the translator is unnamed (presumably Tarbell or the publisher).
   - Wikisource leaves out the heading of the first item in each Part. Those headings were taken from the scan's table of contents.
2. **Proclamation after Jena (Potsdam, October 1806):** Alexandre Dumas, *Napoleon*, translated by John B. Larner (New York: G. P. Putnam's Sons, 1894), pages 98–99. Public domain in the US. Scan: https://archive.org/details/cu31924008954780 (Cornell University Library). Transcribed from the OCR text, with line-end hyphens rejoined. It was checked word by word against the OCR, and every word matched. Dumas dates it "the 27th" from Potsdam. Many sources give October 26, so the context line says only "late October 1806". Larner's text spells the river "the Sale" (the Saale), and this is kept.
3. **"Forty centuries" at the Pyramids:** William Milligan Sloane, *The Life of Napoleon Bonaparte*, vol. 2 (New York: Century, 1896). Project Gutenberg #31026: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/31026. Sloane writes: "Soldiers," cried the general, "forty centuries look down upon you from the summit of the Pyramids!" Only Napoleon's words are kept, so the narrative tag "cried the general" is dropped.
   - This is a reported exclamation, not a written proclamation. Tarbell has no proclamation for the battle. *The World's Famous Orations*, vol. 7 (1906) says in a footnote that the remark "was not part of any of these speeches" and gives Thiers's account: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_World%27s_Famous_Orations/Volume_7/During_the_Egyptian_Campaign
   - Other public-domain renderings differ. Lockhart (1829, Gutenberg #17579) gives "from the summit of yonder pyramids forty ages behold you".
4. **Context lines:** Dates follow Tarbell's headings, except where noted.
   - "Headquarters at Nice, March 1796" comes from Sloane, vol. 1 (Gutenberg #24360), who dates that address March 27, 1796 at Nice.
   - The December 10, 1797 date and the Paris setting for the address to the Directory come from Tarbell's heading and the *World's Famous Orations* note. Tarbell's own heading misprints the year as "1787".
   - "Before Entering Warsaw" is dated January 1, 1807 by Tarbell, but its first sentence places it on the anniversary of Austerlitz. The context line says so rather than choosing a date.
   - Tarbell dates both Elba proclamations March 5, 1815. The *Correspondance* dates them March 1, at Golfe-Juan. The context lines say only "March 1815".
   - Tarbell dates the presentation of the eagles December 3, 1804, and the context line says only "December 1804, days after the coronation".

## Editorial handling

- Spoken text is verbatim from the sources above. A script compared the body of `speech.md` (with headings, italic lines and cues removed) against the extracted source text, and it matched word for word (11,356 words).
- Removed: the quotation marks Tarbell puts around each paragraph of Napoleon's words. Single quotation marks inside the text are kept.
- Removed as not spoken: datelines and letterhead lines, namely "Headquarters on board the Orient, the 4th Messidor, year 6", "Bonaparte, Member of the National Institute...", "19th Brumaire, 11 o'clock, p. m.", and the address line to the Military Commissioners of the Ninth Division.
- In the Farewell to the Old Guard, Tarbell's narrative sentence ("Napoleon then ordered the eagles to be brought, and, having embraced them, he added:") is kept verbatim as an italic stage line, because it is not spoken.
- Long paragraphs were broken at sentence boundaries into paragraphs of about 3 sentences. Very short exclamations ("Soldiers!") stay attached to the next sentence. No words or punctuation were changed. Some single sentences chained with semicolons are still long, the longest being in the Jena proclamation.
- Numbers keep their printed form ("15,000", "39th", "1794"). The brief asks for transcriptions to be verbatim, and that rule was put ahead of the style rule about spelled-out numbers. The reader should say them aloud as words.
- Period spellings and misprints are kept as printed: "Capau" (Capua), "Vergniands" (Vergniaud), "Witepsk", "Konisberg", "the integrity of. your territory" (a stray period in the 1897 printing, kept as printed).
- Six cues were added: [PAUSE], [BUILD] twice, [LET IT LAND] three times and [SLOW] twice. Each is on its own line.

## Caveats

- The translations are nineteenth-century and somewhat free. Tarbell does not say whether her items are complete. Some are probably abridged or excerpted (for example, "Proclamation to the French before the Second Italian Campaign" reads as a fragment). They were not checked against the French *Correspondance*.
- Some Tarbell headings carry approximate or doubtful dates (see "Context lines"). Her titles were shortened in the `###` headings for the HUD.
- The Pyramids line is a reported exclamation, not a document.
- The farewell to the Old Guard is also a reported speech, recorded by those present.
