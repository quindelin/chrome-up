# The Meaning Crisis

## I. The Mutiny at Opis

In the summer of three hundred and twenty-four years before Christ, on the banks of the Tigris River, at a town called Opis, an army turned on its king.

They were Macedonians. They had marched with Alexander for ten years. They had crossed the Hellespont, beaten the Persian Empire in three great battles, walked through Egypt and Babylon and the high passes of the Hindu Kush, and come down into India. They had followed him farther from home than any army of their people had ever gone.

And now, at Opis, he called them together and announced that the old and the wounded among them would be sent home, with gifts, with honor, with enough money to make their neighbors envious.

He thought he was being generous. They heard something else.

[PAUSE]

They heard that they were no longer needed. They saw Persian nobles wearing Macedonian honors. They saw young Persians being drilled in Macedonian ways of war. They saw their king in Persian dress. And from the crowd came a roar: send us all home, then. Send us all home, and go conquer the world with your father, the god.

It was an insult, and it was a mutiny.

Our account of what happened next comes from the historian Arrian, who wrote it down some four and a half centuries later, working from memoirs by men who were there. The words he gives Alexander are almost certainly Arrian's words more than Alexander's. But they have been read for nearly two thousand years because of what they do.

Alexander leapt down from the platform. He had thirteen of the loudest men seized. And in the terrible silence that followed, he climbed back up and spoke.

He did not begin with threats. He began with an inventory.

[SLOW]

My father Philip, he said, found you wandering. Poor. Many of you dressed in sheepskins, grazing a few sheep on the mountainsides, fighting off your neighbors and losing. He gave you cloaks instead of skins. He brought you down from the mountains to the plains. He made you city dwellers, and gave you laws and customs to live by. He made the people who used to plunder you into your subjects.

And then he turned to himself. I inherited a few gold and silver cups and less than sixty talents in the treasury, and debts of five hundred more. I borrowed eight hundred talents on top of that, and with it I opened the road to Asia. Everything we took, Egypt and Babylon and the treasure of Persia, I took for you. What do I have for myself? A purple cloak and a crown.

Show me your wounds, he said, and I will show you mine. There is no part of the front of my body without a scar.

And then, at the very end, the reversal.

You want to go home? Then go. All of you. Go home and tell them that your king, who led you across the world, you left behind among strangers. Tell them that. I am sure it will sound glorious to men and pious to the gods.

Depart.

[LET IT LAND]

He walked off the platform and shut himself in his tent for two days. He would not eat. He would not see anyone. On the third day he called in the Persian nobles and began to give them the commands.

And the Macedonians broke.

They ran to the royal tent. They threw down their weapons in front of the doors as a sign that they were begging. They stood there day and night and refused to leave. When Alexander finally came out and saw them, Arrian says, he wept.

An old cavalry officer named Callines stepped forward and said what was really wrong. You have made Persians your kinsmen, he said. They are allowed to kiss you. No Macedonian has ever had that honor.

And Alexander said: I make all of you my kinsmen. From this moment, I call you all my kin.

Callines kissed him first, and then the others came, one by one. And that night there was a feast. Macedonians sat closest to the king, Persians next to them, then men of every other nation in the army. Arrian says about nine thousand people sat down at that table. They all drew wine from the same bowl and poured the same offering. And Alexander prayed for blessings, and for concord, and for partnership between peoples who had been enemies.

[PAUSE]

I did not come here tonight to praise Alexander. He was a conqueror. The empire he built was paid for in other people's cities and other people's sons, and it fell apart within a few years of his death. I am not holding him up as a model of how to live.

I am telling you this story because of the two questions buried inside it. They are the questions that speech forced on every man standing in front of that platform.

The first question is this. What have we built together?

And the second is this. What do we owe?

[SLOW]

I want to put those two questions to us tonight, as Americans, in the year two thousand and twenty-six. Because I believe we are standing in front of our own platform. I believe a great many of us have been shouting, in our own way, send us home, we do not need any of this. And I believe that a great many of us, when we get home, are finding that home is emptier than we thought.

The name people have started to give this is the meaning crisis.

It is the slow draining of the things that told human beings who they were, what they were for, and to whom they belonged. The decline of religious life. The decline of marriage. The decline of the lodge and the league and the union hall. The decline of a shared story about what this country is and where it is going. And in their place, a hunger that politics, and wellness, and our screens keep promising to feed and keep failing to satisfy.

I am going to spend our time together in four movements.

First, the inventory: what our grandparents and great-grandparents built, and what has happened to it. Second, the diagnosis: why it fell, and the strongest case that its fall is a good thing. Third, the substitutes: the things we have reached for instead, and why they cannot carry the weight. And fourth, the reckoning: what human beings have always leaned on, and what we owe to each other now.

I will try to be honest with you. Where the numbers are clear, I will give you the numbers. Where the experts disagree, I will tell you so. Where I am giving you my opinion, I will say that it is my opinion.

But I will not pretend that I have no view. I do. I think we are richer than any people in history and lonelier than we have any right to be. I think we have inherited a house built by people who sacrificed to build it, and we have been tearing out the beams to burn for warmth. And I think it is not too late to stop.

[BUILD]

So let me begin where Alexander began. Not with a threat. With an inventory.

## II. The Inventory

They found us scattered.

I mean the generations who came before us. They found this continent full of people who had left everything behind. Villages in Ireland and Sicily and Norway and Poland. Plantations in the Caribbean. Farms in Mexico. Shtetls in the Pale of Settlement. People who had crossed an ocean and lost, in the crossing, nearly every institution that had held their lives together.

And they did not simply survive. They built.

[SLOW]

They built churches. Not one or two. Tens of thousands of them. In the first years of a new town on the prairie, before there was a paved road, before there was a bank, there was often a white frame church with a bell. Immigrants who could barely afford their own bread took up collections to build cathedrals in the cities where they landed, stone by stone, so that their grandchildren would have somewhere to be baptized and married and buried.

They built synagogues on the Lower East Side and in Charleston and in Cincinnati. They built Black churches in the South that became, under slavery and then under Jim Crow, the one institution that no one could take from them, the place where people learned to read, learned to organize, learned to lead, and learned to believe that they had a worth that the law of the land denied.

They built lodges. The Masons and the Odd Fellows. The Elks and the Moose and the Knights of Columbus. The Grange for farmers. Mutual aid societies for every immigrant group that arrived, so that when a man was killed in a mine or a mill, his widow would not be put out on the street.

They built unions. They fought, and some of them died, for the right to bargain together, and the union hall became a place where a man was not only a pair of hands but a brother, with a vote and a voice.

They built women's clubs and temperance societies and suffrage associations. They built the Parent Teacher Association and the Boy Scouts and the Red Cross. They built volunteer fire departments, which still today, in most of the towns of this country, are staffed by neighbors who will run into your burning house for no pay at all.

[PAUSE]

A Frenchman came to see what they had built. His name was Alexis de Tocqueville, and he traveled through this country in the early eighteen-thirties. What astonished him was not our government. It was this habit of ours.

"Americans of all ages, all conditions, and all dispositions," he wrote, "constantly form associations."

He said we made associations to give entertainments and to found schools, to build inns and to build churches, to send missionaries to the ends of the earth. Where in France you would find the government at the head of some new undertaking, and in England a lord, in America, he said, you would be sure to find an association.

He thought this was the secret. A democracy, he believed, threatened to dissolve people into isolated individuals, each one equal, each one alone. The associations were what held them together. They were schools of citizenship. They taught people that their own interest was bound up with the interest of their neighbors.

[SLOW]

And they built marriages. I know that sounds like a strange thing to put on a list of institutions. But it is the oldest institution of all. For most of our history, the household was the basic unit of the economy, the school, the hospital, the nursing home, and the retirement plan. A marriage was not mainly a romance. It was a promise, made in public, before witnesses, that two people and the families behind them would bear each other's burdens for as long as they both should live.

Those marriages were often hard. Some of them were cruel. I will come back to that, because it matters. But they were the frame in which most people raised children, and grew old, and understood their own lives as part of a story longer than themselves.

And finally, they built a story.

Not one story that everyone agreed with. Americans have always fought over what this country means. But a story big enough to argue inside of. A story about liberty and about a people who had, in Washington's words in his Farewell Address, "in a common cause fought and triumphed together." A story about the immigrant who arrives with nothing and whose children go to college. A story about a nation that had sinned terribly and could be called back to its own creed, which is what Frederick Douglass and Abraham Lincoln and Martin Luther King all did. They did not throw away the story. They held the country to it.

[BUILD]

So here is the inventory.

They gave us congregations, where we were known by name, where our children were blessed, where there was a casserole on the doorstep when someone died.

They gave us associations, where we learned to run a meeting, to raise money for a cause, to sit across the table from someone we disagreed with and get something done anyway.

They gave us unions and lodges, where work was not only a transaction but a fellowship.

They gave us marriage and family as a public promise, not only a private feeling.

They gave us a story, a shared argument about who we are, wide enough that everyone could find a place inside it.

[PAUSE]

Not one of these things was perfect. Every one of them excluded somebody. The lodges were often segregated. The churches sometimes protected the powerful and silenced the abused. The unions sometimes kept out Black workers and women. The family could be a prison. The national story left out whole peoples who were here first, and whole peoples who were brought here in chains.

I will not pretend otherwise. An honest inventory has a debit column.

But notice what the reformers did, for most of our history, when they found an institution that excluded them. They did not burn it down. They demanded to be let in. Black Americans built their own churches and their own lodges, the Prince Hall Masons, the Elks of the World, because they were barred from the white ones, and because they knew the value of the thing. Women did not abolish the club. They founded their own and made it a political force that won them the vote. Catholics and Jews, shut out of the Protestant establishment, built a parallel world of hospitals and schools and charities that still serves millions of people of every faith and none.

What they understood was that belonging is not a luxury. It is the thing that makes the rest of a life possible.

And so, like Alexander on that platform, I want to ask: what has happened to all of it?

What have we done with what they built?

## III. The Ledger

Let me read you the ledger. I will go slowly, because each of these numbers is a great many human lives.

[SLOW]

In nineteen thirty-seven, the Gallup organization first asked Americans whether they belonged to a church, a synagogue, or a mosque. Seventy-three percent said yes. For about sixty years, that number barely moved. In nineteen ninety-nine, it was still seventy percent.

In twenty twenty, for the first time in the eight decades Gallup had been asking, it fell below half. Forty-seven percent.

Seventy percent to forty-seven percent in about twenty years. Something that had held steady through the Depression, through a world war, through the sixties, gave way in a single generation.

Gallup found that the main reason was simple. More and more people said they had no religious preference at all. That group grew from eight percent around the turn of the century to twenty-one percent. And of those people, only four percent belonged to any congregation. The decline was steepest among the young. Two-thirds of Americans born before nineteen forty-six were members of a congregation. Among millennials, it was about one in three.

[PAUSE]

The Pew Research Center tells the same story from a different angle. In two thousand and seven, seventy-eight percent of American adults called themselves Christians. In Pew's great survey of twenty twenty-three and twenty twenty-four, which interviewed nearly thirty-seven thousand people, that number was sixty-two percent. Meanwhile the religiously unaffiliated, the people researchers call the nones, the atheists and agnostics and the people who say they are nothing in particular, stood at twenty-nine percent. Nearly three in ten Americans.

Now I promised to be honest with you, so here is the other side of that report. Pew found that the decline appears to have slowed, and may have leveled off, at least for now. The Christian share has been roughly stable since twenty nineteen. The nones have stopped growing, at least for the moment.

That is real, and I do not want to wave it away. But Pew also found something that should trouble anyone who thinks the story is over. Young adults are far less religious than older adults, and, in Pew's words, no recent birth cohort has become more religious as it has aged. The old pattern, where young people drift away and then come back when they marry and have children, is weakening. The generations are not returning. They are being replaced.

[SLOW]

Now turn the page of the ledger to marriage.

In nineteen eighty, six percent of Americans who reached the age of forty had never been married. Six percent. By twenty ten, it was twenty percent. By twenty twenty-one, according to Pew's analysis of Census data, it was twenty-five percent. One in four forty-year-olds in this country has never married. A record.

And this is not, mostly, a story of people living happily together without the paperwork. Pew found that most of those never-married adults in their early forties were not living with a partner at all.

The divide runs along lines of class. Forty-year-olds without a four-year college degree are more likely never to have married than those with one. Marriage, which was once the common inheritance of the working family, is becoming one more thing that the educated and the comfortable keep for themselves.

[PAUSE]

Turn the page again, to the union hall. In nineteen eighty-three, the first year the Bureau of Labor Statistics kept comparable records, twenty point one percent of American wage and salary workers belonged to a union. Last year, it was ten percent. Half.

Turn it again, to the associations Tocqueville admired. In nineteen ninety-five, the political scientist Robert Putnam published an essay, and five years later a book, called Bowling Alone. He noticed that more Americans than ever were bowling, but fewer and fewer were bowling in leagues. He used it as a symbol for something much larger: the steady decline, since about the middle of the twentieth century, in the PTA, the Scouts, the Red Cross, the unions, the religious organizations, the public meeting, the committee. The groups that were growing were mostly the kind you join by mailing a check. You get a newsletter. You never meet another member.

Putnam's book is now a quarter century old. Nothing since has reversed it.

[SLOW]

Turn the page once more, to friendship itself.

In nineteen ninety, when Americans were asked how many close friends they had, three percent said none. In twenty twenty-one, the Survey Center on American Life asked the same kind of question. Twelve percent said none. Four times as many. And the share who said they had ten or more close friends fell from about a third to about one in eight.

You can argue about the survey methods. The twenty twenty-one figure was taken in the middle of a pandemic, and the researchers said so. So let me give you a measure that does not depend on how people describe themselves. Let me give you the clock.

Every year, the Bureau of Labor Statistics asks thousands of Americans to account for how they spent every minute of a day. In twenty fourteen, the average American spent forty-three minutes a day socializing and communicating with other people, outside of work. In twenty twenty-four, it was thirty-five minutes. On an average day in twenty fourteen, thirty-eight percent of Americans did any socializing at all. In twenty twenty-four, thirty percent.

On any given day in this country, seven out of ten people do not spend a single recorded minute socializing with another human being for its own sake.

[LET IT LAND]

So there is the ledger. Congregations, down by a third. Christian identification, down sixteen points in under twenty years. The never-married at forty, more than quadrupled since nineteen eighty. Union membership, cut in half. Close friendship, thinned. Time with other people, shrinking by the year.

Each of these numbers has its own causes, and I will get to them. Some of them have been leveling off. Some of them have good explanations. But put them side by side and a pattern appears that no single explanation can account for.

The structures that once wrapped around an American life, from the cradle to the grave, have been coming down one by one. The church that baptized you. The spouse who promised before witnesses. The lodge that buried you. The union that stood with you. The friends who knew you. The neighbors who sat with you on a summer night.

They are not all gone. Tens of millions of Americans still live inside them, and many of those Americans are thriving. But for a growing share of us, especially the young, especially the working class, especially men and increasingly women too, the scaffolding is gone, and nothing has been put up in its place.

And it turns out that human beings were never built to stand without it.

## IV. What the Loss Feels Like

Numbers are the ledger. But the loss is not lived as a number. It is lived at two in the morning.

[PAUSE]

In May of twenty twenty-three, the Surgeon General of the United States did something unusual. He issued a formal advisory, the kind of public warning his office has used for smoking and for drunk driving, and it was about loneliness. He called it an epidemic.

He reported that about half of American adults said they had experienced loneliness. And he cited research finding that a lack of social connection raises the risk of early death about as much as smoking up to fifteen cigarettes a day. More than obesity. More than physical inactivity. Loneliness, he warned, is tied to heart disease, to stroke, to dementia, to depression and anxiety.

Think about that. We spent half a century and billions of dollars persuading Americans to stop smoking, and we were right to. And all the while, we were quietly building a way of life that does to the body what a pack of cigarettes a day does.

[SLOW]

A year later, researchers at Harvard's Making Caring Common project went looking for what lies underneath the loneliness. They found that about twenty-one percent of American adults reported feeling lonely. One in five. That alone is a sobering number.

But the finding I cannot stop thinking about is this one. Among those lonely adults, seventy-five percent said they had little or no meaning or purpose in their lives. Three out of four. Sixty-seven percent said they did not feel part of any meaningful group. And when the researchers asked all Americans what was driving the loneliness, the top answer, at seventy-three percent, was technology.

Loneliness and meaninglessness, it turns out, travel together. The researchers were careful. They said the arrow probably points both ways. People without purpose withdraw; people who withdraw lose their purpose. But that is exactly the point. The two cannot be pulled apart, because they are two faces of the same thing. A human life finds its meaning in the people it is bound to. Cut the bonds and the meaning drains out with them.

[PAUSE]

Now I want to speak carefully, because this is the hardest part of the ledger.

In twenty twenty-four, a little over forty-eight thousand eight hundred Americans died by suicide. According to the National Center for Health Statistics, the suicide rate rose from thirteen per hundred thousand in twenty fourteen to fourteen in twenty seventeen, and has stayed near that level since, dipping slightly to thirteen point seven in twenty twenty-four.

I do not want to oversimplify that. Every suicide has its own story. Mental illness is real and it is medical. Access to firearms matters enormously; more than half of these deaths involve a gun. Economics matters. No speech should pretend that a single cause explains forty-eight thousand deaths.

But I want to tell you about a man who studied this question more than a century ago.

[SLOW]

His name was Émile Durkheim. He was a French sociologist, and in eighteen ninety-seven he published a book called Suicide. Durkheim's insight was that something as private and as desperate as taking one's own life also has a social pattern. Rates rose and fell with the strength of a person's ties to a community.

He found suicide was more common among the unmarried than the married. More common among people without children than among those with children. More common among Protestants, whose faith asked each person to stand alone before God, than among Catholics and Jews, whose faith bound them into a thick community of practice. He found it rose when people were weakly woven into any group, and he called that kind egoistic. And he found it rose when the rules and expectations of a society dissolved, when people no longer knew what they could hope for or what was asked of them. He had a name for that condition. Anomie. A kind of moral weightlessness.

[PAUSE]

Durkheim was writing about nineteenth-century Europe. He could not have imagined our world. But read his list again, and then look at the ledger I just read you. Fewer marriages. Fewer children. Fewer congregations. Fewer associations. Less time with other people. Fewer shared rules about what a good life looks like.

We have run Durkheim's experiment on ourselves, at the scale of a continent.

And then there is what two Princeton economists found. In twenty fifteen, Anne Case and Angus Deaton published a study showing that, between nineteen ninety-nine and twenty thirteen, death rates for middle-aged white Americans had stopped falling and begun to rise, at a time when they were falling for almost every other group in almost every other rich country. The cause was not heart disease or cancer. It was suicide, drug overdoses, and alcoholic liver disease. They gave it a name that has stuck: deaths of despair.

And they found the despair was concentrated among people without a college degree. The people for whom the steady factory job had vanished, the union had faded, the marriage had not happened or had come apart, and the church on the corner had closed. Case and Deaton were careful to say that falling income alone could not explain it. Something larger had been lost. A whole way of life that had told a working man and a working woman who they were.

[SLOW]

That despair is not confined to one group. It has spread. The World Happiness Report, which ranks countries by how their people rate their own lives, used to place the United States comfortably in the top twenty. In twenty twenty-four, we fell to twenty-third. In twenty twenty-five, twenty-fourth. And the main reason, the researchers said, was the young. In the twenty twenty-four report, if you counted only Americans under thirty, the United States ranked sixty-second in the world.

The richest nation in human history. And our young people rate their lives below those of young people in dozens of poorer countries.

[LET IT LAND]

I want you to hold that in your mind as we move to the next question. Because it points to something we have badly misunderstood. We have assumed that if we could only make people free enough, and comfortable enough, and well-entertained enough, they would be happy.

And it turns out that is not what human beings are made of.

## V. What Humans Have Always Leaned On

So let us ask the oldest question. What are human beings made of? What have we always leaned on, in every age and on every continent, to bear the weight of being alive?

[PAUSE]

I want to suggest that there have been, broadly, four great supports. Every civilization has built some version of them, in its own language and its own architecture. They are not the property of any one religion or any one culture. They are, as far as anyone can tell, the common inheritance of our species.

The first is the sacred.

By that I do not mean any particular creed. I mean the sense that there is something greater than the self, something worthy of reverence, something that stands above our appetites and judges them. In the cathedral and the mosque and the synagogue and the temple, but also at the graveside and the wedding, at the sight of a newborn, under the open stars. Every society we know of has had some way of marking certain times and places and acts as holy, set apart, not for sale.

The sacred does a specific job in a human life. It tells you that you are not the center. And paradoxically, that is a relief. It means that your worth does not depend on your performance. It means your suffering may not be meaningless. It means there is a standard outside of you to which you can appeal when the powerful are wrong, and which can call you to account when you are.

[SLOW]

The second support is the household.

The family, in all its many shapes across history. The people who are yours, not because you chose them on an app, but because you were born to them or promised yourself to them. The ones who will come to the hospital. The ones you are obliged to feed, and who are obliged to feed you when you are old.

The household gives a person a place in a chain of generations. You were someone's child before you were anything else. You may become someone's parent, someone's grandparent. Your life has a before and an after. It is a chapter, not the whole book.

The third support is the city, in the old Greek sense. The polis. The guild, the village, the parish, the tribe, the regiment, the team, the union, the club. The middle layer between the family and the state. It is where we learn to work with people who are not our kin, toward a purpose bigger than any of us. It is where a person earns standing, where they are known and needed. It is where Tocqueville's Americans learned to be citizens.

The fourth support is the story.

Every people has told itself a story about where it came from and where it is going. Creation and exodus. Founding and fall. Exile and return. The story gives a person's small daily labor its place in a larger drama. The farmer plowing his field is not only raising corn. He is building the promised land, or keeping faith with his ancestors, or making a nation of free men. The story is what turns a task into a vocation.

[PAUSE]

And woven through all four supports is something we have nearly forgotten how to do. Ritual.

[SLOW]

Every one of the old supports came with its own rituals, and the rituals did quiet, essential work. The baptism and the bris told a family that this child belonged not only to them but to a people. The coming-of-age ceremony, the confirmation, the bar or bat mitzvah, the quinceañera, the first hunt, the graduation, told a young person that childhood was over and that the community now expected something of them. The wedding, said aloud before witnesses, told two people that their promise was not a private feeling but a public fact, and that the whole room would hold them to it.

And the funeral. Above all, the funeral. Every culture we know of has gathered around its dead. The wake, the shiva, the procession, the casseroles, the long afternoon of stories. Ritual is how human beings carry grief that is too heavy for one person. It tells the mourner: you are not alone, and this death is not meaningless, and life will go on, and we will walk with you while it does.

[PAUSE]

Now think about what has happened to those rituals. Many weddings have become elaborate productions about the couple's personal brand rather than solemn promises before a community. Many young people pass from childhood to adulthood with no marker at all, except perhaps a driver's license and a phone plan. And many families, unmoored from any congregation, no longer know what to do when someone dies. They hold no service, or a small private one, and the mourner goes back to work on Monday as if nothing happened.

I do not say this to blame anyone. People who have no tradition cannot be expected to practice one. But we should see clearly what has been lost. A ritual is a kind of technology. It is a tool, refined over thousands of years, for getting human beings through the passages of life together. When we abandon it, we do not become more free. We simply face the hardest moments of life with fewer tools and less company.

[SLOW]

The rituals of the old supports also kept time. The sabbath, whether Friday or Saturday or Sunday, marked one day in seven as different. The church year, the Jewish calendar, the month of Ramadan, the harvest festival, gave the year a shape. People fasted together and feasted together. They knew what season it was, not only by the weather, but by what their community was doing.

Our time, by contrast, has become flat. Every day is a workday, and every night is a screen night. The feed does not know whether it is Tuesday or Easter. And a life with no shape to its time is a life that is easy to waste, because there is no moment that tells you to stop and remember what all of it is for.

Now here is what I want you to notice. Each of those four supports does the same thing in a different way. Each one binds the individual to something larger than himself, and in doing so, makes demands on him.

The sacred asks for reverence and obedience. The household asks for loyalty and sacrifice. The city asks for service and participation. The story asks for faithfulness to the people who came before and the people who will come after.

And it is precisely in answering those demands that people have found their lives to be meaningful.

[SLOW]

There is a remarkable piece of evidence for this, and it comes from an unexpected place. In twenty fourteen, two psychologists, Shigehiro Oishi and Ed Diener, looked at the Gallup World Poll, which had surveyed more than a hundred and forty thousand people in a hundred and thirty-two countries. They asked a simple question. Where in the world do people say their lives have an important purpose or meaning?

You might expect the answer to be the rich countries. The places with the best hospitals, the most education, the most freedom, the most opportunity. And indeed, people in richer countries reported more satisfaction with their lives.

But not more meaning. When it came to meaning, the pattern ran the other way. People in poorer countries were more likely to say their lives had purpose. And the main thing that explained it, even after the researchers accounted for education and family size and individualism, was religion. In the poorer countries, people were far more likely to say that religion was an important part of their daily lives. And that was what predicted meaning.

[PAUSE]

I want to be careful about what that study shows. It compares countries, not individuals. It does not prove that any one person would find more meaning by becoming more religious, and its authors said more research was needed. I am not standing here telling anyone to adopt a faith because a survey says so. That is not how faith works, and it would be an insult to believers and unbelievers alike.

But the pattern is hard to ignore. Comfort and meaning are not the same thing. You can have more of one and less of the other. A society can grow steadily richer, safer, healthier and freer, and at the very same time grow steadily emptier.

[SLOW]

And there is a man who understood that better than almost anyone. His name was Viktor Frankl. He was a Viennese psychiatrist, and he was Jewish, and during the Second World War he was sent to the Nazi concentration camps. His wife, his mother, his father, and his brother were killed. He survived.

In nineteen forty-six, he published a short book that has since sold more than sixteen million copies. In English it is called Man's Search for Meaning. In the camps, Frankl noticed that the prisoners who held on longest were not always the strongest in body. They were often the ones who still had something to live for. A child waiting somewhere. A manuscript to finish. A loved one to see again. Frankl liked to borrow an idea from Nietzsche: that a person who has a why to live for can bear almost any how.

Out of that experience, Frankl argued that human beings find meaning in three ways. In the work we do and the things we create. In the people we love. And in the attitude we take toward suffering that cannot be avoided.

[LET IT LAND]

Notice what each of those has in common. Every one of them points outward. A task to be done. A person to be loved. A suffering to be borne with dignity, often for someone else's sake. Meaning, in Frankl's account, is never found by staring at oneself. It is found by being called out of oneself, toward something or someone that needs you.

That is the deepest thing I want to say tonight, and I will keep coming back to it. Meaning is not a feeling you can manufacture inside your own head. It is a relationship. It is what happens when you are bound to something worth being bound to, and you answer the call.

And the four great supports, the sacred, the household, the city and the story, are the places where human beings have always heard that call.

So why did we stop listening?

## VI. Why It Fell

I want to give you an honest account of why the old supports fell. Because if we get the causes wrong, we will get the cures wrong. And because the honest answer is not a story about villains.

[PAUSE]

The first cause is, believe it or not, success.

For most of human history, people needed each other in a very direct way. If your barn burned down, your neighbors raised a new one, because there was no insurance company. If you got sick, your family nursed you, because there was no hospital you could afford. If your husband died, your church and your lodge kept your children fed, because there was no Social Security. Belonging was not a hobby. It was survival.

Then we got rich. And we built, through markets and through government, a vast machinery that does many of those jobs better than the neighbors ever did. Insurance. Pensions. Hospitals. Unemployment benefits. Restaurants and delivery. Professional care for the elderly. These are wonderful achievements. Nobody here wants to go back to a world where a widow depends on charity for her children's bread.

But something came along with them that nobody quite intended. When you no longer need your neighbors, you stop knowing them. The ties that were once enforced by necessity now had to be maintained by choice. And it turns out that a great many ties, when they become optional, are quietly dropped.

[SLOW]

The second cause is mobility. Americans have always moved, but in the twentieth century we moved for college and for jobs and for the sun, and we left behind the towns where our families had lived for generations. Every move cut roots. The grandmother who might have helped raise the children was a plane ride away. The church you grew up in was in another state. The friends from high school were scattered across the country.

The third cause is the economy itself. The steady, unionized, single-employer job that could support a family on one income, the job your father held for thirty years, began disappearing in the nineteen seventies and eighties. Manufacturing moved overseas or was automated. The towns that were built around a mill or a plant hollowed out. And with the job went the union hall, the company softball league, the bar where the shift gathered on Friday, and for many men, the sense that they could offer a wife and children a stable life. It is no accident, I believe, that marriage has receded furthest among Americans without a college degree, the very Americans for whom those jobs disappeared. Case and Deaton tell a version of that story.

[PAUSE]

The fourth cause is a failure of the institutions themselves. And I want to be direct about this, because people who love these institutions sometimes do not want to hear it.

Some of our churches betrayed the people who trusted them. The abuse scandals that came to light, beginning in earnest around two thousand and two, in the Catholic Church and later in other denominations, were not a misunderstanding. They were a betrayal, and the cover-ups were worse than the crimes. Many people did not drift away from their faith. They were driven out by the people who were supposed to shepherd them.

Some of our churches, too, became so tangled up with partisan politics that, for millions of younger Americans, the word Christian came to mean a voting bloc rather than a way of life. Pew found that among Americans who call themselves liberals, the share who identify as Christian fell from sixty-two percent in two thousand and seven to thirty-seven percent. Among conservatives it fell too, but far less. Whatever you think of that, it tells you that faith and politics have become badly fused in many people's minds, and that the fusion has cost the churches a generation.

And some of the old associations simply failed to adapt. The lodge that would not admit women. The club that would not admit Black members. The union that grew complacent and corrupt. They lost the young because they did not offer the young anything they wanted.

[SLOW]

The fifth cause is the screen, and I will spend a whole section on it later. For now, just remember what Putnam found. When he tried to estimate the causes of the decline in civic life after the middle nineteen sixties, he attributed about half to generational change, the replacement of the generation that came of age in the Depression and the war with generations that never joined at the same rates. And of the rest, the largest single factor he identified was television. Electronic entertainment that took leisure, which had been something people did together, out on the porch and down at the hall, and moved it into the living room, and then into the bedroom, and now into every pocket.

[PAUSE]

And the sixth cause, the deepest, is an idea.

It is the idea that the highest good in life is to be free to choose. That every obligation you did not choose is a kind of chain. That the self is sovereign, and that the purpose of a human life is to discover your authentic self and express it, and that any institution, any tradition, any relationship that stands in the way of that self-expression is an obstacle to be removed.

That idea has deep roots. It has some real truth in it. It powered the great liberation movements of the last two centuries, and I would not undo those for anything.

But taken as the whole truth about human beings, it has a cost. If every bond is a chain, then the freest person is the one with no bonds at all. And that person, as it happens, is exactly the person Durkheim found most likely to despair.

[LET IT LAND]

So there are the causes. Success. Mobility. Economic upheaval. Institutional failure. Technology. And an idea of freedom that forgot what freedom is for.

Notice that none of these is a conspiracy. None of them was designed by anyone to make us lonely. Most of them came from good intentions and produced real goods. That is what makes this crisis so hard to see and so hard to fix. Nobody stole the beams out of the house. We pulled them out ourselves, one at a time, each for a good reason, and only now are we noticing that the roof is sagging.

## VII. The Case for Good Riddance

Now I owe you the strongest argument against everything I have said so far. A speech that does not face its best opponent is not an argument. It is a sermon. So let me make the other case as well as I can.

[PAUSE]

Here it is.

You are romanticizing the past. The old supports you are mourning were also cages. The church you are nostalgic for told gay teenagers they were damned, and some of those teenagers killed themselves. It told women their place was to submit. It covered up the rape of children for decades. The marriages you miss included millions of women trapped with men who beat them, with no money of their own and no way out. Divorce became easier, and studies have linked that change to falling rates of domestic violence and of suicide among married women. The lodges were whites only. The tight-knit small town was also the place where everyone knew your business and anyone different was driven out.

What you call a meaning crisis, the argument goes, is actually the sound of human beings being set free. Of course it is disorienting. Freedom is always disorienting. Men who had been told what to believe now have to think for themselves. Women who had been told whom to marry now get to choose, or not to marry at all. Young people who would have been forced into the faith of their parents can now find their own path. That is not a crisis. That is a triumph, and the discomfort is the price of it.

[SLOW]

And besides, the argument continues, the nones are not nihilists. Look at your own statistics. Pew found that eighty-three percent of Americans still believe in God or some universal spirit. Eighty-six percent believe people have a soul or spirit beyond the body. Seventy-nine percent believe in something spiritual beyond the natural world. Americans have not stopped searching for meaning. They have just stopped outsourcing the search to institutions that failed them.

Millions of people with no religion at all live lives of deep purpose. They raise children with love. They work as nurses and teachers and firefighters. They volunteer. They are faithful to their spouses and their friends. To suggest that meaning requires a church or a marriage is not only false, it is insulting to every one of them.

[PAUSE]

That is a serious argument. And I want to say plainly: much of it is true.

The old institutions did harm, real harm, and the people who left them often had good reason to leave. The freedom to leave a violent marriage is a moral achievement. The freedom to worship differently, or not at all, is a moral achievement. Nobody who cares about human dignity should want to take those freedoms away. I do not.

And it is absolutely true that a person can live a deeply meaningful life outside any church, outside any marriage. I know such people. You know such people. Meaning is not the property of the religious or the married.

[SLOW]

So let me tell you where I think the argument goes wrong.

It goes wrong in assuming that the only alternative to a bad institution is no institution. That is a false choice, and it has cost us dearly.

When you leave a church that wronged you, you are right to leave. But the human needs that church met, for a community that knows your name, for a weekly gathering with people of every age, for rituals that mark birth and marriage and death, for a moral language bigger than your own preferences, for someone to sit with your widow, those needs do not leave with you. They follow you out the door. And if there is nothing on the other side of the door to meet them, they go unmet.

The real question was never whether people should be free to leave. It was where they would go when they left.

[PAUSE]

Look again at the people the argument holds up as proof. The nurse. The teacher. The firefighter. The faithful spouse. The volunteer. What do every one of them have in common? They are bound. They have a calling, a crew, a classroom, a ward, a person they have promised themselves to, a cause they show up for every week. They did not find meaning by having no obligations. They found it by taking on obligations that they chose to honor.

That is my point exactly. Meaning does not require any one institution. But it does require some institution. It requires some set of bonds that make demands on you, that you cannot walk away from on a whim, that persist when the feeling fades.

The secular person who lives a full life has, almost always, built or found a structure that does what the old supports did. The tragedy of our time is not that people are leaving the old structures. It is that, for a growing share of them, nothing has replaced those structures at all.

[SLOW]

And the eighty-three percent who still believe in something? I take that seriously. It tells me the hunger is still there. It tells me that this is not a nation of convinced materialists. But belief alone is not belonging. A spirituality you practice alone, on your own terms, without a community that holds you to anything, is a spirituality that makes no demands and offers no company. It is better than nothing. It is not enough.

Remember the lonely adults in the Harvard survey. Three out of four had little or no meaning in their lives. Two out of three did not feel part of any meaningful group. Those are not people who were liberated from bad institutions into rich lives of chosen purpose. Those are people who were left standing alone.

[PAUSE]

So here is my answer to the case for good riddance. Yes, many of the old cages deserved to be opened. But we did not only open the cages. We tore down the house the cages were in. And now a great many people are out in the weather, free and cold.

Freedom from is not the same as freedom for. A person freed from every obligation is not thereby given a reason to get up in the morning. The great liberators understood that. Frederick Douglass did not escape slavery to be alone. He escaped in order to belong fully to a free people, and to fight for those still in chains. The suffragists did not want freedom from public life. They wanted in.

The goal was never to be unbound. The goal was to be bound to the right things, by choice, with dignity.

[LET IT LAND]

There is a second objection, and it is a more technical one, so let me take it too.

A skeptic will say: you have shown me correlations. Married people are less likely to die by suicide. Religious countries report more meaning. Lonely people report less purpose. But correlation is not causation. Perhaps people who are already healthy and stable are the ones who manage to marry and stay married. Perhaps people who are already hopeful are the ones who keep going to church. Perhaps the arrows run backward, from well-being to belonging, and not the other way around.

That is a fair point, and serious researchers wrestle with it. In many of these studies, we cannot run the experiment. We cannot randomly assign some people to a congregation and others to an empty Sunday and see what happens over forty years.

[SLOW]

But notice three things.

First, the pattern holds across very different kinds of evidence. Durkheim's nineteenth-century records. A Harvard study that followed the same men for a lifetime and watched what predicted their health decades later. A world poll of a hundred and thirty-two countries. A survey of lonely Americans in twenty twenty-four. When so many different methods, in so many different eras, point the same way, the case grows stronger even if no single study is decisive.

Second, the Harvard researchers did not only take a snapshot. They looked at how warm a man's relationships were at fifty, and then waited thirty years to see how healthy he was at eighty. That is not proof, but it is far harder to explain away than a single survey taken on a single day.

And third, even the skeptic must admit that the arrows probably run both ways, which is exactly what the Harvard loneliness researchers concluded. If belonging makes us well and being well helps us belong, then a society that lets its bonds wither is not simply losing an ornament. It is tipping a spiral in the wrong direction, where loneliness breeds despair and despair breeds more loneliness.

[PAUSE]

So I do not claim that the science is finished. I claim that the weight of the evidence, joined to the testimony of nearly every human tradition we know, points one way. And when the stakes are this high, we would be foolish to wait for a perfect experiment before we act.

That is the work in front of us. Not to rebuild the old cages. To build better houses. And before we can do that, we have to look clearly at the shelters we have thrown up in the meantime, because they are not keeping out the rain.

## VIII. The First Substitute: Faction

Human beings abhor a vacuum of meaning. When the old supports fall, people do not simply sit in the emptiness. They reach for something to fill it. And in our time, the first thing many Americans have reached for is politics.

[PAUSE]

I am not talking about ordinary political participation. Voting, campaigning, arguing about policy, running for the school board. That is the lifeblood of a free country, and we need more of it, not less.

I am talking about politics as religion. Politics as the place where you find your identity, your community, your moral drama, your enemies and your salvation.

You know it when you see it. A person's whole sense of who they are rests on which side they are on. The news is consumed the way the faithful once read scripture, as daily confirmation of a cosmic struggle between good and evil. The other side is not mistaken, but wicked. Disagreement within the tribe is heresy. And the end of the story, the promised land, is total victory over the enemy, after which all will be well.

[SLOW]

It is easy to see why politics rushes into the gap. It offers almost everything the old supports offered. A story about where we came from and where we are going. A community of the like-minded, online and off. A moral code, with clear lines between the righteous and the damned. Rituals: the rally, the march, the hashtag, the yard sign. Saints and martyrs. Even a kind of apocalypse, in which the next election is always the last chance to save the world.

What it does not offer is the thing that matters most.

The church at its best told you that you, too, are a sinner, that the line between good and evil runs through your own heart, that you must forgive your enemy and love your neighbor even when he votes the wrong way. The household at its best bound you to people you did not choose and could not agree with, your cranky uncle, your mother-in-law, your teenage son, and forced you to love them anyway. The town at its best made you sit on the zoning board with someone from the other party, and fix the road together.

Politics as religion does the opposite. It tells you that evil lives on the other side. It sorts you into communities of agreement, where you never have to love someone you disagree with. It offers belonging only by defining someone else as the outsider.

[PAUSE]

George Washington saw this coming. In his Farewell Address in seventeen ninety-six, the old general, refusing a third term, warned his countrymen above all against what he called the spirit of party. This spirit, he said, is inseparable from our nature, having its root in the strongest passions of the human mind. He knew it could not be abolished. But in governments of the popular form, he said, it is seen at its rankest, and is truly their worst enemy. It kindles the animosity of one part of the community against another, and it opens the door to foreign influence and corruption.

And notice what Washington placed, in the same address, as the counterweight. Not a better party. The habits of a free people. Religion and morality, he wrote, are indispensable supports. And then this: virtue or morality is a necessary spring of popular government.

[SLOW]

Now I know that line about religion makes some people uneasy, and I understand why. Washington lived in a nation that was overwhelmingly Protestant, and he went on to say that national morality could not prevail without religious principle. Many Americans today, including many good and moral people, would disagree with him. I am not asking you to accept his theology.

But I am asking you to see his point. A free people cannot be held together by government alone. It must be held together by something deeper, by habits of the heart, by shared commitments that run beneath and before politics. When those commitments weaken, politics does not get calmer. It gets hotter. Because it is now being asked to carry the weight that faith and family and community used to carry. It is being asked to provide meaning. And politics was never built to provide meaning. It was built to settle disputes among people who already had it.

[PAUSE]

When politics becomes a religion, every election becomes a holy war. Every policy dispute becomes a question of salvation. Every opponent becomes an infidel. And the country that Washington warned us to keep united becomes two armed camps, each convinced that the other is the end of the republic.

I think that describes a good deal of where we are.

[LET IT LAND]

And here is the cruelest part. Politics as religion does not even cure the loneliness. You can spend six hours a day consumed by political outrage and never once sit across a table from another human being. You can belong to a movement of millions and not have a single friend who would drive you to the airport. It is a counterfeit of community. It feels like belonging. It gives you an enemy and a cause. But when the screen goes dark, you are still alone, and now you are angry too.

## IX. The Second Substitute: The Self as Project

If the first substitute for meaning is the tribe, the second is the self.

[PAUSE]

Walk into any bookstore, if you can still find one, and look at the shelves near the front. Self-help. Self-care. Self-improvement. Self-optimization. Open any app store and count the programs that promise to track your sleep, your steps, your heart rate, your macros, your mood, your meditation streak. Turn on any podcast and you will hear about cold plunges and supplements and morning routines and the ten habits of highly effective people.

There is a name for this. It is the wellness economy. And it is enormous. The Global Wellness Institute, which tracks it, estimated it at about six point eight trillion dollars worldwide in twenty twenty-four. Six point eight trillion. It has been growing about twice as fast as the global economy for a decade.

[SLOW]

Now let me be fair. A great deal of what goes under the name of wellness is good. Exercise is good. Sleep is good. Eating vegetables is good. Therapy, for people who need it, can be lifesaving, and I would never discourage anyone who is struggling from seeking professional help. Meditation and contemplative practice are ancient disciplines that the great religions have always known about. If someone in this room is getting through the week because of a therapist or a running club or a yoga class, I am glad, and I hope you keep going.

My argument is not with any of those things. My argument is with the promise that has been wrapped around them. The promise that the cure for the emptiness is more attention to yourself.

[PAUSE]

Think about the shape of the thing. The old supports pointed outward. The sacred pointed you toward God or something greater. The household pointed you toward your spouse and children and parents. The city pointed you toward your neighbors. The story pointed you toward your ancestors and descendants. Every one of them said, in effect: you are not the point. You are part of something.

The culture of the self-project points inward. It says: you are the point. Your growth, your healing, your boundaries, your authenticity, your journey. Every relationship is evaluated by whether it serves your well-being. A friend who drains you should be cut off. A family that does not affirm you is toxic. An obligation that does not spark joy should be discarded.

Some of that language came from real insight. There are people who are toxic, and boundaries can protect us from them. But taken as a whole philosophy of life, it has a strange result. It takes the very bonds that have always given human lives meaning, and recasts them as threats to be managed.

[SLOW]

Remember Frankl. He insisted that meaning cannot be pursued directly. He argued that happiness, too, cannot be pursued; it must ensue, as the side effect of devoting yourself to a cause greater than yourself or a person other than yourself. The more you aim at it, he said, the more you miss it.

That is the paradox the wellness economy cannot escape. It sells the pursuit of well-being as a product. And the more intently you pursue it, the more you turn your gaze onto yourself, the more you measure and track and optimize, the further you drift from the very thing that would make you well. You become the manager of a portfolio of one.

[PAUSE]

Here is a small picture of what I mean.

Imagine a young woman in a city apartment. She has a meditation app, a fitness tracker, a journal with gratitude prompts, a therapist she sees every other week on a video call, and a subscription to a box of supplements. She is doing everything right, by the standards of her culture. And she is lonely. She has not had a meal with another person in nine days. She does not know her neighbors' names. Her parents live two thousand miles away. She belongs to no congregation, no club, no team. Her work is remote. Every tool she owns is a tool for improving herself alone.

She does not need a better app. She needs someone to need her. She needs a place to be on Tuesday night where people will notice if she does not come.

[SLOW]

I am not mocking her. I am describing a great many of our children, and a great many of us. And the wellness economy will happily sell her another product, because her loneliness is, in a terrible sense, its business model. A person who is held by a community does not need nearly so many things.

There is an old idea, older than any of us, that a human being is not a self-contained project but a node in a web of relationships. That we are made by the people who love us and complete ourselves by loving others. Every wisdom tradition I know of teaches some version of it. Love your neighbor as yourself. The way of the bodhisattva who refuses liberation until all beings are free. The African idea expressed in the word ubuntu, that a person is a person through other people.

[LET IT LAND]

We will not find ourselves by looking inward forever. We find ourselves by being given away.

## X. The Third Substitute: The Glowing Rectangle

And that brings us to the third substitute, the one in your pocket right now.

[PAUSE]

I want to begin with a number. In twenty twenty-four, the Pew Research Center asked American teenagers how often they were online. Forty-six percent said almost constantly. Nearly half. A decade earlier, it was twenty-four percent.

Almost constantly. Not several times a day. Not every evening. Almost constantly.

Think about what that means for a fifteen-year-old. It means that during nearly every waking hour, some part of her mind is elsewhere, in a feed, in a group chat, in a game, in a stream of videos chosen by an algorithm designed by some of the most talented engineers on earth to keep her watching.

[SLOW]

Now, I am going to be careful here, because there is a real scientific debate about how much harm screens and social media do to young people. Some researchers believe the evidence of serious harm is strong. Others think it has been overstated, and that the correlations do not prove causation. Honest people disagree. I am not going to tell you the science is settled when it is not.

But I do not think we need to settle that debate to see the problem I am describing. Because my argument is not mainly that screens make people sick. It is that screens take time. And time is the one thing that every bond in a human life requires.

Remember the clock I read you earlier. In ten years, the average time Americans spent socializing fell from forty-three minutes a day to thirty-five. And the Harvard researchers found that technology was the factor Americans themselves blamed most for their loneliness. Seventy-three percent of us named it.

[PAUSE]

Every hour spent scrolling is an hour not spent at a dinner table, on a front porch, in a pew, at a meeting, on a ball field, in a long, slow conversation with a friend that wanders and has no purpose and is therefore exactly the kind of conversation in which people come to know one another. The screen does not need to be poison to do damage. It only needs to be more convenient than other people. And it is. It is always there. It never needs anything from you. It never has a bad day. It never asks you to drive across town.

That is the deepest danger of the screen. Not that it is evil, but that it is frictionless. And human bonds are made of friction. They are made of inconvenience, of showing up when you would rather stay home, of putting up with people who annoy you, of being needed at bad times. Take the friction away and you take the bond away with it.

[SLOW]

And now there is something new.

In twenty twenty-five, the nonprofit Common Sense Media surveyed more than a thousand American teenagers about artificial intelligence companions, chatbots that will talk with you, remember you, flatter you, and play any role you like. Seventy-two percent of teens said they had used one at least once. About half said they used them regularly. And about a third said they used them for social interaction: for conversation, for friendship, for emotional support, for role play.

To be fair, the same survey found that most of those teens still said they spent more time with real friends than with the bots. Most saw the tools mainly as tools. That is a reassuring sign, and I do not want to raise a panic over it.

But consider what we are building. For the first time in the history of the species, we can offer a lonely person a companion that will never be bored with them, never disagree with them unless they want it to, never need them to come to the hospital, never ask for anything at all. A companion with all of the comfort of a friendship and none of the cost.

[PAUSE]

And that, I want to suggest, is precisely the problem. The cost is the point. The cost is what makes a friendship a friendship. When you sit up all night with a friend whose marriage is falling apart, it is the sacrifice of your sleep that tells both of you what you mean to each other. When your mother changes your diapers and then, decades later, you change hers, the burden is the bond. A relationship that costs nothing means nothing, because nothing was given.

The glowing rectangle offers us connection without obligation, attention without presence, companionship without commitment. And those are not lesser versions of the real thing. They are, in a sense, its opposite.

[SLOW]

So there are the three substitutes. Faction offers us community without love. The self-project offers us growth without belonging. And the screen offers us connection without cost.

Each of them is reaching for something real. Each of them meets a genuine need, in part. That is why they are so powerful. A counterfeit only works if it looks like the real currency.

[LET IT LAND]

But every one of them leaves us more alone than we started. Every one of them turns us inward or turns us against each other. And every one of them, crucially, asks nothing of us that would make us need one another.

Which brings me to the question Alexander asked at Opis. Not just what we have built. What do we owe?

## XI. What Lasts

Before I tell you what I think we owe, I want to tell you about the longest study of human happiness ever conducted. Because it answers, as well as any science can, the question of what lasts.

[PAUSE]

In nineteen thirty-eight, researchers at Harvard began following a group of two hundred and sixty-eight young men, sophomores at the college. Some years later, a second group joined the study: four hundred and fifty-six boys from the poorest neighborhoods of Boston, youths who had grown up in tenements, many in families that were struggling to get by.

Two very different groups. Privileged and poor. And the researchers followed them for the rest of their lives. They interviewed them, examined them, and questioned them about their work, their marriages and their health, decade after decade. Some of the Harvard men went on to great fame. Some of the Boston boys went on to prosperity, and some to alcoholism and ruin. The study has outlived most of its original subjects. Today it is led by a psychiatrist named Robert Waldinger, and it is still going.

[SLOW]

What did more than eight decades of observation teach?

George Vaillant, who directed the study for many years, summarized one of its findings in a sentence that should be carved over the door of every business school in America. Financial success, he wrote, depends on warmth of relationships, not on intelligence. The men with the warmest relationships earned, at their peak, far more than those with the coldest.

But money was the least of it. The men who were most satisfied in their relationships at age fifty were the healthiest at eighty. Warm relationships in childhood predicted greater satisfaction with life in old age. Cold ones predicted anxiety and, in some cases, a higher risk of dementia.

Again and again, across both groups, across rich and poor, the single strongest thread running through a good life was not wealth, not fame, not achievement. It was love. It was the quality of a person's bonds with other people.

[PAUSE]

Now set that beside everything else I have told you. Durkheim's finding that despair rises when the bonds are weak. Frankl's finding that meaning is found by turning outward, toward a task and toward a person. The poll of a hundred and thirty-two countries, where meaning tracked not wealth but belonging to a living tradition. The Harvard loneliness survey, where three of four lonely people said their lives lacked purpose.

They all point the same direction. They are all telling us the same thing that our grandparents knew without needing a study to tell them.

[SLOW]

What lasts is not what we acquire. What lasts is what we are bound to.

Let me put it in Frankl's three terms.

What lasts is work that serves. Not just a job, but a task that someone else depends on. The nurse on the night shift. The carpenter whose house will stand for a hundred years. The mother who feeds her children. The volunteer who drives an old man to his dialysis appointment. Work becomes meaningful when it is a gift to somebody.

What lasts is love that commits. Not just a feeling, but a promise kept. The marriage that endures through sickness and poverty and the long middle years of boredom. The friendship that survives a move across the country. The son who visits his father in the memory care unit even after his father no longer knows his name. Love becomes meaningful when it is faithful.

And what lasts is suffering that is shared. Not suffering for its own sake. Nobody should seek pain. But the grief that is borne together at a funeral. The illness that is faced with a community that brings meals and sits up at night. The hardship of a family or a town that pulls through together, and is closer for it. Suffering becomes bearable, even meaningful, when we are not alone in it.

[PAUSE]

[SLOW]

And now look at what Americans themselves say gives their lives meaning. In twenty twenty-one, the Pew Research Center asked thousands of Americans, in their own words, what they found meaningful, and compared the answers with the same question asked in twenty seventeen.

Some of the answers were what you would expect. Material well-being, meaning money and security and living comfortably, was mentioned by eighteen percent. A job or career, by seventeen percent. But look at what happened to one answer in particular. In twenty seventeen, twenty percent of Americans mentioned a spouse or romantic partner as a source of meaning. In twenty twenty-one, it was nine percent.

That was the first pandemic winter, and some of that drop may have been temporary. I would not read too much into a single survey. But it is a striking picture all the same. In a time of fear and isolation, the share of Americans who named the person beside them as a source of meaning fell by more than half, while money and work held their places near the top.

[PAUSE]

I do not say that to scold anyone. Money matters. Security matters. Good work matters; Frankl put it first on his list. But a nation that finds its meaning mainly in its paycheck and its career is a nation whose meaning can be laid off. It can be automated. It can vanish in a recession. Meaning that rests on love and faith and belonging is not immune to suffering, but it does not disappear when the factory closes. It is precisely what carries people through when the factory closes.

Notice that every one of those has a cost. Every one of them asks something of us. Work that serves takes effort that we could have spent on ourselves. Love that commits closes off other options. Suffering that is shared means taking on someone else's burden.

That is what I meant earlier when I said the cost is the point. The substitutes we have reached for all promise meaning without cost. Belonging without love. Growth without commitment. Connection without presence. And they fail, not because they are evil, but because they are trying to sell us the one thing that cannot be bought. It can only be given, and received, and given again.

[SLOW]

Now I can return to Alexander's question. Because once you see that meaning comes from being bound, the question of what we owe stops being an abstraction.

At Opis, the soldiers had come to believe that they owed their king nothing, that they could simply walk away. And Alexander's answer was to remind them of everything they had received. The cloaks instead of sheepskins. The cities instead of mountain pastures. The laws. The victories. The debts he had paid for them.

And yet, notice what actually broke the mutiny. It was not the list of gifts. They heard the list and stood silent. What broke them was the moment he walked away, and they realized they would be alone. And what healed the breach was not a payment. It was a word. Kinsmen. I make you all my kin. And then a meal. A common table. One bowl.

[LET IT LAND]

That is what we owe each other. Not a payment. A bond. And a place at the table.

## XII. What We Owe

So let me be concrete. Because sermons about community are cheap, and I do not want to send you home with nothing but a warm feeling.

What do we owe?

[PAUSE]

We owe the past a debt of gratitude, and gratitude is not nostalgia. It means recognizing that the institutions we inherited, flawed as they were, were built by people who sacrificed for us. It means that before we tear down a fence, we ought to ask why it was put up. And when we find an institution that has failed, our first instinct should be the instinct of the reformers I talked about earlier. Not to burn it down. To fix it, to open it, to demand to be let in.

If you left a congregation because it wronged you, I will not tell you to go back. But I will ask: is there another one, one that has reckoned with its sins, where you might be welcome? And if you have never belonged to one, I will ask you to consider that the church or the synagogue or the mosque or the temple down the street may have something to offer you that no app can. You do not have to believe everything on the first Sunday. You can start by showing up, sitting in the back, and seeing who notices you are there.

And if faith is simply not for you, as it will not be for millions of Americans, then I ask you to look for what the faithful have, and build it in another form. A weekly gathering. A group that meets across the generations. Rituals that mark the passages of life. A moral community that holds you to something. Some of the most hopeful experiments of our time are secular people trying to build exactly that. They deserve our support.

[SLOW]

We owe our families a recovered seriousness about the promise. I am not talking about pressuring anyone into marriage, or shaming anyone who is single, or divorced, or who has chosen a different path. I am talking about making it possible again for ordinary working people who want to marry and raise children to do so.

That is partly a matter of economics. When a young man without a college degree cannot find steady work that pays enough to support a family, he will not feel ready to marry, and many women will not see him as ready either. So the debate we have about jobs, about wages, about training, about housing costs, about child care, is not only an economic debate. It is a debate about whether working Americans can build the households that have always been the first support of a human life. People of every party can and do disagree about the right policies. But we should agree on the goal.

It is also a matter of culture. We can choose to speak about marriage and parenthood not as a lifestyle option for the fortunate but as one of the great adventures of a human life, full of sacrifice and full of meaning. We can honor the people who keep their promises. We can stop treating commitment as a cage.

[PAUSE]

We owe our towns and cities a rebirth of association.

Tocqueville's America did not happen by accident. It happened because people formed clubs and societies and leagues. We can do that again. Not by waiting for government to build it for us, though government can help, but by doing what Americans have always done: finding a few neighbors with a common purpose and starting something.

Join something. That is my first practical request tonight. Join one thing that meets in person, at least once a month, where people will notice if you are absent. A volunteer fire company. A choir. A bowling league, in honor of Professor Putnam. A garden club. A veterans' post. A parent group at your child's school. A congregation. A union, if your workplace has one or could. A service club. A neighborhood association. It hardly matters which. What matters is that it is real, that it meets in the flesh, and that it asks something of you.

[SLOW]

And for those of us with any influence in public life, we owe our communities the third places that make association possible. The library that stays open in the evening. The park with benches where the old men play chess. The community center. The public pool. The parish hall that hosts the town meeting. The main street that has somewhere to sit. Every one of these is infrastructure, as surely as a bridge or a power line. It is the infrastructure of belonging. And for decades we have been letting it crumble.

We owe our young people something better than the screen.

Here, I think, there is real agreement emerging across the political spectrum. In recent years many states and school districts have moved to keep phones out of classrooms during the school day. There is a growing movement of parents who agree together to delay giving their children smartphones, so that no child is the only one without. These are not government mandates from above. They are communities deciding together what kind of childhood they want for their children. That is exactly the kind of association I am talking about.

But taking away the phone is only half of it. We owe young people something to do instead. Teams and clubs and jobs and camps and service projects and places to gather with no adult agenda at all. Real responsibility. A role in which someone depends on them. The young are not lazy and they are not broken. They are, in many cases, starving for a chance to matter. Give it to them.

[PAUSE]

We owe our country a shared work.

I believe, and this is my opinion, that one of the most powerful things this nation could do is offer every young American a year of national service. Not necessarily a mandate, but an invitation, well funded and widely honored. A year spent rebuilding trails, tutoring children, caring for the elderly, responding to disasters, serving in uniform, alongside young people from every region and race and class and creed. The old draft, for all its injustice, did one thing that nothing has replaced since. It threw young men of every background into a common task, and they came out knowing one another. A modern, voluntary, universal service year could do that for all of our young people. There are honest debates about the cost and the design. But the idea deserves a serious national hearing.

[SLOW]

And we owe our country a recovered story.

I do not mean a story that hides our sins. Our history has slavery in it and conquest in it and cruelty in it, and any story that leaves those out is a lie, and young people can smell a lie. But I also do not mean a story that is nothing but sins. A people cannot live on self-contempt any more than it can live on self-flattery.

The story worth telling is the one Lincoln and Douglass and King told. It is the story of a people who wrote down a creed better than they lived, and who have spent two and a half centuries struggling, failing, and struggling again to live up to it. It is a story that has a place for every one of us. The descendant of slaves and the descendant of the slaveholder. The child of the refugee and the child of the Mayflower. The believer and the doubter. It is a story about a shared work that is not yet finished. And a story about unfinished work is a story that gives every new generation something to do.

[PAUSE]

And finally, we owe each other our presence.

All the policies in the world will mean nothing if each of us, in our own lives, does not choose the harder, slower, more costly path of being present to other people.

Call your mother. Have the neighbors over, even if the house is messy. Go to the funeral. Go to the wedding. Visit the friend in the hospital, even if you do not know what to say. Put the phone in a drawer at dinner. Learn the names of the people on your street. Show up for the meeting. Stay after for coffee. Ask someone how they are, and wait for the real answer.

None of this is heroic. All of it is costly. And all of it, I promise you, is where the meaning is.

[LET IT LAND]

That is what we owe. To the past, gratitude. To our families, a promise kept. To our towns, association. To our children, a childhood. To our country, a shared work and a true story. And to one another, our presence.

## XIII. Counsels of an Old Friend

When George Washington laid down his office in seventeen ninety-six, he did not end with a call to arms. He ended with counsel. He called his words the counsels of an old and affectionate friend. And he said something remarkably humble for a man of his stature. He said he dared not hope that his advice would make the strong and lasting impression he wished, or that it would stop the nation from running the course that has marked the destiny of nations. He hoped only that it might do some partial good, now and then.

I want to close the argument of this evening in that spirit. Not as a prophet. Not as a partisan. As a friend who has thought about this for a long time and wants to tell you plainly what he has come to believe. Take what is useful. Leave the rest.

[PAUSE]

My first counsel is this. Be suspicious of anything that promises meaning without cost.

The market will sell it to you. The feed will scroll it to you. The movement will shout it at you. Each of them will offer you belonging and purpose and identity in exchange for your attention, your money, or your outrage. But real meaning has always been paid for in the currency of commitment. If it costs you nothing, if you can walk away from it at any moment without anyone being hurt, it is probably not the thing you are looking for.

My second counsel is this. Prefer the near to the far.

It is easy to love humanity. It is hard to love your neighbor, who plays his music too loud and voted for the other candidate. It is easy to care about a cause on the other side of the world. It is hard to visit your aunt in the nursing home on the other side of town. But meaning lives in the near. In the particular face, the particular street, the particular congregation. Begin there. You can change the world later. Change your block first.

[SLOW]

My third counsel is this. Do not let politics become your religion.

Hold your political convictions firmly. Argue for them. Vote for them. But do not let them become the place where you find your worth or your community or your salvation. Keep friends on the other side. Keep a part of your life, your faith, your family, your club, your team, where politics is not allowed in the door. Remember Washington's warning that the spirit of party is the worst enemy of popular government. Remember that the person across the aisle is not your enemy but your fellow citizen, and that you will need him the day the flood comes.

My fourth counsel is this. Make a promise and keep it.

In an age that prizes keeping your options open, the most countercultural thing you can do is to close some of them. Marry, if you can, and stay married, if you can. Join a congregation and stay through the boring years. Join a club and become the person who sets up the chairs. Promise a friend that you will be there and then be there. Every promise kept is a beam put back into the house.

[PAUSE]

My fifth counsel is this. Take the young seriously.

The generation now coming of age has been handed the thinnest set of supports of any generation in our history. They did not choose that. We built it for them, or failed to build what they needed. And they are telling us, in every survey we take, that they are struggling. Do not lecture them. Do not mock them. Invite them. Give them real work, real responsibility, real belonging. Mentor one. Coach one. Hire one. Make room for one at your table. They are not the problem. They are the people we owe the most.

My sixth counsel is this. Reform, do not abandon.

When an institution fails you, and many will, your first question should not be how to escape it but how to repair it. That is harder. It takes longer. It requires staying in rooms with people who frustrate you. But it is how every good institution we have was built in the first place, and how every great one was saved. The church that faced its abuse scandal and changed. The union that cleaned out its corruption. The town that integrated its pool. If every person of conscience walks away, the institutions are left to the worst. Stay and fight for them.

[SLOW]

My seventh counsel is this. Leave room for the sacred.

I will not tell you what to believe. That is between you and your conscience and, if you believe in God, between you and God. But I will counsel you not to close the door. Whatever you believe, find some time each week that is set apart. A sabbath from work and from screens. A walk in silence. A visit to a house of worship, even as a guest. A moment of gratitude spoken aloud at a meal. Human beings seem to need the sense that some things are not for sale, that some moments are holy. A life with no sacred time at all is a life in which everything is for sale, including you.

My eighth counsel is this. Count your inheritance.

When you feel the emptiness, and many of us do, do what Alexander made his soldiers do on that platform at Opis. Count what you have been given. The language you speak. The freedoms you enjoy. The roads and the hospitals and the libraries built by people you will never meet. The parents or grandparents who sacrificed so that you could be here. The faith or the philosophy or the traditions that someone carried across an ocean for you. You did not make yourself. No one does. And a person who knows how much he has received is a person who already knows something about what he owes.

[PAUSE]

And my last counsel is the simplest. Go to the table.

Whatever table you can find. The family dinner. The church potluck. The union picnic. The block party. The Thanksgiving meal where half the guests disagree about everything. The table is where human beings have always made peace, made kin, made meaning. It is the oldest sacrament of our species. Go to it, and bring someone with you.

[SLOW]

Those are my counsels. Like Washington, I do not flatter myself that they will change the course of a nation. The forces I have described tonight are enormous. Economics, technology, the slow tides of culture. No speech can turn them.

But I will say this, as Washington did. If these words do some partial good, now and then. If they send one person back to a congregation, or into a club, or across the street to meet a neighbor. If they persuade one parent to wait a little longer before handing a child a phone. If they move one person to make a promise and keep it. Then they will have been worth the saying.

[LET IT LAND]

Because the meaning crisis is not, in the end, something that will be solved in Washington or in Silicon Valley. It will be solved, if it is solved at all, at a million kitchen tables, in a million small acts of faithfulness, by a million ordinary people deciding to be bound to one another again.

## XIV. The Common Table

Let me take you back, one last time, to the banks of the Tigris.

[PAUSE]

Picture the soldiers on that third day. They have shouted their defiance. They have demanded to go home. They have heard their king tell them, with bitter scorn, to go and tell their families that they abandoned him. And then they have watched him walk away.

And in the silence, they realize something that they did not understand when they were shouting. They realize that they did not actually want to go home alone. They had been angry, and their anger had real causes. They had been neglected, and passed over, and made to feel that they no longer mattered. But what they wanted was not to be free of him. What they wanted was to be his. To be kin. To be counted.

So they went to his door and laid down their arms.

[SLOW]

My friends, I think that is where a great many Americans are tonight. We have shouted our defiance at the old institutions, and much of our anger had real causes. We were betrayed by some of them. We were excluded by some of them. We were bored by some of them. And so we demanded to be released, and in large part we were. We walked away from the pews and the lodges and the union halls. We postponed the marriages, or skipped them. We moved away from home. We retreated into our screens and our tribes and our self-improvement plans.

And now, in the silence, a great many of us are discovering that we did not really want to be alone. That freedom without belonging is a cold and narrow room. That the young are more miserable than their grandparents were, in a country far richer than the one their grandparents knew. That one in five of us is lonely, and three of every four of the lonely say their lives lack meaning.

[PAUSE]

So here is my answer to the taunt at Opis. Alexander told his soldiers: go home, and tell them you deserted your king. Tell them that, and see how it sounds.

I want to turn that around tonight.

Go home. Yes. Go home tonight. But do not go home and tell them that you deserted.

[BUILD]

Go home and tell them that you remembered what you were given.

Tell them that you remembered the churches built from immigrant pennies, and the lodges that buried the miners, and the unions that won the weekend, and the women's clubs that won the vote, and the Black congregations that kept faith alive under a law that denied their humanity.

Tell them you remembered the parents who kept their promises when keeping them was hard. The neighbors who brought the casserole. The coach who stayed late. The old woman who taught Sunday school for forty years and never missed a week.

Tell them that you counted your inheritance, and that it was larger than you knew.

[BUILD]

And then tell them what you are going to do about it.

Tell them you are going to join something. Tell them you are going to show up. Tell them you are going to make a promise and keep it. Tell them you are going to put the phone down at dinner, and learn your neighbors' names, and go to the funeral, and stay after for coffee.

Tell them you are going to repair the institutions that failed instead of walking away from them. Tell them you are going to build new ones where the old ones cannot be saved. Tell them you are going to give the young a reason to stay.

Tell them you are not leaving. Tell them you are staying, and you are bringing others with you.

[PAUSE]

And then, my friends, set the table.

Because that is how the story at Opis ends. Not with a speech. With a meal.

Remember the scene. The king comes out of his tent, sees his soldiers on their knees before his door, and weeps. An old officer tells him the truth about what hurt them. And the king says the word that heals it. Kinsmen. You are all my kin. And then, one by one, they come forward to embrace him.

And that night, nine thousand people sit down at a single table. Macedonians and Persians and men of every nation in that vast army. People who had been enemies a few years before. People who had been at each other's throats that very week. They all draw wine from the same great bowl, and pour the same offering, and they sing together. And the king prays for one thing above all. Concord. Partnership. That these divided peoples might share a common life.

[SLOW]

Alexander did not live to see that prayer answered. He died the next year, in Babylon, at thirty-two, and his empire tore itself apart. His prayer was made at the end of a conquest, and it could not be granted by a conqueror.

But the prayer itself was right. And it is ours to answer, in a way he never could. Not by conquest. Not by command. By choice.

[PAUSE]

We are a people of three hundred and forty million, of every faith and none, of every race and origin, divided by party and region and class and screen. We have more than nine thousand reasons to stay apart. And we have one great reason to come together. Because we cannot bear the weight of being human alone. We never could. No people ever has.

So let us set the table. In our homes and our congregations, our union halls and our libraries, our town squares and our ball fields. Let us pull up enough chairs for the neighbor we do not know and the cousin we do not agree with and the young person who has never been invited. Let us draw from the same bowl. Let us learn again the old words that bind: brother, sister, neighbor, friend. Kin.

[SLOW]

What have we built together? More than we know. More than any people in history.

What do we owe? Each other.

[LET IT LAND]

Go home tonight. And when you get there, do not tell them you deserted.

Tell them you came back to the table.

Thank you, and good night.
