# The Career and the Cradle

## I. Friends and Fellow Citizens

Friends and fellow citizens.

I want to begin with a picture, and I want to be honest that it is a composite. She is not one woman. She is thousands of women, and if you are of a certain age, you have met her, or you have been her, or you have loved her.

She is thirty-four years old. On a Tuesday morning she is told that she has made it: the promotion she has worked for since she was twenty-two, the title on the door, the seat at the table where decisions are made. On Thursday afternoon of the same week she is sitting in a quiet office with soft lighting and a box of tissues on the side table, and a doctor is explaining, kindly and carefully, what the numbers on her blood test mean.

[PAUSE]

Nobody lied to her. Nobody forced her. Every choice she made along the way was reasonable. Every choice was hers. She went to school because school was the door. She took the job because the job was the ladder. She worked the late nights because the late nights were the price of the ladder. She waited to have a child because there never seemed to be a year when a child would not cost her the thing she had just earned.

And now the two clocks in her life, the one on the office wall and the one inside her own body, have finally met, and she is discovering that they were never set to the same time.

[LET IT LAND]

I have come here tonight to talk about her. And about him, the man beside her, who also waited, and who also worked the late nights, and who has never once been asked at a job interview whether he plans to start a family. And about the couple down the street who have one child and wanted three. And about the young people in their twenties who look at the price of a house, the price of daycare, and the hours their parents worked, and quietly conclude that a family is a luxury good, like a boat or a second home, something other people have.

I want to talk about the career and the cradle. About how our country, in the space of two generations, rebuilt its economy, its housing, its schedules and its expectations around two full careers in every household, and in the rebuilding, forgot to leave a room for the children.

[PAUSE]

Let me say at the outset what I am not here to say, because in this debate, the things you are not saying matter as much as the things you are.

I am not here to tell any woman that she should not have gone to work. I am not here to tell anyone that her education was a mistake, that her ambition was selfish, that her place is anywhere other than the place she chooses. The entry of women into every profession in this land is one of the great achievements of the last hundred years, and I will defend it tonight, and I will defend it tomorrow.

I am not here to tell anyone that they must have children. Some of the finest people I know have chosen not to, and they owe no one an explanation. A free country does not draft its citizens into parenthood.

And I am not here to sell you nostalgia. There was no golden age. The world of nineteen fifty-seven, when American women had more children than at any time in living memory, was also a world in which a woman often could not get a credit card in her own name, in which whole professions were closed to her, in which a married teacher could be dismissed for the offense of being married. We are not going back there. We should not want to.

[BUILD]

What I am here to say is this. We built a society that says to young women, and to young men, you may have a career, and you may have a family, but we will arrange things so that it is very hard to have both, and we will arrange the timing so that the years you most need for one are exactly the years you most need for the other. And then we act surprised when the cradles stay empty.

That is not a moral failure of women. It is a design failure of a nation.

And design failures can be fixed.

[PAUSE]

I come to you in the spirit of an old form of American speech. When George Washington laid down the presidency, he did not issue commands. He addressed the people as friends and citizens, and he offered what he called the counsels of an old and affectionate friend. He told the country what he feared, and what he hoped, and he trusted them to weigh it.

That is what I intend to do tonight. To counsel, not command.

But I also come in the spirit of another American voice, one that did not have the luxury of gentleness. Frederick Douglass stood before audiences who thought of themselves as good people and told them, without flattery, that their practice did not match their profession. He held up the nation's own words and asked it to live by them.

So let me borrow a little of that directness as well. This country says it loves children. It says it values families. It says that motherhood is sacred and fatherhood is noble. Every politician of every party says so, every election year, standing in front of a backdrop of smiling babies.

And yet.

We are the only member of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, the club of the world's advanced economies, without a national law requiring paid maternity leave. We charge young families more for daycare than they pay in rent. We build careers that demand the most of a person in exactly the decade when a family would demand the most of them. We penalize mothers in their paychecks and reward fathers for the same child. And we price the homes in the neighborhoods with good schools as though every household has two full salaries, because, by now, the market has learned that it must.

[SLOW]

You cannot say you love children and then build a country with no room for them. You can say it. You cannot mean it.

So for the next two hours I want to walk through this with you carefully. What the numbers say. What the history says. What the best economists of our time have found. What the strongest objections are, and how I answer them. And then, what we owe to each other, and to the children who are not yet born, and to the young people who want them and do not see a way.

Let us begin with the numbers.

## II. The Numbers We Have Stopped Noticing

In nineteen fifty-seven, at the height of the baby boom, the total fertility rate in the United States stood at about three point seven seven. That number is an estimate of how many children the average woman would have over her lifetime if the birth rates of that year held steady. Nearly four children per woman.

By two thousand seven, on the eve of the Great Recession, it had fallen to about two point one two. That is almost exactly what demographers call replacement: the level at which, over the long run, each generation is about the same size as the one before it.

And then it kept falling.

[PAUSE]

By twenty twenty, about one point six four. By twenty twenty-three, about one point six two. In twenty twenty-four, according to the final federal data, one point five nine nine: the lowest total fertility rate ever recorded in this country. And provisional figures for twenty twenty-five put it a little lower still, at about one point five eight.

In April of this year, the National Center for Health Statistics reported that there were about three million, six hundred and six thousand births in the United States in twenty twenty-five. That was down one percent from the year before. The general fertility rate, the number of births per thousand women between fifteen and forty-four, fell to fifty-three point one. The center noted that this rate has fallen by twenty-three percent since two thousand seven.

[LET IT LAND]

Twenty-three percent in less than twenty years. Not in a war. Not in a famine. Not in a plague. In a period of peace and, for most of it, growing prosperity.

Now, numbers like these are easy to hear and easy to forget. One point five nine nine sounds like a decimal on a spreadsheet. So let me translate. A country at replacement replaces itself. A country at one point six, held over the long run without immigration, shrinks each generation by about a quarter. Every hundred parents have about eighty children. Those eighty have about sixty-four. The arithmetic is patient, and it is merciless.

But I did not come here to frighten you with arithmetic. Demography is not destiny, and there are people far better qualified than I am to argue about pensions and labor shortages and the long-run size of the economy. What I want you to notice is something more human.

[PAUSE]

Look at when women are having their first child. In nineteen seventy, the average age of an American mother at her first birth was about twenty-one and a half. By twenty sixteen it was twenty-six point six. By twenty twenty-three, according to the federal government's own report, it was twenty-seven and a half, and a university analysis of the twenty twenty-four data puts it at about twenty-seven point six, the highest in more than half a century of records.

In our big cities, the average first-time mother is now around twenty-eight and a half. In rural counties, closer to twenty-five. The later the first child comes, the fewer children tend to follow, and that gap between city and country tells you something about where the pressures of career and cost bite hardest.

[BUILD]

Now here is the number that matters most, and it is the one we have stopped noticing.

In twenty twenty-three, Gallup asked Americans what they thought the ideal number of children was for a family. The average answer was two point seven. Forty-five percent said three or more. That was the highest share preferring larger families since nineteen seventy-one.

Two point seven is what Americans say they want. One point six is what we are having.

[LET IT LAND]

That gap is the whole story of this speech. If Americans had simply stopped wanting children, I would have no business standing here. A free people may want what it wants. But that is not what the evidence says. The evidence says that a great many Americans want more children than they are having, and that something stands between the wanting and the having.

I want to be fair. Not everyone wants a large family, and some of the falling numbers reflect real changes in what people want. I will come back to that, because it is the strongest objection to what I am arguing, and I will not dodge it. In twenty twenty-four, the Pew Research Center found that forty-seven percent of adults under fifty without children said they were unlikely ever to have them, up from thirty-seven percent just six years earlier. And the most common reason they gave was simply that they did not want to.

But listen to the other reasons on that list. Forty-four percent said they wanted to focus on other things, such as their careers. Thirty-six percent said they could not afford to raise a child. Twenty-four percent said they had not found the right partner.

Focus on career. Cannot afford it. No partner yet. Those are not reasons that come from nowhere. They come from the shape of the life we have built for young adults.

[PAUSE]

There is a phrase that demographers use: the fertility gap. The space between the children people say they want and the children they actually have. And I want to suggest to you tonight that this gap is not a statistic. It is a sorrow. It is the sum of millions of private disappointments, each of them invisible, each of them carried quietly. The child who was always going to come next year. The second child who was never possible because the first one cost so much. The family that was deferred until it was declined, and declined not by a decision, but by a calendar.

A nation that has a gap like that between its hopes and its life ought to ask why.

So let us ask.

## III. A Revolution Worth Defending

To understand where we are, we have to understand how we got here, and that means telling one of the great stories of American history. It is the story of how women entered the paid economy, not as a temporary wartime measure, not as a stopgap, but as a permanent and equal part of the nation's working life.

No one has told that story more carefully than Claudia Goldin, the Harvard economist who in twenty twenty-three received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for having advanced our understanding of women's labor market outcomes. She was the first woman to receive that prize alone. And her work is, in a sense, the scholarly backbone of everything I want to say tonight.

[PAUSE]

Goldin spent decades in the archives. She did not take the conventional story for granted. And one of the first things she found was surprising. Women's work did not simply rise, steadily, from the beginning of industrial history to now. It followed something more like a U.

In farming societies and family workshops, women worked constantly, alongside their husbands, in the same house and the same fields. Then, as work moved into factories and offices, as the workplace separated from the home, married women's paid work fell. Social norms, and laws, and employers pushed them out. Only later, as education spread and the economy shifted toward clerical and professional work, did women's participation climb back up the other side of the U.

Think about what that means. For a long stretch of our history, the separation of the workplace from the home made it harder, not easier, for women to work. The factory clock and the office clock did not care that someone had to be home with the baby. And so the person who stayed home, overwhelmingly, was the mother.

[SLOW]

That is the root of everything. The modern workplace was designed, from the beginning, on the assumption that the worker had someone at home.

For much of the twentieth century, that assumption was written into rules. There were what historians call marriage bars: policies under which women, especially teachers and clerical workers, could be refused employment or dismissed when they married. A woman's career, for many, ended at the altar. The country did not ask her to choose between work and family. It chose for her.

Then came the changes. In nineteen sixty, the oral contraceptive pill was approved. Goldin and her colleague Lawrence Katz later showed, in a famous paper called The Power of the Pill, that access to reliable contraception allowed young women to delay marriage and childbirth and to invest in long professional training: law school, medical school, doctoral programs. For the first time, a young woman could plan a career with some confidence that her life would not be upended before her training was finished.

Goldin called what followed a quiet revolution. Beginning in the late nineteen sixties and seventies, young women stopped expecting that their work would be a job they took until marriage, and began expecting that it would be a career that shaped their identity and lasted their lifetime. They went to college in greater numbers than men. They entered law, medicine, business and science. They changed the country.

[BUILD]

And look at where that revolution has brought us. In twenty twenty-four, the share of women between the ages of twenty-five and fifty-four who were working or looking for work reached about seventy-eight percent, a record high.

Seventy-eight percent. Our hospitals, our courts, our classrooms, our laboratories, our businesses, our armed forces, our legislatures: all of them are stronger, smarter and more just because women are in them.

So let me say this with all the force I have. This revolution was right. It was right because it was just: no free society should close its professions to half its citizens. It was right because it was wise: a country that wastes half its talent is a poorer country. And it was right because the women who made it wanted it, earned it and deserved it.

[PAUSE]

If anyone in this room has come tonight hoping to hear that the answer to falling birth rates is to send women home, you have come to the wrong speech. I will not give you that. The answer is not less freedom for women. The answer is a country that finally finishes the job the quiet revolution began.

Because here is the thing about revolutions. They change some things fast and other things slowly. The quiet revolution changed what women expected of their own lives. It changed the law. It changed the universities. It changed the professions.

But it did not change the clock on the office wall. It did not change the assumption, baked into the structure of work since the first factory whistle, that a serious worker has someone at home to handle everything else. It did not change the biology of human reproduction. And it did not change, nearly enough, the expectations placed on men.

[LET IT LAND]

Women entered a world of work that had been built by and for men who had wives at home. They were told: you are welcome here, on the same terms. And the terms were these. Give us your twenties. Give us your early thirties. Give us your evenings and your weekends and your willingness to be on call. Prove yourself in exactly the years when, if you want children, your body is telling you the time is now.

Same terms. As if the terms were neutral. As if a set of rules written for people who never had to carry a pregnancy, nurse an infant or leave work for a pediatrician could be applied, without change, to people who do.

That is the unfinished business of the quiet revolution. And it is the business I want to talk about for the rest of tonight.

## IV. The Shape of a Life

Claudia Goldin tells the story of the twentieth century through generations of college-educated American women. It is worth walking through, because each generation faced a different version of the same question: career or family, and if both, in what order?

For the women who graduated from college in the early years of the last century, the answer was often stark. It was one or the other. A large share of the earliest college women never married or never had children. Those who built careers often did so by giving up a family. Those who had families often gave up the career. It was a fork in the road, and you could take only one path.

For the generation that came after, particularly in the years after the Second World War, the answer shifted. Now it was family first, and maybe a job later. Women married young and had children young, and some returned to work once the children were in school. The baby boom was, in part, the product of that sequence.

[PAUSE]

Then came the generations shaped by the quiet revolution and the pill. For them, the order flipped. Career first, family later. Get the degree. Get established. Make partner, make tenure, make a name. And then, in your thirties, have the children.

And for the most recent generations, the aspiration changed again. Not one, then the other. Both. At the same time. A career and a family, held together, in the same years of life.

That is a beautiful aspiration. It is also, given the way we have organized work, an extraordinarily hard one to achieve.

[SLOW]

Think about the shape of a professional life in America today. Ask a young lawyer, a young doctor, a young engineer, a young academic, a young executive when the hardest, most consequential years of their career are. They will tell you: the years from the mid-twenties to the late thirties. That is when you are an associate trying to make partner. That is when you are a resident working shifts that would be illegal in many other industries. That is when you are an assistant professor racing a tenure clock. That is when you are proving, to people who are watching closely, that you are the kind of person who will give the firm everything.

Now ask a doctor of reproductive medicine when the years of greatest fertility are. She will tell you: from the late teens to the early thirties, with a gradual decline in the early thirties and a steeper one after the mid-thirties.

[LET IT LAND]

Lay those two timelines on top of each other. They are nearly the same years. The decade we ask young adults to devote to proving themselves is the decade that biology offers most generously for starting a family.

That collision is not an accident of individual choices. It is a structural fact. And it falls hardest on women, for the simple reason that it is women who carry pregnancies, women who give birth, women who nurse, and still, in most households, women who take on the larger share of the work of raising small children.

[PAUSE]

Now, some will say: so what? People can have children in their late thirties. Medicine has advanced. Many women do. And that is true. I will come to the biology in a moment, and I want to be careful and honest about it. But even setting biology aside, consider what the late start does to the size of a family.

If you have your first child at twenty-six, you have time and room for a second and a third if you want them. If you have your first child at thirty-six, after a decade of building a career, the arithmetic changes. Each additional child must come quickly. The career is at its most demanding. Your energy is not what it was. Your parents, who might have helped, are older too and may need help themselves. The second child is harder. The third becomes, for many, unthinkable.

And so a society that pushes the first birth later is also, quietly, a society that removes the second and the third.

[BUILD]

I want you to notice how reasonable every step is. No one in this story is doing anything wrong.

The young woman who goes to graduate school is doing exactly what we told her to do. The firm that asks its associates to work long hours is doing what its clients reward. The couple who wait until they are financially secure are being responsible. The landlord who charges what the market will bear is following the market. The school district that funds itself through property taxes is following the law.

Every individual step is rational. And the sum of all those rational steps is a country where people have fewer children than they want, later than they planned, with more anxiety than they deserve.

That is what a design failure looks like. Nobody chose it. Everybody built it.

[PAUSE]

Washington, in his farewell, warned the young republic about forces that could pull it apart without anyone quite intending it, above all the spirit of faction, which works quietly and by degrees. I think of the spirit of that warning when I think of this. We did not hold a national debate and decide, as a people, that childbearing should be pushed to the edge of biological possibility. We drifted there. One reasonable decision at a time.

And drift can be corrected. But only if we first admit that we have drifted.

[SLOW]

There is one more thing to say about the shape of a life, and it concerns marriage and partnership. Children, for most people, come within a partnership. And partnerships, too, have been pushed later. Young adults spend longer in school, longer establishing themselves, longer feeling that they are not yet ready. Remember that Pew survey: nearly a quarter of young adults who said they were unlikely to have children said one reason was that they had not found the right partner.

There are many causes for that, and I will not pretend to understand them all. But one of them is surely this: when both partners in a couple are expected to build full careers before they can afford a family, the whole timeline of adult life is stretched. The partnership waits for the careers. The children wait for the partnership. And the clock does not wait for anyone.

## V. Two Clocks

I have promised to be honest about biology, so let me be honest, and let me be careful. This is a subject on which people are easily hurt, and on which there is a great deal of fear-mongering in both directions. Some voices exaggerate the decline in fertility with age in order to scare young women. Other voices minimize it in order to reassure them. Neither does women any favors.

Here is what the evidence actually says, as best I understand it.

[PAUSE]

A French demographer named Henri Leridon, in a widely cited study published in two thousand four, estimated the chance that a woman who starts trying to conceive, without fertility treatment, will have a conception leading to a live birth within a year. At age thirty, his estimate was about seventy-five percent. At thirty-five, about sixty-six percent. At forty, about forty-four percent.

Read those numbers twice. They are not a cliff at thirty-five, despite what you may have heard. Most women who start trying in their mid-thirties will succeed. But they are a slope, and the slope gets steeper. A woman who starts at forty has, by that estimate, a little better than even odds of failing in the first year.

And researchers who estimate the share of women who will never have a live birth if they begin trying at a given age find something similar: low numbers at thirty, higher at thirty-five, and much higher at forty and beyond, though the estimates at older ages vary widely and the experts disagree among themselves.

[SLOW]

Medicine helps. In vitro fertilization helps. Egg freezing helps. I do not want anyone to leave here thinking those technologies are useless; they have given children to many families who would otherwise have had none. But they are not a guarantee, they are expensive, they are physically and emotionally demanding, and they do not erase the risks that come with pregnancy at an older age. Freezing an egg can preserve the egg. It does not freeze the rest of the body.

There is a cruel irony here. Some employers now offer to pay for egg freezing as a benefit. I do not condemn them for it; it may be a genuine kindness to an individual employee. But think about what it implies as a social policy. It says to a young woman: we would rather pay to put your fertility on ice than reorganize the job so that you could have a child when your body is ready. It treats the biology as the problem to be managed, and the job as the fixed point around which life must bend.

[LET IT LAND]

I want to propose the opposite. The job is the thing that humans invented. The biology is the thing we did not. It makes more sense to bend the invention than to bend the body.

[PAUSE]

Now, let me be fair to men, because this is not only a story about women's bodies. Men's fertility also declines with age, more gradually, and older fathers face some increased risks as well. And men, too, are delaying partnership and parenthood. The two clocks are a shared problem within a couple, even if the hands move faster on one of them.

But the burden is not shared equally, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. The woman's clock is the binding constraint. And that is precisely why a workplace that demands the most in the years from twenty-five to forty asks far more of women than of men, even when the rules on paper are identical.

[BUILD]

Here is what I find most troubling. Most young people, in my experience, do not know these numbers with any precision. They hear vague warnings, or vague reassurances, and they make the most important decision of their lives on the basis of a feeling that there will be time.

And who can blame them? Our institutions give them every incentive to believe there will be time. Their schools tell them to focus on their studies. Their employers tell them to focus on their careers. The culture tells them that thirty is the new twenty. And nobody in authority sits them down, respectfully, and says: here is the actual evidence, here are the trade-offs, the choice is yours, but you deserve to make it with your eyes open.

Information is not coercion. Telling a young woman the truth about her own body is not telling her what to do with it. It is treating her as an adult. We teach our young people about compound interest and retirement accounts so that they can plan their financial lives. We should be able to give them honest, unfrightening, evidence-based information about fertility so that they can plan their family lives, if they want families.

[PAUSE]

But information alone will not solve this, and I want to be very clear about that. If you tell a twenty-seven-year-old associate that her odds are better now than they will be at thirty-seven, she may well say: I know. But if I have a baby now, I will not make partner. My salary will stall. My student loans will not wait. We cannot afford childcare. And my husband's firm does not give fathers real leave.

She is not ignorant. She is trapped. The problem is not that she does not know the time on the biological clock. The problem is that we have set the career clock to strike at the same hour.

So let us look, in turn, at the mechanisms that make the cradle so expensive for the career. The first is the simplest. It is time.

## VI. The Price of a Child Is Paid in Time

Economists have thought about the cost of children for a long time. The Nobel laureate Gary Becker, in the nineteen sixties, offered a framework that still shapes how economists think. Children, in his model, are something parents invest in. As societies grow richer and as education becomes more valuable, parents tend to invest more in each child, and to have fewer of them.

But there is another part of the story that economists came to see more clearly over time, and it is the part that matters most for us tonight. The largest cost of a child is not the diapers or the crib or even the college fund. The largest cost is time. And, in most families, historically, it has been the mother's time.

[PAUSE]

Here is how the logic works. When a woman's wage is low, and her opportunities outside the home are few, the time she spends raising children costs the household relatively little in lost income. When her education is high, and her opportunities are many, every hour she spends at home rather than at work costs the household more. Economists call this the opportunity cost. Not what you pay. What you give up.

And so, as women's education and earnings rose over the twentieth century, the opportunity cost of children rose with them. For a woman who could earn a professional salary, a year at home with a newborn did not cost a few hundred dollars. It cost tens of thousands of dollars, plus the promotion she did not get, plus the clients who went to someone else, plus the skills that grew stale.

This is not a story about women valuing money over children. It is a story about a society that made women pay for children with the most valuable thing they had, and then asked why they bought fewer.

[SLOW]

Now, there is a way to reduce that cost, and many families have found it. You can pay someone else to provide some of the care. A daycare center. A nanny. A preschool. A grandparent, if you are lucky enough to have one nearby and healthy. In economic terms, you convert a time cost into a money cost.

The economists Matthias Doepke, Anne Hannusch, Fabian Kindermann and Michèle Tertilt, in a major survey of the new economics of fertility, make exactly this point. When childcare is provided by someone other than the mother, whether a hired caregiver, a public kindergarten or the child's father, the cost of children is no longer tied so directly to the mother's lost earnings.

That is an important insight, and it is a hopeful one. It means the trade-off between career and children is not a law of nature. It depends on who provides the care, and what it costs.

[BUILD]

So what does it cost?

According to Child Care Aware of America, the national average price of child care in twenty twenty-four was about thirteen thousand, one hundred and twenty-eight dollars a year. For a single child. For a married couple with children at the median income, that is about ten percent of their household income. For a single parent, it is about thirty-five percent.

Thirty-five percent. More than a third of a single parent's income, for one child's care, so that she can go to the job that pays for everything else.

And for families with two young children in a care center, the annual price exceeded the median annual rent in forty-nine states and the District of Columbia. In most states, it exceeded the average annual mortgage payment as well.

[LET IT LAND]

Let that sink in. For a young family with two little ones, the cost of someone watching the children while the parents work is more than the cost of the roof over their heads.

And this is the point where the trap closes. A couple has a first child. They do the math on a second. Two children in care costs more than the rent. One parent, usually the one who earns less, which given the pay gap is usually the mother, looks at her take-home pay after childcare and realizes that she would be working, in effect, for nearly nothing. Perhaps for less than nothing, once you count commuting and the cost of work clothes and the sheer exhaustion.

So she has a choice. She can step back from her career, and pay the opportunity cost in time, in lost earnings, in a résumé gap that employers will hold against her for years. Or she can keep working and pay the money cost, which consumes so much of the household budget that a second child becomes unaffordable.

Either way, she pays. And the second child, so often, is the casualty.

[PAUSE]

I want to underline something here, because it is the moral heart of this section. The care of young children is work. It is real work, essential work, skilled work. It is the work on which every other kind of work depends: no economy anywhere has ever run without people who were once raised by someone.

And our society has decided to treat that work in one of two ways. Either it is unpaid, done by a parent who loses income and standing for doing it. Or it is paid, but at prices families can barely afford, to childcare workers who are themselves often paid so little that many struggle to support families of their own.

That is not a market functioning well. That is a society that has refused to decide who will pay for the next generation, and so has left the bill on the kitchen table of every young couple in America.

## VII. The Motherhood Penalty

There is a moment in the economic life of a woman that economists can now see with startling clarity. It is the moment her first child is born.

For a long time, the debate over the gap between men's and women's pay was a debate about discrimination in the simple sense: employers paying women less for the same work because they were women. That kind of discrimination has not vanished, and it matters. But in recent decades, careful researchers have found that a large and growing share of the gap is explained by something else. It is explained by children.

[PAUSE]

The economists Henrik Kleven, Camille Landais and Jakob Egholt Søgaard used the extraordinary records kept by the government of Denmark to follow men and women through their working lives, year by year, before and after the birth of their first child. What they found is one of the most striking pictures in modern economics.

Before the first child, the earnings paths of men and women look remarkably similar. They rise together. Then the first child is born. The father's earnings keep rising, almost as if nothing had happened. The mother's earnings drop sharply, and, crucially, they never fully recover. Even ten years later, the gap is still there.

They called it the child penalty. In Denmark, which has some of the most generous family policies in the world, the long-run penalty amounted to about twenty percent of a mother's earnings relative to a father's. It came about through fewer hours, through lower participation in the workforce and through lower wage rates, roughly in equal measure.

[SLOW]

And here is the finding that should stop us in our tracks. In Denmark, the share of the overall gender gap in earnings that is explained by this child penalty rose from about forty percent in nineteen eighty to about eighty percent by twenty thirteen.

Think about what that means. Over a generation, as other forms of inequality between men and women narrowed, the inequality that remained came to be, overwhelmingly, about who carries the cost of children. The pay gap has become, more and more, a parenthood gap. A motherhood gap.

The same researchers and their colleagues later compared countries. They found child penalties across the board, and the penalty in the United States and Britain was reported to be larger than in Scandinavia: around thirty-one percent in the United States, and around forty-four percent in the United Kingdom.

[LET IT LAND]

Now compare that to fathers. The sociologist Michelle Budig, studying American data, found that after accounting for other factors, mothers earned about four percent less for each child they had. Fathers, on the other hand, earned more: over six percent more than comparable men without children.

The same child. The same event. A penalty for one parent and a bonus for the other.

I will put it plainly, in the manner of Douglass, who never let a comfortable audience hide behind abstractions. We say we honor mothers. We put it on greeting cards. We buy flowers once a year. And then, for the rest of the year, we pay them less for having done the very thing we claim to honor. That is not honor. That is a fine.

[PAUSE]

Why does this happen? Part of it is choice, and I want to respect that. Many mothers want to spend more time with their young children, and some choose jobs with more flexibility and lower pay. That is their right, and nothing I say tonight is meant to judge it.

But part of it is not choice at all. In a well-known experiment published in two thousand seven, the sociologists Shelley Correll, Stephen Benard and In Paik asked people to evaluate job applicants whose résumés were identical except for a subtle signal of parenthood. The childless women were recommended for hire about eighty-four percent of the time. The mothers, only about forty-seven percent. The mothers were offered starting salaries that averaged about eleven thousand dollars less. Fathers, by contrast, were not penalized; if anything, they were seen favorably.

The same qualifications. The same experience. Judged differently because of a child.

[BUILD]

And there is one more finding from the Danish study that I want to dwell on, because it speaks to the future. The researchers found that the size of a woman's child penalty was related to the family she grew up in. Daughters whose own mothers had worked more, relative to their fathers, tended to experience smaller penalties themselves. The penalty, in other words, was passed down from mothers to daughters, but not to sons.

That tells us something hopeful and something sobering. Sobering, because it means the patterns we set now will echo for generations. Hopeful, because it means those patterns are not fixed. They are learned. And what is learned can be changed.

[PAUSE]

Now let me connect this back to the cradle. A young woman watching all of this is not a fool. She sees her older colleagues come back from maternity leave to find their best accounts reassigned. She sees the woman who asked for a flexible schedule quietly moved off the partner track. She sees the gap open, and she sees that it never closes.

And so she does what any rational person would do. She delays. She thinks: let me get further up the ladder first, so the fall will not be so far. Let me build enough savings, enough seniority, enough reputation, that a child will not cost me everything. And when she finally feels secure enough, she is thirty-six, or thirty-eight, and the second child is a long shot and the third is out of the question.

[SLOW]

The motherhood penalty does not only punish the women who become mothers. It deters the women who might. It reaches backward in time and shapes decisions years before a child is ever conceived. Every penalty we impose on mothers is also a tax on future children: a tax collected in births that never happen.

If we want to understand the empty cradles, we have to understand that we have built an economy that charges a woman one fifth, or one third, of her lifetime earnings for the privilege of becoming a mother. And then we wonder why the cradle waits.

## VIII. Greedy Work

Why is the penalty so large, and why does it fall so heavily on mothers rather than being shared by both parents? Claudia Goldin's answer is one of the most illuminating ideas in all of modern economics, and it has a memorable name. She calls it greedy work.

[PAUSE]

Here is what she means. In many of the best-paid occupations in our economy, pay does not rise in proportion to hours. It rises faster than hours. The lawyer who is available at any hour, who can fly to a client on a day's notice, who answers the email at eleven o'clock at night, does not earn just a little more than the lawyer who leaves at five-thirty to pick up the children. She earns much more. Sometimes the person who works fifty percent more hours earns not fifty percent more, but double.

Economists call this a nonlinear relationship between hours and pay. Goldin calls it greedy, because the job is greedy for your time. It wants all of it, and it pays a premium to get it.

[SLOW]

Now think about what greedy work does to a couple. Two people, equally educated, equally talented, equally ambitious. They have a child. Someone needs to be able to leave work when the daycare calls with a fever. Someone needs to be home for the pediatrician, the school play, the snow day. Both of them cannot be on call for the firm at the same time as being on call for the child.

So the couple faces a choice. If both of them take the predictable, flexible, lower-paid jobs, they leave a great deal of money on the table, because greedy work pays so much more. The rational thing, financially, is for one of them to take the greedy job and one of them to take the flexible job. One to be on call for the office. One to be on call for the home.

And which one, so often, takes the flexible job? Given the existing gap in pay, given social expectations, given that she may already have taken time off for the birth: the mother.

[LET IT LAND]

That is how couple equity breaks. Not through a single act of discrimination. Not through anyone's malice. Through the structure of pay itself, which rewards a couple for specializing, for splitting into one parent who belongs to work and one parent who belongs to home.

Goldin's phrase for what is lost is couple equity: the ideal of two partners who share both career and care equally. She argues that this ideal remains out of reach, not mainly because of individual prejudice, but because of how work itself is organized.

[PAUSE]

And here is where greedy work connects to the cradle. Notice what greedy work does to the incentive to have a child at all. If you are a young couple both in greedy jobs, a child forces you to break the symmetry. One of you must step back. The child is not just an expense; it is the event that ends one partner's run at the top. So you put it off. And you put it off again.

And if you do have one child, and one partner has already stepped back, the second child comes with its own calculations. The partner who stepped back is now further behind; another child pushes her further still. The partner still in the greedy job is now more indispensable; another child means he must work even harder to support a bigger household on what has become, in practice, one and a half incomes.

Greedy work is not just a theory of the pay gap. It is a theory of the fertility gap.

[BUILD]

Now, here is the hopeful part, and it is the part Goldin herself emphasizes. Greedy work is not a law of nature. It is a feature of how particular jobs are organized, and some jobs have changed.

Consider pharmacy. Over the course of a few decades, pharmacy in this country transformed from a profession dominated by independent owners who worked long and unpredictable hours into one dominated by employees of large chains and hospitals, with standardized systems, shared information and shifts. One pharmacist can hand off to another without loss. As a result, the penalty for working fewer or more predictable hours shrank, and pharmacy became, as Goldin has described it, one of the most egalitarian of the high-paid professions.

What happened in pharmacy can happen elsewhere. When work is organized so that people are good substitutes for each other, when information is shared, when teams can cover for each other, the premium for being personally available at every hour falls. And when that premium falls, the trade-off between career and family eases. For mothers and for fathers.

[PAUSE]

There is another hopeful sign. When the pandemic came in twenty twenty, it was, for working mothers, a catastrophe. Schools closed, daycare closed, and the burden fell overwhelmingly on women. Goldin's own account of the period recognizes how severely it set women back. But it also forced a vast experiment in flexible and remote work. And millions of workers, and employers, discovered that a great deal of work could be done without being physically present in an office from nine to six, or later.

That discovery is a gift, if we are wise enough to keep it. I do not say that every job can be remote. Nurses cannot be remote. Electricians cannot be remote. Teachers, as we learned at great cost, should not be remote. But many jobs can be made more predictable, more flexible and more forgiving of a parent who needs to leave at four and log back on at eight.

[SLOW]

So here is the counsel I would offer to every employer listening tonight. Look at your organization and ask: where is our work greedy, and does it need to be? Are we paying a premium for availability that we could replace with better hand-offs, better teams, better systems? Are we promoting people for the hours they show up rather than the value they create? Are we, without meaning to, telling our most talented young employees that they must choose between rising here and raising a family?

If the answer is yes, you are not only losing talent. You are taking part, in your small way, in the emptying of the cradles. And you have the power to stop.

## IX. The Two-Income Trap

There is a third mechanism I want to discuss, and it is the one that most directly explains why this is not only a story about professionals. It reaches every family, at every income.

In the early two thousands, a Harvard law professor who studied bankruptcy, Elizabeth Warren, and her daughter, Amelia Warren Tyagi, a business consultant, wrote a book with a title that has stayed in our vocabulary ever since: The Two-Income Trap.

You may have opinions about Elizabeth Warren's later political career. Set them aside for a moment. The argument of that book is worth hearing on its own terms, whatever your politics.

[PAUSE]

Their question was simple. In the generation since the early nineteen seventies, millions of mothers had entered the paid workforce. The typical middle-class family now had two earners instead of one. So why, they asked, did so many of these families feel less secure, not more? Why were so many of them going broke?

Their answer was that much of the second income had not gone into luxuries. They argued that families were not spending wildly on clothes and appliances and frivolous things. Instead, a great deal of the second income had been bid away, mostly on a few big fixed costs. Above all: housing in neighborhoods with good public schools.

[SLOW]

Here is how the trap works. Imagine a town where, a generation ago, most families had one earner. Houses in the good school district were priced according to what a one-income family could afford. Now imagine that, over time, more and more families gain a second earner. Those families can afford to pay more for the house near the good school. And they do, because every parent wants the best for their child.

But when everyone has a second income and everyone wants the same limited supply of houses near good schools, the second income does not buy a better life. It simply raises the price. The house that once cost one income now costs two. And the family that tries to live on one income finds that it has been priced out of the neighborhood altogether.

[LET IT LAND]

That is the trap. What began as a choice became a requirement. A second income that was once an option for women who wanted to work became, for millions of families, a necessity for anyone who wanted a decent home in a decent school district.

And there was a second, hidden cost. Warren and Tyagi pointed out that in the old one-earner family, the parent at home was, in a sense, an insurance policy. If the breadwinner lost his job or fell ill, the other parent could go to work. If a child or a grandparent got sick, there was someone home to care for them. They called the stay-at-home mother of earlier generations the most important part of the family's safety net.

In the two-income family, that safety net is gone. Both incomes are already committed to the mortgage and the fixed costs. If either parent loses a job, the family can fall. If a child gets sick, there is no slack. The household is running at full capacity, all the time, with nothing in reserve.

[PAUSE]

Now, I want to be fair. The book has critics. Some economists, such as Matt Bruenig, have argued that its numbers were wrong in important ways, that it used inflation measures that overstated the squeeze, and that the real answer was a stronger public safety net rather than nostalgia for one-earner households. That is a serious critique, and I take it seriously.

But notice that Warren and Tyagi themselves did not call for sending mothers home. They explicitly rejected that. What they called for were policies to restore the security that had been lost: better public schools in every neighborhood so that families need not bid against each other, universal preschool, limits on predatory lending. And whatever one thinks of their specific numbers, the central insight has held up in the lived experience of millions of families. The market adjusted to two incomes. It priced the basics of family life, the house, the school, the care, as if two full salaries were normal.

[BUILD]

And now connect it to the cradle.

A child, in the two-income world, is not only a joy. It is a threat to the arrangement that keeps the family afloat. If one parent steps back from work to care for a newborn, the family may not be able to make the mortgage. If both keep working, they must pay for childcare that may cost more than the rent. Warren and Tyagi went so far as to describe having children as the single best predictor that a woman would end up in bankruptcy.

Think about that sentence. In the richest country in the history of the world, having a child was identified as the single best predictor of a woman going broke.

[SLOW]

So the young couple in their late twenties does the math. They look at the price of a house in a district with good schools. They look at the price of daycare. They look at their student loans. They look at what would happen if either of them lost a job. And they conclude, not out of selfishness, but out of prudence: not yet. Maybe one child, later. Maybe none.

And who could tell them they are wrong? They are not wrong. They are doing exactly what the system, as we have built it, tells them to do.

[PAUSE]

Here is what I want you to see. The two-income trap is not about whether women should work. Most women want to work, and many families need them to, and that is fine. The trap is about what happens when an entire economy reorganizes itself around the assumption that every household will deliver two full-time workers to the labor market. When that assumption gets priced into housing, into schools, into the hours of work, into the very definition of a normal middle-class life, then a family with a newborn, which needs at least one parent to slow down for at least a while, becomes an abnormal and precarious thing.

We did not mean to make children a financial hazard. But that is what we have done. And no amount of speeches about family values will change it until we change the arithmetic.

## X. The Strongest Objection

I promised you that I would face the strongest objection to my argument, and I will do it now, squarely. A speech that only hears itself is not counsel. It is propaganda.

Here is the objection, and it is a powerful one.

[PAUSE]

If women's work were the cause of falling birth rates, the objection runs, then the countries where the most women work should have the fewest babies. And for a while, that was true. In the nineteen seventies, across the wealthy democracies, the countries with more women in the workforce did tend to have lower fertility.

But that relationship has reversed.

The economists Doepke, Hannusch, Kindermann and Tertilt, in the survey I mentioned earlier, tracked the correlation between women's participation in the workforce and the fertility rate across twenty-two wealthy countries over several decades. In the nineteen seventies, the correlation was negative: more women working, fewer children. But beginning in the nineteen eighties, it turned positive. Today, among the advanced economies, the countries where more women work tend, on the whole, to have more children, not fewer.

Think of the countries with some of the lowest fertility in the developed world in recent decades: Italy, Spain, Japan, South Korea. These are not countries where women have flooded into the workforce more than anywhere else. In several of them, women's participation has lagged. Meanwhile the Nordic countries, the United States, France, places with high female employment, have for most of this period had comparatively higher fertility.

[SLOW]

So the objector says: you have it backwards. Women's work is not the cause of low fertility. If anything, it goes along with higher fertility. Your whole speech is built on a mistake.

I want to thank the objector, because the objection is correct, and because it makes my argument stronger, not weaker.

[LET IT LAND]

Look again at what those economists concluded. They did not say that work and family do not conflict. They said the opposite. They said that what now determines fertility in rich countries is how easy it is for women to combine a career and a family. Where the two are compatible, women work and have children. Where the two are in conflict, women have to choose, and many choose fewer children, or fewer women work, or both.

And they named four things that make the combination possible. First, family policy, like affordable childcare and paid leave. Second, fathers who share the work of raising children. Third, social norms that support working mothers rather than condemning them. And fourth, flexible labor markets.

[PAUSE]

That is exactly my argument. I have never claimed that women working is the problem. I have claimed that a society which requires women to work on terms designed for people without children, and which provides little help with the children, will have fewer children. The cross-country evidence does not refute that. It confirms it. The countries that made room for both the career and the cradle got more of both. The countries that did not got fewer cradles.

The problem is not the career. The problem is the collision. And the collision is a choice we make as a society, every day, in our laws and our offices and our homes.

[BUILD]

But there is a second version of the objection, and it is harder. It goes like this.

Even the countries that have done everything right are now seeing fertility fall. Look at the Nordic countries. Look at Finland, with its famous baby boxes, its generous parental leave, its high-quality public daycare. Finland's fertility rate was about one point eight seven in twenty ten. By twenty twenty-four it had fallen to about one point two five, among the lowest in Europe. If generous family policy cannot hold up the birth rate even in Finland, the objector asks, why do you think it will work here?

And there is a third version, closer to home. The economists Melissa Kearney, Phillip Levine and Luke Pardue studied the fall in American birth rates since two thousand seven. They looked carefully for an economic or policy explanation: changes in the cost of housing, in childcare, in student debt, in state policies. They found that the Great Recession explained part of the early drop. But they could not find any single economic or policy change that explained most of the decline. Instead, they concluded that it seemed to reflect shifting priorities among younger generations: changing preferences about children, about life goals, about what it means to be a good parent.

And then there is the Pew finding I mentioned at the beginning: that the most common reason young childless adults give for not planning to have children is simply that they do not want to.

[PAUSE]

So the objector says: maybe this is not about economics at all. Maybe people just want different things now. And if so, who are you to tell them otherwise?

That is a serious objection, and I want to answer it in three parts.

First: I am not telling anyone what to want. If a person genuinely does not want children, that is their right, and I have said so, and I mean it. My argument is not about those people. It is about the gap. It is about the Americans who tell Gallup that the ideal family has two point seven children, and who are having one point six. It is about the forty-four percent of young childless adults who say they want to focus on their careers, and the thirty-six percent who say they cannot afford a child. These are people for whom the obstacles are not in their hearts. They are in their circumstances.

[SLOW]

Second: preferences do not form in a vacuum. When Kearney and her colleagues say that priorities have shifted, we have to ask: shifted in response to what? A young person who has watched her mother struggle through the two-income trap, who has seen the motherhood penalty up close, who has absorbed the message that a serious career requires your whole self, who looks at the price of a house and the price of daycare, may well come to tell a survey taker that she just does not want children. But that wanting, or not wanting, has been shaped by everything she has seen.

Remember the Danish finding: that the child penalty passes from mothers to daughters. Preferences are learned. A culture that makes parenthood look like a sacrifice of everything else will, over time, raise young people who do not want to make that sacrifice. That is not a refutation of the economic argument. It is the economic argument, working slowly, over a generation, through culture.

Third, and most important: I do not claim that money alone will solve this. The Finnish example is a genuine warning, and I will not pretend otherwise. Cash and leave and daycare, by themselves, are not enough. If they were, the problem would already be solved in Scandinavia. What the evidence suggests is that policy is necessary but not sufficient. You also need fathers who share the load. You need work that is not greedy. You need housing that families can afford. And you need a culture that tells young people, honestly and warmly, that a family is not the end of a meaningful life but one of the deepest ways of having one.

[LET IT LAND]

So yes, the objector is right that this is not only about women's careers. It is about the whole structure of adult life: the career, the house, the partner, the culture, the meaning. But the career is at the center of that structure, because it is where young adults spend the most hours of the most decisive years of their lives. And the career, as we have built it, has no room in it for a cradle.

[PAUSE]

I will grant the objector one more thing. Nobody, anywhere, has found a reliable way to bring fertility all the way back to replacement once it has fallen far below. Countries that have tried have sometimes nudged the numbers up a little, and sometimes not at all. I am not promising you a baby boom. I am not promising that any policy I propose will raise the birth rate by a specific amount. I would be lying if I did.

What I am promising is something more modest and, I think, more important. I am saying that a country that makes it easier for people to have the children they want is a better country, whatever happens to the aggregate numbers. If we close even part of the gap between the families people want and the families they have, we will have relieved a great deal of private sorrow, and we will have done right by the young. That is worth doing for its own sake.

## XI. What the World Is Telling Us

Let me take you on a short journey around the world, because other countries have been running the experiment we are now running, and some of them are further down the road.

[PAUSE]

Start in South Korea. In twenty twenty-three, South Korea's total fertility rate fell to about seven-tenths of a child per woman, the lowest in the world and among the lowest ever recorded in a nation at peace. It has edged up a little since. But think about what seven-tenths means. If it held, each generation would be roughly a third the size of the one before it.

South Korea is a country that, within living memory, went from one of the poorest nations on earth to one of the richest. Its young women became among the most highly educated in the world. And yet the culture of work, with its long hours and expectations of total devotion, and the expectations placed on wives and mothers at home, changed far more slowly.

[SLOW]

Claudia Goldin, in a recent paper titled Babies and the Macroeconomy, asked why some countries that had moderate fertility in the nineteen eighties later fell to what demographers call lowest-low fertility, below one point three children per woman. Her answer is illuminating. The countries that fell furthest were often those that experienced very rapid economic growth after the Second World War, after long periods of stagnation. Their economies modernized faster than their beliefs, values and traditions did. And that mismatch, she argues, produced conflict, between generations and between men and women. When young women's opportunities change faster than the expectations of their husbands, their employers and their parents, fertility can fall very quickly.

Read that again in the light of everything I have said tonight. It is the same story, told on a national scale. The economy changed. The opportunities changed. The women changed. But the structures around them, the job, the home, the husband's share of the work, the culture's expectations, did not change fast enough. And the cradle paid the price.

[PAUSE]

Now look at fathers, because the international evidence on fathers is some of the most striking I know.

In the same survey of the new economics of fertility, the researchers looked at how much of the childcare fathers do across a range of European countries. In every country, mothers did more. But the share varied a great deal: from less than a quarter of the childcare in Russia to more than forty percent in Norway.

And they found that in countries where fathers did little of the childcare, women were more likely to say they did not want another child, even when their partners did. Couples disagreed more. And fertility was lower.

When they widened the lens, using international survey data on men's share of housework and caregiving, the pattern held. The five countries where men contributed the most to housework and childcare all had fertility rates of one point eight or higher. The five countries where men contributed the least all had fertility rates below one point five. The correlation was above seven-tenths, which for social science is remarkably strong.

[LET IT LAND]

Let me say that in plain English. Where men do more at home, women have more babies.

It turns out that a baby requires the agreement of two people. And if one of them expects to carry nearly the whole burden of raising that baby, she is, quite reasonably, more likely to say no to the next one. You cannot have a cradle without a consent. And you will not get consent from a woman who has learned that the cradle is hers alone to rock.

[BUILD]

So here is a counsel for the men of this country, and I give it as a friend. If you want children, and if you want your country to have a future, then pick up the laundry. Learn the pediatrician's name. Take the leave your employer offers, and if your employer offers none, ask for it. Leave work early when the school calls, and let your boss see you do it. Be the father who is known at the daycare. Every hour you carry is an hour your partner does not have to, and it may be the hour that makes the next child possible.

That is not a burden. It is the great privilege of fatherhood, and too many men of my generation, and of the generations before, missed it.

[PAUSE]

One more lesson from abroad, and it is about culture. After the reunification of Germany, women from the former East and the former West lived in the same country under the same laws. But they had been raised in very different cultures. In the East, working mothers were the norm. In the West, there was a strong tradition that mothers of young children should stay home.

Researchers studying women who moved between the two regions after reunification found that East German women living in the West still returned to work much faster after having a child than their West German neighbors did. Norms traveled with them. Culture persisted, even under identical law.

That tells us that law matters, but it is not everything. The stories a society tells itself about what a good mother is, and what a good father is, and what a serious worker is, shape behavior for decades. If we want to make room for the cradle, we have to change some of those stories too.

[SLOW]

So what is the world telling us? It is telling us that there are two ways for a modern country to fail its families. One is to keep women out of the workforce, and lose their talent. The other is to bring women into the workforce on terms that make motherhood a career catastrophe, and lose their children. The countries that have done best have refused both failures. They have made room for the career and the cradle together.

We can learn from them. We do not need to copy anyone. We are Americans, and we will find our own way, suited to our own history and our own federal system and our own love of liberty. But we would be fools not to look at the evidence.

## XII. What We Must Not Do

Before I offer my counsel on what we should do, let me offer counsel on what we must not do. Because there are voices in our public life, on more than one side, that see the falling birth rate and reach for remedies that would be worse than the disease.

[PAUSE]

First, we must not blame women. I want to say this as clearly as I know how. The women of this country did not cause this problem. They responded, rationally and often heroically, to a system they did not design. They were told to get an education, and they did. They were told to build careers, and they did. They were told they could have it all, and then they discovered that "having it all" meant doing it all, alone, on a schedule built for someone else.

Any public figure who stands up and says that the problem with America is that women are too ambitious, too educated, too independent, is not only wrong on the facts. He is insulting the very people whose choices we need to make easier. You do not persuade someone to start a family by sneering at the life she has built.

[SLOW]

Second, we must not coerce. There is a long and ugly history, in our century and the last, of governments that tried to force their citizens to have more children, or fewer. Governments that banned contraception, restricted divorce, punished the childless, or treated women's bodies as instruments of national policy. Those experiments were cruel, and many of them did not even achieve their goals.

A free country persuades. It invites. It makes things possible. It does not compel. Every policy I will propose tonight is a policy that expands choice, not one that narrows it. If a woman chooses a career and no children, she should be honored. If she chooses children and a pause in her career, she should be honored. If she chooses both at once, she should be helped. The goal is not to dictate what people choose. The goal is to make sure that the choices people want are actually available to them.

[PAUSE]

Third, we must not pretend that the answer is to turn back the clock. Some people look at the nineteen fifties, with its high birth rates, and imagine that if we could just restore the one-earner family, the cradles would fill again. But that world rested on things we should not want to restore: closed professions, legal inequality, women with no independent means of support. And it rested on economic conditions, such as cheap housing and the unusual position of postwar America, that no policy can recreate.

More to the point, most women today do not want that world. Seventy-eight percent of prime-age women are in the workforce. Any plan that depends on reversing that is not a plan. It is a fantasy, and a disrespectful one.

But let me add a word of balance here, because I want to be fair to all families. Some women, and some men, do want to stay home with their young children, at least for a time. That is a legitimate choice too. Our goal should be a country where it is economically possible to stay home for a while and possible to keep working, without being punished for either. The family that chooses one earner for a season should not be priced out of the good school district, and the family that chooses two should not be bankrupted by daycare. Respect for women's choices means respect for all of them.

[BUILD]

Fourth, we must not use children as a weapon in our culture wars. The birth rate has become, in some quarters, a partisan football. One side accuses the other of not caring about the future. The other side accuses the first of wanting to control women. And the young couple trying to decide whether they can afford a second child is lost in the noise.

Children belong to no party. The longing for a family is felt in red states and blue states, in cities and in small towns, among the religious and the secular. If this issue becomes just another front in the culture war, we will accomplish nothing. If it becomes common ground, a place where people who disagree about much else can agree that young families deserve help, we can accomplish a great deal.

[PAUSE]

And fifth, we must not lie. We must not tell young people that fertility lasts forever, and we must not frighten them with exaggerations. We must not promise that a single tax credit will cause a baby boom. We must not pretend that the problem is simple or that our preferred solution is guaranteed to work. Washington, in his farewell, hoped his counsel might help his countrymen guard against what he called the impostures of pretended patriotism. There is a pretended love of family, too, the kind that offers easy answers and costs nothing. I will try to avoid it tonight. The problem is real, the causes are many, and the solutions are partial. But partial solutions, honestly pursued, are far better than none.

## XIII. Counsel: What We Should Do

Now let me turn to counsel. What should we do?

I am going to offer you a set of proposals, and I want to frame them honestly. Some of them our country has begun to try. Some of them are ideas that other countries have tested. Some of them are simply principles that I believe should guide us. None of them is a magic switch. Together, I believe, they would make a meaningful difference in the lives of young families, and in the number of children those families feel free to have.

They come in five parts: time, care, money, work and culture.

[PAUSE]

The first part is time. A newborn needs a parent. Every other wealthy democracy has recognized this in law. The United States remains the only member of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, the club of the world's advanced economies, without a national law requiring paid maternity leave.

What we have instead is the Family and Medical Leave Act, passed in nineteen ninety-three. It guarantees up to twelve weeks of job-protected leave. But that leave is unpaid. And it covers only workers at employers with fifty or more employees, who have worked there for at least twelve months and at least one thousand, two hundred and fifty hours in the past year. By one estimate, roughly four in ten American workers are not even eligible.

Twelve weeks, unpaid, for some. That is our national answer to the birth of a child.

[SLOW]

The states have begun to move. As of twenty twenty-five, twelve states and the District of Columbia had enacted mandatory paid family leave programs. And at the federal level, the tax law signed in July of twenty twenty-five made permanent a tax credit for employers who offer paid family and medical leave. Those are real steps, and I give credit to everyone of every party who took them.

But a tax credit that an employer may or may not use is not a guarantee. And a patchwork of state programs means that whether you can afford to stay home with your newborn depends on which side of a state line you happen to live on.

My counsel is this. Every working parent in America, mother and father, should have access to paid leave at the birth or adoption of a child. And a meaningful portion of that leave should be reserved for fathers, on a use-it-or-lose-it basis, because the evidence from abroad tells us that when leave is simply offered to the couple, mothers take almost all of it, and the motherhood penalty only deepens. When some leave belongs to the father and is lost if he does not take it, more fathers take it. And every father who takes it is a father who learns, in the first weeks of his child's life, that the cradle is his to rock as well.

[PAUSE]

The second part is care. I told you that the average price of childcare in America is more than thirteen thousand dollars a year for one child, and that care for two children costs more than rent in nearly every state. That is the single largest barrier I know of between the first child and the second.

Here, too, there have been steps. The tax law of twenty twenty-five raised the limit on dependent care flexible spending accounts, which let parents set aside pre-tax money for childcare, from five thousand dollars to seven thousand, five hundred. It also expanded the tax credit for employers who provide childcare to their workers: from twenty-five to forty percent of qualifying costs, and fifty percent for small businesses, with a much higher annual cap. Those are welcome.

But a pre-tax account does little for a family that cannot afford to put money aside in the first place, and an employer credit does nothing for the parent whose employer does not use it.

[BUILD]

My counsel is this. We should treat early childhood care the way we came, over the course of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, to treat elementary school: not as a private luxury, but as a public good that every family can count on. That does not mean one government program that every family must use. It can mean many things. It can mean support that follows the child to whatever provider the parents choose, a center, a church program, a neighbor who runs a licensed home daycare. It can mean support for a parent who chooses to stay home and provide the care herself or himself. What it must mean is that no young couple has to decide against a second child because care for two costs more than the roof over their heads.

And we must pay the people who do this work enough to make it a career, not a way station. The women and men who care for our youngest children are doing work on which the whole economy depends. A society that pays them poorly and then complains that there are not enough of them has only itself to blame.

[PAUSE]

The third part is money. Children cost money, and families with children carry those costs while also contributing to the future of a country that will one day depend on those children's work and taxes.

Again, there has been movement. The tax law of twenty twenty-five raised the maximum child tax credit from two thousand to two thousand, two hundred dollars per child, and indexed it to inflation. It also created a new kind of savings account for children, with the federal government depositing one thousand dollars for each citizen child born from the start of twenty twenty-five through the end of twenty twenty-eight. Whatever one thinks of the politics of that law, those provisions recognize a principle I share: that the arrival of a child is something the whole nation has a stake in.

But let us be honest about scale. Two hundred extra dollars a year is a gesture, not a solution, when childcare alone can cost thirteen thousand. And the refundable portion of the credit, the part that helps the families who earn the least, did not grow in the same way.

My counsel is this. Family support should be large enough to matter, simple enough to understand and reliable enough to plan around. A young couple deciding whether to have a child should be able to count on it, not wonder whether it will survive the next budget negotiation. It should be largest in the years when children are youngest and costs are highest. And it should be honest: offered as a recognition of what families contribute, not as a bribe to produce babies.

[SLOW]

The fourth part is work, and it is the part where government can do the least and the rest of us can do the most.

Remember greedy work. Remember pharmacy. The single most powerful change we could make is not a law at all. It is a change in how employers organize work, so that being a parent is not a career-ending event.

So here is my counsel to employers, and to the managers and executives who will be listening tonight. Build teams where people can cover for each other, so that no one is indispensable at every hour. Make schedules predictable, so that parents can plan. Offer flexibility where the work allows it, and do not punish the people who use it. Stop promoting on the basis of hours visible in the office, and start promoting on the basis of results. Create real paths back for parents who step away for a time, so that a year or two at home with a baby is a chapter in a career, not the end of one.

And to the institutions that run the most demanding career ladders in our society, the universities with their tenure clocks, the firms with their partnership tracks, the hospitals with their residencies: look hard at the calendar you impose. You have built ladders whose steepest rungs fall in exactly the years of peak fertility. Some of you have begun to allow pauses in the clock for new parents. Do more. Ask whether the ladder needs to be that steep, that early, for everyone. You are the gatekeepers of the professions. You can decide whether the gate swings open for parents or slams shut.

[PAUSE]

There is a piece of the work question that goes beyond employers, and it is housing. Remember the two-income trap: the bidding war for homes in neighborhoods with good schools. The deepest answer to that trap is to build more homes, and to make good schools available beyond a few expensive zip codes, so that families are not forced to bid against each other for the privilege of a decent education. I will not pretend that housing policy is simple, or that it is mainly a federal matter; much of it is decided in town halls and zoning boards. But every local official who blocks family housing should understand that the cost of a home is, for millions of young couples, a cost paid in children who are never born.

[BUILD]

And the fifth part, the hardest and the most important, is culture.

Laws can make room for the cradle. Employers can make room for the cradle. But only a culture can make young people want to fill it, and feel welcomed, not judged, when they do.

We need a culture that tells the truth about fertility, gently and early, so that young people can make their choices with open eyes. We need a culture that honors fathers who change diapers as much as fathers who close deals. We need a culture where a pregnant woman in a job interview is seen as a person with a future, not a liability. We need a culture where a crying baby on an airplane or in a restaurant is met with a smile, not a glare. We need a culture where a young couple announcing a third child hears congratulations, not "Are you sure you can afford that?"

And we need a culture that speaks honestly about what children give, not only about what they cost. Every economic study in this speech has measured the cost of children in dollars and hours and promotions. Those costs are real. But nobody has ever measured, on a spreadsheet, the weight of a sleeping child on your chest, or the first time a small hand reaches for yours, or the strange and overwhelming love that rearranges everything you thought mattered. We have become fluent in the language of what children take from us. We have grown shy about the language of what they give.

[SLOW]

I am not asking anyone to have children for the sake of the country. That is not a good reason, and it is not my business. I am asking the country to stop standing between its people and the children they want. And I am asking us, as a culture, to speak of family as one of the great adventures of a human life, open to all who want it, rather than as a sacrifice reserved for those who can afford it.

[PAUSE]

Time, care, money, work, culture. None of these alone will close the gap. Together they could change the calculation that every young couple in this country makes, in quiet conversations late at night, about whether there is room in their lives for a child, or for one more.

And let me add one counsel about how we pursue all of this. Do it together. Do it across party lines. A paid leave law that passes with one party's votes will be repealed by the next. A family policy built on common ground will last. The young people deciding whether to start families need to know that the help they are counting on will still be there when their child is five, and ten, and fifteen. That kind of reliability comes only from consensus. Seek it.

## XIV. The Room We Leave

Friends and fellow citizens, I am nearly done. Let me gather up what we have said.

[PAUSE]

We began with a woman of thirty-four, with a promotion on Tuesday and a fertility consultation on Thursday. We have seen that she is not alone. That the total fertility rate of this country has fallen from nearly four children per woman at the height of the baby boom to about one point six, the lowest ever recorded. That the age at which women have their first child has climbed to the highest in more than half a century. And that the families Americans say they want are larger than the families they are having.

We have seen that women's entry into the workforce was a revolution worth defending, and that it remains unfinished. That the modern career was built for a worker with someone at home, and that the years it demands most are the years when a family would demand most too.

We have seen the two clocks. We have seen that the greatest cost of a child is paid in time, and that it is paid mostly by mothers. We have seen the motherhood penalty, a fifth or more of a woman's earnings, and the fatherhood bonus that comes with the same child. We have seen greedy work, which rewards couples for splitting into one parent for the office and one for the home. We have seen the two-income trap, in which a second paycheck became a necessity and a child became a financial hazard.

And we have faced the strongest objection, and found that the evidence from around the world does not say that women's work empties cradles. It says that the collision between work and family empties cradles. Where nations make room for both, they get more of both.

[SLOW]

So here is my counsel, gathered into a single sentence. We restructured our society around two careers. Now we must restructure it again, around two careers and the children those careers were supposed to be for.

Because that is the thing we have forgotten. Why does anyone work? Not for the title on the door. Not, in the end, for the salary. We work to build a life. And for most people, across all of human history, the heart of that life has been the people we love, and among them, the children we raise. We have built an economy so efficient at producing careers that it has begun to crowd out the very lives that careers were meant to sustain.

[PAUSE]

Let me speak now, in closing, to a few people directly.

To the young women in this audience, and those who will hear these words later. You owe no one a child. Your life is yours. But if you want a family, I want you to know that wanting it is not a betrayal of your ambition or of the women who opened the doors you walked through. They opened those doors so that you could choose. A family is one of the things you are free to choose. And if the world makes that choice harder than it should be, the fault is the world's, not yours. Demand better. You have earned the right.

To the young men. Your partners cannot carry this alone, and they should not have to. The evidence is as clear as social science ever gets: where men do more at home, there are more children. If you want to be a father, be a father in full. Not a helper. Not a babysitter of your own children. A parent. It will cost you some hours at the office. It will give you something no office can.

[BUILD]

To the employers. You hold more power over the future of the American family than any politician. You decide whether the years from twenty-five to forty are years of impossible choices or years in which a career and a cradle can grow together. Make the work less greedy. Make the path back real. Make the father's leave normal. You will keep your best people, and you will help build a country with a future worth investing in.

To the lawmakers of both parties. You have taken first steps. A larger child credit. A savings account for newborns. A permanent credit for paid leave. A bigger credit for employers who provide childcare. I honor those steps. Now take the next ones. Guarantee paid leave. Make care affordable. Build homes for families. And do it together, so that the young couple deciding tonight whether to have a child can trust that the help will still be there tomorrow.

And to my own generation, the parents and grandparents. We built much of the world our children are struggling to live in. We bid up the houses. We designed the jobs. We wrote the rules. We owe the young more than lectures about why they are not giving us grandchildren. We owe them a world in which giving us grandchildren is not an act of financial recklessness.

[PAUSE]

When Washington ended his farewell, he did not promise that his counsel would be followed. He said, in effect, that he could not expect his words to change the course of the nation, but that he hoped they might do some good, now and then, and that this hope would be his reward. I feel something like that tonight. I do not expect one speech to fill a single cradle. But I hope that, here and there, in a boardroom or a legislature or a kitchen late at night, these words might tip a decision toward making room.

And from Douglass I take a different lesson. He taught us that a nation should be judged not by what it professes but by what it practices. That the gap between our words and our deeds is where our moral work lies.

[SLOW]

So let us be judged that way. We say we love children. Let us build a country with room for them. We say we honor mothers. Let us stop fining them. We say we believe in fathers. Let us give them the time to be fathers. We say we believe in equality between women and men. Let us finish the revolution, not by asking women to be more like the men of nineteen fifty-seven, but by asking work, and men, and all of us, to make room for the whole of a human life.

[BUILD]

Picture, one last time, that woman of thirty-four. Now imagine her ten years younger, in a different America. An America where her firm lets her step back for a year without stepping off the ladder. Where her husband takes his leave too, and is proud to. Where the daycare down the street costs what a family can bear. Where the house near the good school was priced for a family, not a bidding war. Where her colleagues congratulate her, and mean it. Where she knows the facts about her own body, and makes her choices with her eyes open, and the choices are real.

In that America, perhaps she still chooses to wait. That is her right. Perhaps she chooses no children at all. That is her right too. But perhaps, just perhaps, she chooses at twenty-eight to have the first child she always wanted, and at thirty-one the second, and finds that her career survived, and her marriage grew deeper, and her life was larger, not smaller, for the choice.

[PAUSE]

That America is not a fantasy. Pieces of it already exist, in some companies, in some states, in some countries, in some homes. Our task is to gather those pieces and build them into the ordinary life of a nation.

[SLOW]

A country is not only an economy. It is a promise between generations. Those who came before us built a world and handed it to us. We are building a world to hand to those who come after. And the question that will define us is not only whether we built a prosperous world, or a fair world, or a free world, though we must do all of those. It is whether we left room in it for the people who would inherit it.

The career and the cradle were never meant to be enemies. The career was supposed to build the house. The cradle was supposed to fill it.

[LET IT LAND]

Let us build a country where both can stand in the same room.

Thank you, and God bless your families, the ones you have, and the ones you hope for.
