# The Coming Depression

## I. The Alarm Is Ringing

Friends, a great many speeches are being made about artificial intelligence. They are made in boardrooms and in Congress, on earnings calls and on podcasts, in the opening paragraph of every commencement address in the country.

And nearly all of them say the same two things. They say the machines are coming. And they say that everything will be fine.

[PAUSE]

I have come to tell you that only the first half of that is true.

I am not here to tell you that the machines are evil. I am not here to tell you to smash them. I am here to talk about something older and plainer than any machine. I am here to talk about money: who earns it, who spends it, and what happens to a nation when the people who spend most of it stop earning it.

Because that is the question no one in the boardroom wants to sit with. Not "can the machine do the work?" The machine can do more of the work every month. The question is: when the work is done by the machine, who buys what the machine makes?

[SLOW]

Let me tell you about a young woman. I will not give her a name, because she is not one person. She is hundreds of thousands of people.

She graduated two springs ago. She did everything she was told to do. She took the loans. She picked the practical major. She learned to code, or to write marketing copy, or to answer customer tickets, or to do the first pass of a legal review. She sent out a hundred applications.

And the jobs that her older cousin walked into five years ago were simply not there. Not closed with a sign on the door. Just thinner. Fewer openings. Longer waits. A contract gig here, a part-time shift there. Somewhere in a building she will never see, a manager looked at a budget and decided that the entry-level seat could stay empty this year, because the software could do the first draft.

That young woman is a canary. And the coal mine is the American economy.

[PAUSE]

Now, I know what some of you are thinking. You are thinking: unemployment is low. The stock market is high. The economy grew again this spring. What depression?

And I want to honor that objection, because it is a fair one, and I will come back to it at length tonight. Unemployment is four point two percent. The economy is still growing. I will not stand here and tell you the depression has arrived. It has not.

But I want you to remember something. In the summer of eighteen seventy-three, the railroads were the greatest technology the world had ever seen, and the men who financed them were the heroes of the age. In the summer of nineteen twenty-nine, electricity and the automobile were remaking every home and every town in America, and the newspapers said that prosperity had become permanent.

Both of those summers were followed by the longest and the deepest economic collapses in the history of this country.

[LET IT LAND]

A depression does not announce itself in advance. It does not come with a press release. It arrives in the gap between what a few people are saying in public and what millions of people are feeling at their kitchen tables.

And I put it to you that the gap is wide open tonight.

So here is what I am going to argue, and I want to state it plainly so you can hold me to it.

I am going to argue that artificial intelligence, deployed the way we are deploying it now, under the rules we have now, threatens a working-class depression. Not a stock market crash first, though that may come. A depression of demand. A slow and then sudden collapse in the spending power of the bottom and middle of this country, because the wages that fund that spending are being pulled out from under it.

I am going to show you that this economy is already standing on one leg. That the households of working America are carrying more debt than ever before. That the house, the one asset that built the working-class middle, has slipped out of reach for a whole generation.

I am going to take you back to eighteen seventy-three, to nineteen twenty-nine, to the frame-breakers of Nottinghamshire, and to the steel mills of Youngstown, Ohio, because history does not repeat, but it does keep very good records.

I am going to give you the strongest argument against everything I have just said, and I am going to answer it.

And then I am going to tell you what we must do. Not someday. Now. While the alarm is still only an alarm, and not yet a fire.

[PAUSE]

There is an old speech, given in Athens more than two thousand three hundred years ago, by a man named Demosthenes. His city was being swallowed piece by piece by a power on its northern border, and his fellow citizens kept telling each other that there was no war, because no one had declared one.

His answer, in substance, was this: you keep asking when the war will begin. Look at the map. It began long ago. You are simply the last to notice.

I do not think the danger before us is a foreign king. The danger before us is a bargain coming apart: the bargain that said if you work, you will be able to live. And like those Athenians, we keep waiting for someone to announce that it is over before we admit that it is ending.

I am here to say: look at the map.

## II. The Advance, Step by Step

Let us look at it together, step by step, the way you would trace an army's march across a map.

[SLOW]

Step one. In August of twenty twenty-five, three economists at Stanford, Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar and Ruyu Chen, published a paper with a title that should have been on the front page of every newspaper in America. They called it "Canaries in the Coal Mine."

They did not rely on surveys or on guesses. They used payroll records from the largest payroll processing company in the country, covering millions of workers every month. And they found that since generative AI spread into the workplace, young workers, ages twenty-two to twenty-five, in the occupations most exposed to AI, saw their employment fall by about thirteen percent relative to everyone else, even after accounting for what was happening at their particular firms.

Thirteen percent. In the jobs that young people take to start a career.

And here is the detail that matters most. The researchers found that the damage was concentrated where AI automates the work, not where it assists the worker. And they found that the adjustment came through jobs, not through pay. Companies did not cut the young workers' wages. They simply stopped hiring them.

Older and more experienced workers in the same fields were holding steady. For now. The bottom rung of the ladder was the first to be sawed off.

[PAUSE]

Step two. In twenty twenty-five, the chief executive of one of the leading AI companies in the world, Dario Amodei of Anthropic, warned publicly that AI could eliminate half of all entry-level white-collar jobs within one to five years.

Think about who said that. Not a union organizer. Not a protester outside a data center. A man who builds the technology, telling us, in effect, what he believes his own product will do.

When the people selling the fire alarm tell you there is smoke, you should probably believe them.

Step three. By the spring of this year, twenty twenty-six, the outplacement firm Challenger, Gray and Christmas, which has tracked American layoff announcements for decades, reported that artificial intelligence had become the leading reason employers gave for cutting jobs. In April alone, employers announced more than eighty-three thousand cuts, and more than twenty-one thousand of them were attributed to AI. More than one in four.

By the end of the summer, the count of announced cuts attributed to AI this year had passed one hundred thousand.

Now, I promised you honesty, so here it is. Some of those companies are surely blaming the robot for cuts they would have made anyway. Experts have a name for it: AI-washing. And in August the AI number dipped. But listen to what Andy Challenger himself said this spring, and I think it is the truest sentence anyone has spoken about this economy all year. He said that whether or not individual jobs are being replaced by AI, the money for those roles is.

[LET IT LAND]

The money for those roles is being replaced. Hold onto that. Because the money is the whole story.

Step four. Look where the money is going. This year, the four largest technology companies in America, Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta, told investors they would spend somewhere around seven hundred billion dollars on capital expenditure, most of it on data centers, chips and the power to run them. The Financial Times put the figure at about seven hundred and twenty-five billion, up roughly three quarters from the year before. Analysts expect the number to pass a trillion dollars next year.

Seven hundred billion dollars in one year. That is not a bet on a gadget. That is a bet on replacing human effort at the scale of a nation's economy. No company spends that kind of money to make its workers slightly more comfortable. They spend it because they expect the machines to do work that people are paid to do today.

Step five. Look at the labor market itself. On the second of October, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that the American economy added just twenty-nine thousand jobs in September. The revisions took sixty thousand jobs away from July and August combined. July is now recorded as a month in which the country lost jobs.

Seven point one million Americans are unemployed. One point nine million of them have been out of work for more than six months. That is more than one in four of all the unemployed, stuck.

And look at the industry the bureau singled out: financial activities, the world of insurance offices and back-office processing, has shed one hundred and twenty-nine thousand jobs since its peak in May of last year, most of them at insurance carriers. That is precisely the kind of paperwork, claims, and routine judgment that these systems are built to absorb.

Step six. Look at what Americans are telling their own central bank. Every year the Federal Reserve surveys households about their finances. In the survey published this year, forty-two percent of adults said that finding or keeping a job was a concern for them, up from thirty-seven percent the year before. And fifteen percent of adults under thirty said they were not working and that being unable to find work was part of the reason.

[PAUSE]

Do you see the march? Step by step. The young first. Then the back office. Then the announcements. Then the money pouring into machines rather than people. Then the slowing payrolls. Then the fear in the surveys.

None of these steps alone is a depression. Each of them, taken alone, can be explained away. A bad month. A noisy statistic. A company cutting costs. A generation that needs to be more flexible.

That is exactly what the Athenians said about every town they lost. It was far away. It was small. It was a special case. Demosthenes' answer was to read the list out loud, one town after another, until the pattern could not be denied.

I have read you the list. And I put it to you that the pattern can no longer be denied.

[BUILD]

We were told the machines would take the dangerous jobs first. They are taking the entry-level jobs first.

We were told the machines would take the dull jobs first. They are taking the jobs that were supposed to lead somewhere.

We were told this would happen slowly, over decades, giving us time to adapt. The money says otherwise. Seven hundred billion dollars a year says otherwise. The men who build these systems say otherwise.

And so the question before us is not whether this is happening. The question is what happens to an economy like ours when it does.

## III. The Machine That Eats Its Own Customers

To answer that question, I need to tell you something about how this economy actually works, something so basic that it is easy to forget.

[SLOW]

Your wages are someone else's revenue.

Every paycheck that a nurse, a trucker, a call center worker, a paralegal or a junior programmer receives is spent, most of it, within weeks. It goes to the landlord and the grocery store, to the car dealer and the dentist, to the restaurant on Friday night and the hardware store on Saturday morning. And every one of those businesses uses that money to pay its own workers, who spend it again.

That is not a theory. That is the circulatory system of the economy.

In the second quarter of this year, personal consumption, the spending of households, made up sixty-seven and a half percent of the entire gross domestic product of the United States. Two out of every three dollars of economic activity in this country is a household buying something.

So when you remove wages from the system, you are not just hurting the workers who lose them. You are draining the bloodstream that feeds every business in the country, including, eventually, the businesses that did the firing.

[PAUSE]

Here is the trap, and I want you to see it clearly, because it is the heart of my whole argument.

For any single company, replacing workers with software is rational. The software is cheaper. It does not get sick. It does not ask for a raise. The company's costs go down, its margins go up, its stock price rises. The chief financial officer is doing exactly what she is paid to do.

But what is rational for one company can be ruinous for all companies together. Economists call this the fallacy of composition. If one person in a crowded stadium stands up, he sees better. If everyone stands up, no one sees better, and everyone's legs get tired.

If one company replaces its customer service department with software, it saves money. If every company replaces every customer service department, every claims office, every junior analyst, every first-draft writer, every entry-level coder, then millions of people stop earning, and millions of people stop spending. And every one of those companies discovers that its costs went down, but so did its sales.

The machine, in the end, eats its own customers.

[LET IT LAND]

Now, you might say: but that has been true of every technology. The tractor, the assembly line, the computer. Each of them replaced workers, and each time, the economy grew and the workers found new jobs. I will deal with that argument in full later tonight, because it is the strongest one against me.

But I want you to notice something first. The link between what American workers produce and what American workers are paid broke a long time ago. Long before anyone had heard of a chatbot.

Here are two numbers from the government's own data.

Output per hour of work in the American business sector, the measure economists call productivity, has risen to about two and four tenths times what it was in nineteen seventy-nine. More than doubled.

Over the same span, the inflation-adjusted weekly earnings of the typical full-time worker, the worker right in the middle, rose by about thirteen percent.

[PAUSE]

I want to be fair about these numbers. They do not measure exactly the same thing. One is an average of output across the whole business sector, and the other is the pay of the median worker. Economists argue, rightly, about how to line them up, about benefits, about which price index to use. You can shrink the gap with careful adjustments. But you cannot make it go away.

And there is a third number that settles the argument for me. The Bureau of Labor Statistics keeps an index of labor's share of income in the business sector, the slice of everything produced that goes to the people who work, as opposed to the people who own. In the second quarter of this year, that index stood at its lowest level since the series began in nineteen forty-seven.

The lowest share for workers in nearly eighty years of records. And this is before the machines have done most of what their builders promise.

[BUILD]

So understand what we are being asked to believe. We are being asked to believe that a technology designed to replace human labor will, this time, unlike the last forty-five years, share its gains with the people it replaces.

We are being asked to believe that the same incentives which let productivity double while median pay crept along will suddenly reverse themselves.

We are being asked to believe that companies spending seven hundred billion dollars to reduce their need for workers will turn around and pay the workers they still have more.

I do not believe it. And I do not think you do either.

[PAUSE]

And there is one more piece of this machine that we need to look at. When a dollar of wages moves from a working family to a wealthy shareholder, it does not get spent the same way.

This is one of the most solid findings in economics. A family living paycheck to paycheck spends nearly every extra dollar it gets, because it has to: on rent, on food, on a car repair it has been putting off. A wealthy household spends a much smaller share of each additional dollar, because its needs are already met. The rest goes into savings, into stocks, into assets.

So when AI takes income from the many and concentrates it among the few, the total amount of spending in the economy falls, even if total income stays exactly the same. The money does not vanish. It just stops circulating. It pools at the top like water behind a dam, and the fields downstream go dry.

That is how you get a depression in the middle of a technological miracle. Not because we produce too little. Because the people who would buy what we produce no longer have the money to buy it.

[SLOW]

There is an old story, told about Henry Ford, that he raised his workers' pay so they could afford the cars they built. Historians argue about how much of that story is true; he had plenty of other reasons, like keeping workers from quitting. But the story has lasted for a century because it captures something true: a car company needs people who can afford cars. Somewhere along the way, we forgot the lesson. We built an economy where the companies that make the products no longer think of their workers as their customers, because the customers are someone else's workers.

AI completes that forgetting. It offers every company the dream of revenue without payroll. And if every company achieves that dream at once, the revenue disappears with the payroll.

That is the machine that eats its own customers. And it is already at the table.

## IV. An Economy Standing on One Leg

Now let me show you how close to the edge we already are, before the full weight of this technology has landed.

[SLOW]

Picture the letter K. One arm goes up and to the right. The other goes down and to the right. That is the shape economists have used for several years now to describe the American economy. One part of the country rising. Another part sliding.

It is not a slogan. It is in the spending data.

Mark Zandi, the chief economist at Moody's Analytics, has been tracking how much of America's consumer spending comes from the top ten percent of earners. In the second quarter of twenty twenty-five, that top tenth accounted for forty-nine point two percent of all consumer spending in the United States. The highest share since his data series begins in nineteen eighty-nine. In twenty twenty, it had been about forty-three percent.

Let that sink in. Ten percent of the people are doing half of the spending. Zandi estimated that the spending of that top tenth alone comes to nearly a third of the entire economy.

And the other ninety percent? Moody's found that middle-income households in the spring of twenty twenty-five spent scarcely more than they had spent in the same months of twenty twenty-three and twenty twenty-four. Flat. Running in place while prices rose around them.

[PAUSE]

The Bank of America Institute looked at its own customers' card and bank data and found the same shape. In November of last year, spending by households in the top third of the income distribution was up four percent from a year earlier, the fastest pace in four years. Spending by the bottom third was up less than one percent.

Equifax, the credit bureau, reported this April that for the lowest-income households, real earned income, income after inflation, was actually falling.

And Mark Zandi, a careful and moderate man not given to theatrics, described lower-income consumers as hanging on by their fingertips.

[LET IT LAND]

Now, I promised fairness, so let me give you the other side here too. Economists at Goldman Sachs argued this spring that the K-shape may be overstated. They suggested that weak sales at stores serving lower-income shoppers partly reflect changes in immigration, fewer customers rather than poorer ones. That is a serious point, and I take it seriously.

But notice what even the optimists concede. The bottom is not leading. The bottom is not even keeping pace. The question is only whether it is sliding or merely stalled.

And here is the thing about an economy that stands on one leg. It can stand for a long time. It can even look graceful. But it cannot take a shove.

[BUILD]

So ask yourself: what holds up that one leg?

The top ten percent are spending freely for one main reason. Their wealth has soared. Their stock portfolios have soared. Their home values have soared. Zandi said of them that their financial situation is about as good as it has ever been.

And what has driven the stock market? To a remarkable degree, the very same companies pouring seven hundred billion dollars into artificial intelligence, and the chip makers and power companies that supply them. On the sixth of this month, the S&P five hundred index closed at a record high, more than double where it stood in October of twenty twenty-two, a few weeks before the first public chatbot set off this whole boom. The market's great leap has been, in large part, a bet that AI will pay off.

Do you see the knot we have tied ourselves into?

The bottom half of the economy is being squeezed by AI taking wages. And the top half of the economy is spending because of the stock market wealth created by the bet on AI. Both legs of the K are tied to the same machine.

If the AI bet pays off the way its backers hope, it does so by replacing labor, which means the bottom leg weakens further. And if the AI bet disappoints, if the returns on those trillions do not arrive on schedule, then the stock market falls, the wealth effect reverses, and the top leg buckles too.

[PAUSE]

Heads, the workers lose. Tails, everyone loses.

That is not a stable economy. That is a tightrope. And we have taken away the net.

[SLOW]

There is something else that history teaches about an economy shaped like a K. When spending depends on the confidence of a small number of wealthy households, the whole country's prosperity becomes hostage to their mood. A wealthy family that sees its portfolio fall by a fifth does not go hungry. It simply postpones the kitchen remodel, skips the second vacation, waits on the new car.

But those postponements are someone's job. The contractor who would have built that kitchen. The hotel worker. The car salesman. The top of the K sneezes, and the bottom catches pneumonia.

In a healthier economy, the spending of the broad middle acts as a keel. It is steady because it is necessary. People need groceries and rent and gas in good times and bad. An economy whose spending is spread across a hundred million working households is hard to capsize.

We have been quietly removing the keel for years. And now we are about to sail into the strongest wind of the century.

[PAUSE]

One more thing about the K before we leave it. The top arm does not just go up. It pulls away. Every year that half of spending comes from the top tenth, more of the economy reorganizes itself to serve them. Luxury goods. Premium services. Concierge medicine. Second homes.

The businesses that served the middle, the department stores, the family restaurants, the main street shops, find themselves serving a customer who has less every year. They close or they consolidate. And the jobs they provided, which were themselves the incomes of the middle, close with them.

The K is not a snapshot. It is a motion. And AI is about to step on the accelerator.

## V. The Ledger

So far I have talked about income. Now let me talk about the other side of the ledger. Because a family that loses its paycheck does not start from zero. It starts from below zero.

[SLOW]

In August, the Federal Reserve Bank of New York published its quarterly report on American household debt. The total stood at eighteen trillion, seven hundred and seventy-one billion dollars.

Eighteen point eight trillion dollars. More than thirteen trillion of it in mortgages. One point seven trillion in auto loans. One point six five trillion in student loans. And one trillion, two hundred and sixty-three billion dollars on credit cards, up twenty-one billion dollars in just three months.

I want to be honest about these numbers too. Total debt actually dipped slightly last quarter, mostly because of a quirk in how mortgage transfers were reported. And the economy has grown alongside the debt; a bigger economy carries bigger numbers. Debt is not a sin. A mortgage is how working families have built wealth for three generations.

But debt has one property that matters more than any other in a downturn. It does not shrink when your income does.

[PAUSE]

Your paycheck can disappear tomorrow. Your car payment will not. Your credit card minimum will not. Your student loan, which cannot be discharged in bankruptcy except in rare cases, certainly will not.

And look at how much strain is already in the system, before any wave of AI layoffs.

The New York Fed reports that four point seven percent of all household debt is in some stage of delinquency. Look at the rate at which balances are falling ninety days or more behind, what the Fed calls serious delinquency. For credit cards, on an annualized basis, the flow into serious delinquency was nearly seven percent of balances, higher than a year earlier. For auto loans, three percent, also higher than a year earlier. For student loans, more than seven and three quarters percent.

These are not crisis numbers yet. But they are the numbers of a population with no cushion.

[SLOW]

How thin is the cushion? The Federal Reserve asks Americans a famous question every year. If you faced an unexpected expense of four hundred dollars, how would you pay for it?

In the survey published this year, sixty-three percent said they would cover it with cash or its equivalent. That means more than a third of American adults could not handle a four hundred dollar emergency without borrowing, selling something, or simply not paying.

Four hundred dollars. A tire and a tow. A trip to urgent care. One week of groceries for a family.

And the nation's personal saving rate, the share of after-tax income that households set aside, fell to four point one percent in August. The cushion is getting thinner, not thicker.

[PAUSE]

Then there is a newer kind of debt that does not even show up cleanly in the old statistics. Buy now, pay later. Split your purchase into four easy payments. The Federal Reserve found that fifteen percent of adults used these services in the year it surveyed, and nearly a quarter of those users had paid late.

When people are splitting groceries into installments, they are not living on their income. They are living on their future income. And that is precisely the income artificial intelligence is threatening to take away.

[BUILD]

Now let me tell you why this matters so much, because it is the mechanism that turns a recession into a depression.

In nineteen thirty-three, at the bottom of the Great Depression, the economist Irving Fisher published a theory that has haunted economics ever since. He had been one of the most celebrated economists in America. He had also, infamously, declared shortly before the crash of nineteen twenty-nine that stock prices looked permanently high. He lost a fortune. And in the wreckage he understood something that the optimists had missed.

He called it debt-deflation. It goes like this.

When people and businesses are heavily in debt and a shock arrives, they all try to pay down their debts at once. They cut spending. They sell assets. But when everyone sells at once, prices fall. When prices fall, the real burden of every debt grows, because the dollars you owe are now worth more and the things you own are worth less. So people cut spending further and sell more. Businesses lose sales, lay off workers, and those workers default on their debts. Banks, facing losses, stop lending.

Fisher's summary of the trap is one of the most chilling sentences in economics: the more the debtors pay, the more they owe.

[LET IT LAND]

Later economists built on his insight. Hyman Minsky explained how debt piles up during good times on the assumption that good times will continue. And Ben Bernanke, who would go on to chair the Federal Reserve through the crisis of two thousand eight, showed how falling asset values and bankruptcies wreck the balance sheets of banks, so that credit dries up exactly when it is needed most.

Now put that together with what I have shown you.

A working population carrying eighteen point eight trillion dollars of debt. A third of it unable to absorb a four hundred dollar shock. Credit card delinquency flows near seven percent. And a technology arriving that threatens not a temporary layoff, the kind you wait out until the factory reopens, but the permanent disappearance of whole categories of work.

A temporary layoff, you can borrow your way through. A permanent one, you cannot. The debt stays. The income does not come back.

[SLOW]

That is the difference between a dip and a spiral. That is the difference between a bad year and a lost decade. Not the technology itself, but the technology landing on a population that is already leveraged to the hilt, in an economy that depends on that population's spending.

The machine takes the paycheck. The debt takes the savings. The spending stops. The businesses that depended on the spending lay off their own workers. And those workers, too, are in debt.

[PAUSE]

Ask yourselves who holds those debts. The banks. The credit card companies. The auto lenders. The pension funds and insurance companies that hold bundles of consumer loans as investments. When the defaults come, they do not stay in the kitchen. They walk straight into the financial system.

We learned that in two thousand eight. A crisis that began with mortgages in the outer suburbs of Las Vegas and Phoenix and Riverside ended with the largest financial institutions on earth begging Washington for rescue. The ledger connects everyone. The waitress's credit card and the banker's balance sheet are the same piece of paper, viewed from opposite ends.

## VI. The House That Wages Built

Let me turn now to the house. Because for most working families in America, the house is not just where they live. It is the only real wealth they will ever own.

[SLOW]

For three generations, the American bargain went something like this. You get a steady job. You save for a down payment. You buy a modest house in your late twenties or early thirties. You pay it off over thirty years. And by the time you retire, you own something solid, something you can pass on, something that is yours.

That bargain built the American middle class. The house was the savings account of people who did not have savings accounts.

Now look at what has happened to it.

[PAUSE]

In the year two thousand, the median price of a house sold in the United States was about one hundred and sixty-five thousand dollars. The median household income was about forty-two thousand dollars. A house cost roughly four times a year's income.

In the second quarter of this year, the median sale price was four hundred and ten thousand, seven hundred dollars. The latest median household income, for twenty twenty-five, was eighty-seven thousand, four hundred and sixty dollars. A house now costs nearly five times a year's income.

That may not sound like much of a change. Four to five. But you do not pay for a house with its price. You pay for it with its monthly payment. And the monthly payment depends on the interest rate.

[SLOW]

In January of twenty twenty-one, the average thirty-year mortgage rate in America hit two point six five percent, the lowest in the history of the series. In January of this year, it was a little over six percent. Last week, it was seven point four percent.

Seven point four percent, on a house that costs nearly five times the typical income. Do the arithmetic on a four hundred thousand dollar mortgage and the interest alone in the first year is nearly thirty thousand dollars. For the median household, that is a third of their gross income before they have paid a single dollar of principal, property tax or insurance.

[LET IT LAND]

And so the young have been locked out. Last November, the National Association of Realtors reported that first-time buyers made up just twenty-one percent of home purchases, the lowest share since the association began tracking in nineteen eighty-one. Their chief economist observed that the first-time buyer share had been cut in half since two thousand seven.

And the typical first-time buyer is now forty years old.

[PAUSE]

Forty. Think about what that means. A whole generation that, at the age their parents were paying down a mortgage, is still paying rent. Paying someone else's mortgage. Building someone else's wealth.

Now bring the machines back into this picture.

The very generation most locked out of housing, the young, the entry-level, the recent graduates, is the generation the Stanford economists found is being hit first by AI. The canaries are not just losing their first jobs. They are losing the first step toward the only form of wealth working people have ever reliably built.

If you cannot get the entry-level job, you cannot build the work history. If you cannot build the work history, you cannot get the raise. If you cannot get the raise, you cannot save the down payment. If you cannot save the down payment, you rent at forty, and at fifty, and you enter old age with nothing that is yours.

[BUILD]

And for those who did get in, there is another danger. Remember two thousand eight.

In that crisis, house prices fell, and families who had borrowed heavily found that they owed more than their homes were worth. When they lost their jobs, they could not sell their way out, because selling would not cover the loan. Foreclosures spread from house to house, street to street. Each foreclosed house pushed down the price of every house around it. Neighborhoods that had been the pride of their owners became rows of boarded windows.

Now imagine that dynamic in a region where AI has hollowed out a major employer. An insurance hub. A city built around back-office processing, call centers, logistics coordination, medical billing. The jobs go. The people who held them cannot make their payments. Some sell. More try to sell. There are fewer buyers, because the buyers would have been the people who worked at the same employer.

A housing market is only as strong as the incomes of the people who would buy in it. Take away the incomes, and the houses are just wood and nails with a mortgage attached.

[SLOW]

Meanwhile, the cost of simply living keeps rising. Over the twelve months to September, consumer prices rose by about three point seven percent. Average hourly earnings rose by three percent. Read those two numbers together and you find that the typical hour of work bought less in September than it did a year before.

A gallon of regular gasoline that cost about three dollars and six cents in the middle of October last year cost about four dollars and thirty-three cents last week.

I am not going to stand here and tell you why gasoline went up or why mortgage rates climbed. There are many causes, and they deserve their own speech. My point is simpler. Every one of these pressures lands hardest on the bottom of the K. The wealthy household barely notices a dollar more at the pump. The family already splitting its groceries into four payments notices it every single week.

[PAUSE]

So here is the picture of the American working family on the eve of the AI transition. Real pay slipping. Savings thin. Debt high. The house out of reach for the young and a heavy burden for those who stretched to buy. Gasoline up. Rent up. Insurance up.

That family is not ready for a shock. And we are about to deliver one.

## VII. How the Spiral Would Turn

Before I take you into history, I want to do something that economists rarely do in public. I want to walk you, step by step, through how a demand depression of this kind would actually unfold. Not as a prophecy. I cannot see the future. But as an illustration, built from the mechanisms I have shown you, so that you can recognize the signs if they come.

[SLOW]

Imagine a mid-sized American city. Call it any name you like. It lost its factory a generation ago, and it rebuilt itself the way many such cities did, with white-collar service work. A regional insurance office. Two large call centers. A medical billing company. The back office of a bank. A logistics firm that coordinates trucks across three states. Together, they employ perhaps ten thousand people.

These are decent jobs. Not glamorous, but steady. They pay enough for a car, an apartment or a modest house, a family vacation once a year. And the people who hold them spend their paychecks in the city: at the supermarkets, the restaurants, the gyms, the daycare centers, the auto repair shops, the dentists.

[PAUSE]

Year one. The insurance company installs a system that handles the first review of claims. It does not lay anyone off. It simply stops replacing people who leave. The call centers deploy automated agents that resolve most routine calls. They let their contracts with a staffing agency lapse. The billing company cuts its night shift.

No headlines. No mass layoff notices. The city's unemployment rate barely moves. But the young people who would have taken those jobs, the recent graduates of the community college and the state university branch campus, find the doors quietly closed. Some move away. Some take part-time work in retail and food service. Some move back in with their parents.

That is the canary phase. It is where I believe we are today, in much of the country.

[SLOW]

Year two. The systems get better. The companies that were cautious become confident. The insurance office announces a restructuring: eight hundred positions eliminated. One of the call centers closes entirely, its work absorbed by software running in a data center in another state. The bank consolidates its back office.

Now the unemployment rate moves. And now the second ring of the spiral begins to turn. The supermarket sees its sales soften and cuts hours. The restaurants on the strip near the office park lose their lunch crowd; two of them close. The daycare center loses families who no longer need full-time care, or can no longer afford it. The gym loses members.

Every one of those businesses has workers of its own. They are not in the AI-exposed occupations at all. They are cooks and cashiers and childcare workers and personal trainers. But their jobs depended on the paychecks of the people who were.

Economists call this the multiplier. Money spent becomes someone else's income, which is spent again. It works in both directions. When the first paycheck disappears, the second and third paychecks start to disappear too.

[PAUSE]

Year three. Now the debt enters the story. The families that lost the office jobs have burned through what little savings they had. Remember, more than a third of American adults cannot cover a four hundred dollar emergency with cash. They have been living on credit cards. Now the card balances hit their limits. The minimum payments fall behind. The car loans fall behind.

Some of them have houses. They try to sell. But who is buying? The people who would buy were the people who worked at the same offices. Prices in the city begin to soften. Families who bought at the peak, with small down payments, find they owe more than their homes are worth. And the foreclosures begin.

[SLOW]

Now the city itself feels it. Property tax revenue falls as home values fall. Sales tax revenue falls as spending falls. Income tax revenue, in a state that has one, falls as wages fall. The city and the state face budget gaps, and most states are required by their own constitutions to balance their budgets. So they cut. Teachers. Bus routes. Library hours. Road repairs.

And so the public sector, which could have cushioned the blow, instead deepens it. More layoffs. Less spending. Another turn of the spiral.

[PAUSE]

Year four. The local banks and credit unions, which hold the car loans and the mortgages and the small business loans of this city, find their losses mounting. They tighten lending. A restaurant owner who wants to adapt by opening a catering business cannot get a loan. A laid-off claims adjuster who wants to start a small firm cannot get credit. The very entrepreneurship that the optimists count on to create new jobs is choked off by the balance sheets of the lenders.

That is Bernanke's insight in miniature. When the banks are wounded, credit dries up exactly when it is most needed.

[BUILD]

Now multiply that city by a hundred. By three hundred. Spread the pattern across every region that rebuilt itself on routine office work. And at the same time, picture the top of the K.

The wealthy households who are doing half the spending have been fine through all of this. Their portfolios are full of the companies doing the automating, and those companies' profits have been strong, because their costs have fallen. But at some point, the falling sales across hundreds of cities show up in the earnings reports. Consumer companies miss their targets. Retailers warn. Banks report rising losses.

And the market, which had priced in endless growth from the AI bet, begins to ask whether the customers will be there to buy what the machines produce.

When the stock market falls, the top of the K pulls back. The second home is postponed. The luxury car can wait. The renovation is cancelled. And now the spiral has reached the top of the economy, and there is no leg left to stand on.

[SLOW]

That is how the spiral would turn. Not in a single crash, but in rings, spreading outward from the jobs that were automated to the jobs that depended on them, from wages to debt, from debt to banks, from cities to states, and finally from Main Street to Wall Street.

[PAUSE]

Now you might say: but the government will step in. The Federal Reserve will cut interest rates. Congress will pass a stimulus. That is what happened in two thousand eight and in twenty twenty.

And I hope it would. But let me tell you why I am less confident than I would like to be.

The Federal Reserve fights downturns mainly by cutting interest rates. Today, the top of its target range for its policy rate is four percent. That gives it room to cut, more room than it had in two thousand eight. But inflation over the past year has been running at about three point seven percent, well above the Fed's two percent goal. The yield on the ten-year Treasury bond is around five percent. Mortgage rates have been rising, not falling, this year.

A central bank facing high inflation is a central bank with its hands partly tied. If it cuts rates aggressively to fight a demand collapse, it risks fanning inflation. If it holds rates high to fight inflation, it risks deepening the collapse. That is a terrible position to be in, and it is a position we may already be in.

[SLOW]

And even if the Fed cuts, there is a deeper problem. Lower interest rates work by encouraging people and businesses to borrow and spend. But if the problem is that wages are being permanently replaced by machines, cheaper borrowing does not solve it. It may even make it worse, by making it cheaper still for companies to borrow money to buy more machines.

You cannot fix a shortage of paychecks with a discount on loans.

[PAUSE]

As for Congress: every one of us has watched Washington in recent years. Ask yourself honestly how quickly and how wisely it would act in the middle of a crisis that began quietly, in a hundred cities, without a single dramatic day to point to. A crisis where every lost job could be explained away as a special case. A crisis that the people winning from it would have every incentive to deny.

That is why I keep returning to the same point. The time to build the defenses is before the spiral turns. Because once it is turning, every institution we would rely on to stop it will be under strain at the same time.

## VIII. Eighteen Seventy-Three

Now I want to take you back in time. Because everything I have described so far, the miracle technology, the flood of investment, the confidence that this time is different, has happened in this country before.

[SLOW]

In the decade after the Civil War, the railroad was artificial intelligence.

It was the technology that changed everything. It shrank a continent. A journey that had taken months took days. It created national markets where there had been local ones. It made fortunes on a scale the world had never seen. And it drew in capital, from American savers and from European investors, in quantities that dwarfed anything that had come before.

Everyone could see that the railroad was the future. And everyone was right. The railroad was the future.

That is the first lesson of eighteen seventy-three, and I want you to hold onto it. Being right about the technology does not protect you from being ruined by the economy built around it.

[PAUSE]

The most famous banker in America in those years was a man named Jay Cooke. He had helped finance the Union during the Civil War by selling government bonds to ordinary citizens. He was a hero. And after the war, he threw himself into the greatest railroad project of the age, the Northern Pacific, a line meant to run across the northern plains to the Pacific.

The money required was staggering. Cooke's firm sold bonds to finance the construction. And in September of eighteen seventy-three, it could not sell enough of them. The buyers were not there. The future was still coming, but the money for it had run out ahead of the returns.

On the eighteenth of September, eighteen seventy-three, Jay Cooke and Company declared bankruptcy.

[LET IT LAND]

What followed was a panic. Two days later, the New York Stock Exchange closed its doors, and it stayed closed for ten days. In New York City, bank reserves fell from fifty million dollars to seventeen million in a matter of weeks. By November, fifty-five railroads had failed. Within a year of the crisis, sixty more had gone bankrupt.

Railroad construction, the great engine of the boom, collapsed. In eighteen seventy-two, America had laid about seven thousand five hundred miles of new track. In eighteen seventy-five, it laid about sixteen hundred.

And between eighteen seventy-three and eighteen seventy-five, eighteen thousand businesses failed.

[SLOW]

The National Bureau of Economic Research, which keeps the official chronology of American recessions, dates the contraction that began in October of eighteen seventy-three as lasting until March of eighteen seventy-nine.

Sixty-five months. Five and a half years.

It is the longest economic contraction in the bureau's entire record. Longer than the Great Depression's fall from nineteen twenty-nine to nineteen thirty-three, which lasted forty-three months. In Europe, the era of slow growth and falling prices that began in those years became known as the Long Depression.

[PAUSE]

And who paid for it?

Not, for the most part, the men who had built the bubble. Some of them were ruined, it is true. But the lasting damage fell on the people who worked.

In New York, by some accounts, a quarter of workers were unemployed. Across the country, employers cut wages again and again. And in the summer of eighteen seventy-seven, four years into the depression, the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad cut its workers' pay for the third time in a single year. The railroad's president cut wages by ten percent and, as the record shows, raised the dividend to shareholders by the same proportion.

The workers in Martinsburg, West Virginia, refused to let the trains move.

[BUILD]

What began in Martinsburg spread along the rails like fire along a fuse. To Baltimore. To Pittsburgh. To Buffalo and Chicago and St. Louis. It became known as the Great Railroad Strike of eighteen seventy-seven, and it lasted fifty-two days.

The governors called out the militias. When the militias would not or could not stop it, President Rutherford Hayes sent federal troops. In Baltimore, militia fire killed ten people. In Pittsburgh, National Guardsmen fired into a crowd, and the city erupted; more than a hundred locomotives and more than twelve hundred railroad cars were burned. Across the country, somewhere between one hundred and twenty-six and one hundred and sixty people were killed.

That is what an economic collapse looks like when the costs are pushed onto the people least able to bear them. It does not stay economic. It becomes social. It becomes political. It becomes, sometimes, violent.

[PAUSE]

Now I want to be careful. I am not predicting troops in the streets. I am not drawing a straight line from eighteen seventy-seven to today. History does not repeat like a recording.

But I want you to see the shape of the story, because the shape is what repeats.

A transformative technology, real and genuine, attracts a flood of investment on the promise of future returns. The investment runs ahead of the returns. The financing becomes fragile. And when it breaks, the burden lands not on the technology, which survives, and not mostly on the financiers, who are diversified, but on the workers, whose wages are cut and whose jobs vanish, in an economy that has no safety net to catch them.

[SLOW]

Does any of that sound familiar?

Seven hundred billion dollars in capital spending this year. More than a trillion projected for next year. Much of it financed by the cash flows of enormously profitable companies, which is a real difference from Jay Cooke's bonds, and I grant it. But every dollar of it is a bet on revenue that has not yet fully arrived. And the longer the spending outruns the revenue, the more of it will have to be financed with borrowing, by the companies themselves or by the partners building data centers on their behalf.

The railroad was real. The railroad was the future. The railroad still bankrupted a generation.

AI is real. AI is the future. And I ask you: what in our current arrangements guarantees that it will not do the same?

[PAUSE]

There is one more lesson from eighteen seventy-three, and it is the most important. In the end, the railroads were built. The tracks were laid. America became an industrial giant. Measured over fifty years, the railroad was an extraordinary success.

But the workers of eighteen seventy-seven did not live over fifty years. They lived in eighteen seventy-seven. They had children who needed to eat that year. The long-run success of the railroad was no comfort to the brakeman whose wages were cut three times while the dividend went up.

When someone tells you that AI will be wonderful in the long run, remember the brakemen of Martinsburg. They were living in someone else's long run. And it nearly destroyed them.

## IX. Nineteen Twenty-Nine

Let me take you forward half a century, to the most famous economic catastrophe in American history. And let me tell it not as a story of a stock market crash, which is how we usually remember it, but as a story of a technological miracle with an unequal heart.

[SLOW]

The nineteen twenties were the decade when modern life arrived.

Electricity spread across the country. American electricity production nearly quadrupled. Factories that had run on steam and coal rewired themselves around electric motors, and productivity leapt. The assembly line, perfected by Ford, turned the automobile from a rich man's toy into a working man's tool. Ford alone sold about fifteen million Model Ts. By the end of the decade, Americans had just under twenty-seven million registered motor vehicles.

Radio arrived. The refrigerator arrived. The vacuum cleaner and the washing machine began to arrive. It was, by any measure, one of the great technological transformations in human history.

[PAUSE]

And it was a K.

While the cities boomed, the countryside did not. Farming, which still employed a huge share of Americans, stagnated through the decade. So did coal mining. The new machines, the tractors and the combines, were making farms more productive, which meant more crops, which meant lower prices, which meant that farmers who had borrowed to buy land and equipment during the good years found they could not pay it back.

The prosperity of the twenties was real. But it was not shared. The gains flowed upward and toward the cities, while large parts of the country were already in quiet depression before the crash.

[SLOW]

Then came October of nineteen twenty-nine.

You know the story of Black Tuesday. Share prices collapsed. Over the following years, stocks fell by close to ninety percent from their peak. And what followed was not a correction. It was a catastrophe that spread from Wall Street to every main street in America.

Banks failed. More than nine thousand of them had closed by nineteen thirty-three. There was no deposit insurance then. When a bank failed, the savings of the families who trusted it simply disappeared.

The price of corn, which had been a dollar forty-three a bushel in nineteen twenty-nine, fell to thirty-one cents in nineteen thirty-two. Unemployment, which the government did not even measure at the time, reached by the best later estimate about twenty-five percent in nineteen thirty-three. One in four. Families lived in shantytowns of cardboard and scrap metal that people bitterly named Hoovervilles, after the president who kept telling them that prosperity was just around the corner.

[LET IT LAND]

Why did it happen? Economists have argued about that for nearly a century, and I will not pretend to settle the argument tonight. The Federal Reserve made terrible mistakes and let the money supply collapse. The gold standard tied the hands of governments. Tariffs choked world trade. All of these are true.

But underneath all of them was the mechanism Irving Fisher identified. Debt. A decade of borrowing on the assumption that the good times would continue, followed by a shock, followed by a rush to pay down debts all at once, followed by falling prices that made the debts heavier still.

The more the debtors paid, the more they owed.

[PAUSE]

And there is one more element of nineteen twenty-nine that matters for us. The demand side.

The factories of the twenties could produce more than ever. But could Americans buy it all? Much of the decade's consumer boom was financed by a new invention: buying on credit. Installment plans for cars, radios, furniture. A dollar down and a dollar a week.

It sounds a lot like buy now, pay later, does it not?

When incomes are not rising fast enough to buy what the economy can produce, credit can fill the gap for a while. Then the credit runs out. Then the demand collapses. And then the factories that were so magnificently productive find that their production has no buyers.

[BUILD]

So here is the lesson of nineteen twenty-nine, and I want to state it as plainly as I can.

A technological boom can make an economy more productive and more fragile at the same time. More productive, because the machines are real and they work. More fragile, because the gains are concentrated, the spending is propped up with debt, and the broad mass of people do not earn enough to buy what the machines produce.

That is not a description of nineteen twenty-nine alone. It is a description of the risk before us now.

Productivity up. Labor's share at its lowest in nearly eighty years. Spending concentrated at the top. The bottom propped up with credit cards and installment plans. And a technology arriving that promises to make production even cheaper while taking even more wages out of the hands of the people who would buy what is produced.

[SLOW]

Herbert Hoover was not a stupid man. He was one of the most capable administrators of his generation. He believed, sincerely, that the economy would correct itself, that the fundamentals were sound, that government intervention would only make things worse.

He was wrong. And because he was wrong, and because those around him were wrong, millions of families lost everything before Washington finally acted.

I raise this not to mock him. I raise it because I hear the same confidence today. The fundamentals are sound. The market will adjust. The workers will find new jobs. Government should stay out of the way.

Maybe. But that confidence was also the confidence of nineteen twenty-nine. And I would rather be accused of alarm before a depression than of complacency after one.

[PAUSE]

In the end, America recovered from the Great Depression. It recovered through enormous public action: the New Deal, deposit insurance, Social Security, unemployment insurance, the right to organize, and finally the vast mobilization of the Second World War. The country that emerged was more prosperous, and far more equal, than the one that went in.

But it took more than a decade. It took a war. And it was built on institutions that we were forced to invent in the middle of catastrophe, rather than before it.

We have the chance, tonight, to do what that generation could not. To build the institutions before the catastrophe instead of after it. Whether we take that chance is up to us.

## X. The Stockingers and the Long Pause

Now I want to go back further still, to the very beginning of the industrial age. Because there is a word that will be thrown at anyone who gives a speech like this one, and I want to deal with it directly.

The word is Luddite.

[SLOW]

You have heard it used as an insult. A Luddite is someone who hates technology. Someone who wants to smash the machines and go back to the past. Someone who simply does not understand progress.

That is not who the Luddites were.

On the eleventh of March, eighteen eleven, in the village of Arnold in Nottinghamshire, England, a group of men broke into workshops and smashed the knitting frames inside. They were stockingers and weavers. Many of them were skilled machine operators themselves. They did not hate machines. They worked machines. Machines were their livelihood.

What they objected to was something more specific. They targeted employers who were using new machinery and new practices to undercut the established standards of their trade: to cut wages, to hire unapprenticed workers at lower pay, to produce cheaper and shoddier goods that undermined the whole craft. Their complaint was not with the machine. It was with the bargain.

[PAUSE]

They signed their threatening letters in the name of a mythical figure, General Ned Ludd. Over the next several years, the movement spread across the Midlands, into Yorkshire and Lancashire. They drilled at night on the moors. They raided mills. They broke frames.

And the British government's response tells you everything about whose side the state was on.

It deployed about twelve thousand troops to the disturbed districts. The historian Eric Hobsbawm noted that this was a larger force than the army Wellington had taken into Portugal to fight Napoleon in eighteen oh eight.

And in eighteen twelve, Parliament passed a law making the breaking of machines a crime punishable by death.

[LET IT LAND]

Not every voice in Parliament agreed. On the twenty-seventh of February, eighteen twelve, a young lord rose in the House of Lords to make his first speech. It was the poet Lord Byron. He had seen the conditions in Nottinghamshire with his own eyes, and he condemned the poverty that drove these men to desperation. He asked his fellow lords how they could rush to hang starving workers while doing nothing about their starvation.

He lost. The law passed.

In January of eighteen thirteen, at York, more than sixty men were put on trial after an attack on a mill. About thirty were acquitted. Others were hanged or transported to the penal colonies. The movement was broken.

[SLOW]

So history remembers the Luddites as fools who fought progress and lost. And in one sense they did lose. The machines won. The textile industry was transformed. Britain became the workshop of the world.

But here is what the insult leaves out. Were the Luddites wrong about what the machines would do to their lives?

The economic historian Robert Allen has studied the wages of British workers through the Industrial Revolution. And he identified a period he named after Friedrich Engels, who wrote about the condition of the English working class in the eighteen forties. Allen called it Engels' pause.

From roughly seventeen ninety to eighteen forty, output per person in Britain grew rapidly. The machines were working. The economy was growing. And the wages of ordinary workers stagnated.

[PAUSE]

Fifty years.

For half a century, the greatest technological revolution in human history to that point delivered its gains almost entirely to the owners of capital. Labor's share of national income fell, from about half in eighteen oh one to about forty-five percent by the mid-eighteen forties. Profits rose. And the workers who powered the revolution lived in the conditions Engels described: crowded, filthy, short-lived.

Only after about eighteen forty did wages begin to rise alongside output. And when they did, it was not because the market spontaneously decided to share. It was in an era of new laws limiting working hours, of trade unions slowly gaining ground, of reforms that the workers fought for over decades.

I should tell you that some economic historians dispute Allen's account. They argue that wages grew faster than his figures show. That debate is real, and I would not want you to think the evidence is unanimous. But even the skeptics do not claim that the gains of early industrialization flowed quickly or evenly to those whose work was displaced.

[BUILD]

So when someone calls you a Luddite for worrying about AI, here is what you should say.

The Luddites were right that the machines would make their bosses rich.

The Luddites were right that the machines would cut their wages.

The Luddites were right that the government would side with the owners against them.

And the Luddites were right that, for their lifetimes and their children's lifetimes, the gains of the new technology would not come to them.

Where they were wrong was in their method. Breaking the machines did not work. It could not work. You cannot un-invent a knitting frame, any more than you can un-invent a language model.

[SLOW]

The lesson of the Luddites is not that we should smash the machines. It is that a technology does not decide by itself who benefits from it. The rules decide. The laws decide. The balance of power between those who own the machines and those who work beside them decides.

In eighteen twelve, the rules said that breaking a machine was a hanging offense, and starving a worker was nobody's business. Those were choices. They were made by people. And they condemned a generation to fifty years of pause.

We are making those choices again, right now. Every time a legislature debates what to tax and what to subsidize. Every time a regulator decides what companies owe the people they displace. Every time we decide whether the safety net is a trampoline or a trapdoor.

[PAUSE]

Engels' pause lasted fifty years. I ask you: how long will ours last? And how many young people, like that canary I described at the start, will spend their entire working lives inside it?

## XI. Youngstown, and the Places After

Now let me bring the story closer to home. Not a century ago, not two centuries ago, but within the living memory of many people in this room.

[SLOW]

On Monday, the nineteenth of September, nineteen seventy-seven, the Youngstown Sheet and Tube Company announced that it was closing its Campbell Works in the Mahoning Valley of Ohio. Five thousand workers were put out of work, more or less overnight.

People in Youngstown still call it Black Monday.

It was not the end. It was the beginning. Two years later, the company's Brier Hill Works closed as well, part of a wave of mill closings that devastated the whole regional economy. The valley had been one of the great steelmaking centers of the world. Within a few years, the furnaces were cold.

[PAUSE]

Think about what a steel mill was to a town like Youngstown. It was not just an employer. It was the reason the town existed. The mill paid the steelworkers. The steelworkers paid the grocers, the barbers, the bar owners, the car dealers, the churches. Their property taxes paid the teachers and the police. Their union dues built the halls where weddings were held. Their pensions were going to carry them through old age.

When the mill closed, all of that went with it. Not in one day, but over years. The stores on main street closed one by one. The young people left for other states. The tax base shrank, so the schools got worse, so more young people left. The houses lost value, so the families who stayed were trapped, unable to sell for enough to move somewhere with jobs.

That is what economists mean by regional hollowing. It is not a statistic. It is a town slowly emptying out.

[SLOW]

And Youngstown was not alone. Let me give you the national numbers, from the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

In June of nineteen seventy-nine, the United States employed nineteen million, five hundred and fifty-three thousand people in manufacturing. That was the all-time peak.

By January of two thousand, the number had drifted down to about seventeen point three million. Then came the great collapse. By January of twenty ten, manufacturing employment had fallen to about eleven point four million. Nearly six million manufacturing jobs lost in a single decade.

Today, after years of effort and rhetoric about bringing manufacturing back, the number stands at about twelve point six five million.

[LET IT LAND]

Part of that collapse came from automation, as factories did more with fewer workers. And part of it came from trade. In two thousand one, China joined the World Trade Organization, and a flood of imports followed. Economists including David Autor, David Dorn and Gordon Hanson studied what they called the China shock. One prominent estimate, by Daron Acemoglu and colleagues, put the number of American jobs lost to it at between two and two point four million.

But the most important thing the China shock researchers found was not the national number. It was where the losses landed and how long they stayed.

The damage was concentrated in particular places: furniture towns in North Carolina, textile towns in the South, factory towns across the Midwest. And in those places, the researchers found higher unemployment, lower participation in the workforce, and lower wages, not for a year or two, but for many years after.

The economic models had said that displaced workers would move to new jobs in new places. Many did not. They could not. Their houses were worth too little to sell. Their families were rooted there. Their skills did not match the jobs that existed elsewhere. They stayed, and they waited, and the jobs did not come back.

[PAUSE]

And then something even worse began to happen.

In twenty fifteen, the economists Anne Case and Angus Deaton published a finding that shocked the world. Death rates for middle-aged white Americans without college degrees, which had been falling for decades, had started to rise. Unlike any comparable group in other rich countries, unlike Black and Hispanic Americans whose death rates were still falling at the time.

The causes were what Case and Deaton called deaths of despair: drug overdoses, suicide, and alcoholic liver disease. In nineteen ninety-five, about sixty-five thousand Americans died of these causes. By twenty eighteen, about one hundred and fifty-eight thousand did.

The life expectancy of Americans without a college degree peaked around twenty ten and has declined since.

[SLOW]

Case and Deaton were careful. They did not say the loss of factory jobs alone caused these deaths. They pointed to many things: stagnant wages, the decline of unions, social isolation, the predatory marketing of opioids. And they argued that underneath the economics was something deeper, a loss of meaning, of status, of the structure that good work gives a life.

But that is precisely my point. Work is not only income. It is identity. It is community. It is the reason to get up in the morning. When you take it away from whole regions and offer nothing in its place, people do not simply retrain and relocate as the models assume. Too many of them fall apart. And their communities fall apart with them.

[BUILD]

Now here is my warning, and I want you to hear it clearly.

Deindustrialization hit places that made things. Steel towns. Furniture towns. Textile towns. It hit the blue-collar worker.

The AI transition, if it unfolds the way its builders expect, will hit places that process things. Information. Claims. Transactions. Calls. Documents. It will hit the white-collar and pink-collar worker in the office park, the call center, the regional back office, the insurance hub, the medical billing firm, the outsourced service center.

And those places exist all across America. Many mid-sized cities rebuilt themselves after the factories left precisely by attracting this kind of work. The call center in the old mill building. The claims processing office in the suburban business park. The customer service operation that brought five hundred jobs to a town that had lost its plant.

[PAUSE]

That is the work that the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported is already shrinking in insurance. That is the work that the Stanford researchers found is already closing to the young. That is the work that the builders of these systems say they will automate.

I cannot tell you exactly which towns will be hit first. No one can. But I can tell you the pattern, because we have lived it. The jobs will go. The local businesses that depended on those paychecks will shrink. The house prices will soften. The young will leave. The tax base will thin. And the people who stay will face the same choices the people of Youngstown faced: move to a place they cannot afford, or stay in a place that cannot afford them.

[SLOW]

We told the steelworkers they would be retrained. We told the furniture workers they would find new jobs in the new economy. We told them the market would adjust.

Some of them did. Many of them did not. And a generation later, their children are dying younger than their grandparents did.

We cannot tell the claims processors and the call center workers and the junior analysts the same comforting things and expect them to believe us. We have used up our credibility. If we want them to believe that this transition will be different, we will have to make it different. With money. With law. With institutions. Not with speeches.

[PAUSE]

And let me say one more thing about the places after. When a region is hollowed out, it does not only suffer economically. It suffers politically. People who feel abandoned by the system stop trusting the system. They turn to anyone who promises to burn it down. Every lasting democracy depends on a broad population that believes it has a stake in the future.

Youngstown taught us what happens when a town loses its stake. The AI transition threatens to teach that lesson to the whole country at once.

## XII. The Strongest Case Against Me

Now I have made my case. And I promised you at the start that I would give you the strongest argument against it, not a straw man, but the real thing, the argument that the most thoughtful economists in the world would make if they were standing here instead of me.

So here it is. I am going to make it as strongly as I can.

[SLOW]

The argument goes like this.

We have heard this before. Every generation for two hundred years has been told that the machines will take all the jobs, and every generation has been wrong.

The Luddites feared the knitting frame. Yet within a few generations the mills of Britain employed vast numbers of people, and wages eventually rose. In the nineteen thirties, the great economist John Maynard Keynes popularized the phrase "technological unemployment," and even he called it a temporary phase of maladjustment. In the nineteen sixties, Americans worried that automation would put millions out of work. Instead came one of the greatest booms in history.

In nineteen hundred, a large share of American workers worked on farms. Today a tiny share do. If you had told a farmer in nineteen hundred that almost all farm jobs would disappear, he would have predicted mass unemployment. Instead, his grandchildren became engineers and nurses and programmers and graphic designers, doing jobs he could not have imagined.

[PAUSE]

And that is not just a story. It is in the data. The economist David Autor and his colleagues studied the history of new work in America. They found that the majority of Americans today are employed in job specialties that did not exist in nineteen forty. Most of today's work was invented after nineteen forty.

The economists have a name for the fear I have been expressing. They call it the lump of labor fallacy. The mistaken belief that there is a fixed amount of work in the world, so that if a machine does some of it, there is less left for people. In reality, they say, there is no fixed amount. When technology makes things cheaper, people buy more of them, and new demands appear, and new jobs with them.

When a company saves money with AI, they argue, it lowers prices, or invests in new products, or pays its shareholders, who spend or invest. The money does not disappear. It flows somewhere, and where it flows, it creates jobs. Economists call these compensation effects.

[SLOW]

And look at the evidence today, they would say. You have been talking for an hour about a coming depression. But unemployment is four point two percent. That is low by historical standards. The economy grew in the second quarter of this year. Consumer spending is holding up. The stock market is near record levels.

The layoff announcements you cited include companies that are AI-washing, blaming the machine for ordinary cost cuts. In August, the AI share of announced cuts actually fell.

And look at the workers themselves. The same Federal Reserve survey you quoted found that one in four workers used generative AI on the job in the past month, that most users said it saved them time, and that users were more likely to expect AI to improve their careers than to replace them.

Even the K-shape, they would say, may be overstated, as the economists at Goldman Sachs have argued.

[PAUSE]

So, the argument concludes, you are a Luddite after all. A well-read one, but a Luddite. You are mistaking the pain of transition for the end of work. Be patient. The new jobs will come. They always have.

[LET IT LAND]

That is a serious argument. It is made by serious people. And parts of it are true. Let me tell you which parts, and then let me tell you why I still believe I am right.

[SLOW]

First, they are right that I cannot prove a depression is coming. No one can prove the future. Unemployment is four point two percent today. I have said so tonight, and I say so again. If I am wrong, I will be glad to be wrong.

Second, they are right that, over the very long run, technology has always created new work. I believe that. I believe it will probably be true again. My grandchildren will likely do jobs I cannot imagine.

But now let me answer.

[BUILD]

My first answer is the one I have been building all night: the long run is not where people live.

Keynes, the same Keynes who called technological unemployment temporary, also wrote one of the most famous sentences in economics: in the long run we are all dead. He meant that it is no comfort to tell people in a storm that the sea will be calm once the storm is over.

The Luddites lived through Engels' pause. Fifty years. The brakemen of eighteen seventy-seven lived through the longest contraction in American history. The families of nineteen thirty-three lived through a decade of depression. The steelworkers of Youngstown lived through a hollowing that their grandchildren are still living through.

In every one of those cases, the optimists were right about the long run. And in every one of those cases, the people who lived through the transition paid a terrible price, a price that could have been reduced if the society around them had acted in time.

My argument is not that work will end forever. My argument is that a depression is a short-run event, measured in years and decades, and that the short run is where human beings actually live.

[PAUSE]

My second answer is about speed.

When the tractor displaced the farm worker, it did so over decades. A farmer's son saw the writing on the wall and went to the city to work in a factory. The transition happened across generations. Children did not do the jobs their fathers did; they did the new jobs instead. That is how an economy absorbs change gracefully: not by retraining fifty-year-olds, but by letting the young choose different paths.

But look at where AI is hitting first. Not the fifty-year-olds. The twenty-two-year-olds. The very group that is supposed to absorb change by choosing new paths is having those paths closed before they begin. If the entry-level jobs in the new occupations are also being automated, where do the young go?

And look at the money. Seven hundred billion dollars in a single year. A trillion projected the next. This is not a technology diffusing slowly over decades. It is being installed as fast as the chip factories and power plants can be built.

[SLOW]

My third answer is about what the economists' own research is telling us.

Remember David Autor's study, the one that found most of today's work did not exist in nineteen forty? It found something else too, and it is the part that is rarely quoted. Autor and his colleagues distinguished between innovations that augment workers, making them more productive and creating new tasks for them, and innovations that automate workers, replacing them. They found that the demand-destroying effects of automation have intensified over the last four decades, while the demand-creating effects of augmentation have not.

In other words, the engine that created new work in the twentieth century has been weakening for forty years, while the engine that destroys old work has been getting stronger. That is not my opinion. That is what the research behind the optimists' favorite statistic found.

And the Stanford study of young workers found the same pattern in AI. Where AI augments workers, employment has held up. Where it automates them, employment has fallen. The question is which kind of AI our companies are buying. And with seven hundred billion dollars on the table, you can be sure that most of them are buying it to need fewer people.

[PAUSE]

My fourth answer is about the compensation effects. The economists say the money saved by automation does not disappear; it flows somewhere and creates jobs. That is true. But where does it flow?

It flows to shareholders. And as I showed you, a dollar in the hands of a wealthy shareholder is far less likely to be spent than a dollar in the hands of a working family. It flows into stock buybacks and asset prices and more data centers. It flows, increasingly, to the top of the K.

Compensation effects work when the gains are broadly shared. They work poorly when the gains are concentrated. And labor's share of income is at its lowest point in nearly eighty years of records. The compensation is happening. It is just compensating the wrong people.

[BUILD]

My fifth and final answer is the simplest.

The optimists say: unemployment is low, the economy is growing, there is no crisis yet. They are right. There is no crisis yet.

But that is exactly when you prepare. You do not install sprinklers after the fire has started. You do not build the levee after the flood. Every one of the institutions that saved Americans in later downturns, deposit insurance, unemployment insurance, Social Security, was invented in the middle of a catastrophe, at enormous cost, because no one would build it before.

The argument that we should wait until the depression arrives before preparing for it is not an argument about economics. It is an argument for repeating the mistakes of eighteen seventy-three and nineteen twenty-nine.

[SLOW]

So let me grant the optimists everything I can. Perhaps new jobs will come. Perhaps the transition will be gentler than I fear. Perhaps the AI bet will pay off in a way that lifts everyone.

If they are right, then the measures I am about to propose will cost us relatively little, and they will make a good transition better.

If I am right, those measures will be the difference between a hard decade and a lost generation.

That is an easy bet to make. Insure against the catastrophe. If it never comes, you have lost only the premium. If it does come, you have saved the house.

## XIII. What Must Be Done

Demosthenes did not end his speech with a lament. He ended it with a program. Send envoys. Raise money. Prepare the fleet. Stop waiting for someone else to save the city.

So let me end with a program too. I want to be clear that what follows is my argument, my judgment of what the evidence calls for. Reasonable people will disagree with parts of it. But I would rather be debated on a plan than applauded for a warning.

[SLOW]

First: build the automatic stabilizers now, before the downturn.

Our unemployment insurance system was designed for a world of temporary layoffs, where a worker loses a job for a few months and then returns to the same kind of work. It was not designed for a world where whole categories of work disappear. In most states, benefits replace only a fraction of lost wages and run out after a few months.

We should modernize it. Make benefits stronger and longer when national unemployment rises, automatically, without waiting for Congress to argue about it in the middle of a crisis. Extend coverage to the contract and gig workers who are now left out. And design it so that it kicks in on a trigger, the moment the data turns, not months later when the damage is done.

[PAUSE]

Second: wage insurance for displaced workers.

When a worker loses a good job to automation and takes a new job at lower pay, we should make up part of the difference for a period of years. This rewards people for going back to work rather than penalizing them, and it keeps money flowing into communities rather than draining out of them. It treats the displaced worker not as a failure, but as someone who absorbed a cost on behalf of the whole society's progress, which is exactly what she did.

[SLOW]

Third: give working people a stake in the machines themselves.

If the great gains of the coming decades will flow to the owners of capital, then the answer is not to stop the gains. It is to broaden the ownership. There are many ways to do this, and we should debate them. Public wealth funds that hold a share of the companies building and deploying AI and pay dividends to every citizen. Stronger incentives for employee ownership. Retirement accounts seeded for every child at birth.

The principle is simple. If the robots are going to do the work, then everyone should own a piece of the robots. Otherwise, we are building an economy in which a small number of people own everything and everyone else owns nothing but their labor, at the very moment that labor is losing its value.

[PAUSE]

Fourth: rebalance the tax code so that it stops favoring machines over people.

Today, when a company hires a worker, it pays payroll taxes on that worker's wages. When it buys a machine to replace that worker, it can often deduct the cost of the machine on generous terms. We have built a tax system that, in effect, subsidizes the replacement of people. That made less difference when the machines were looms and lathes. It makes an enormous difference when the machine can do the work of an office.

We should look honestly at shifting the burden. Lighten the taxes on work, especially for the low-paid. Make sure that the profits generated by automation are taxed fairly, and that the gains on capital are not taxed at a fraction of the rate of the wages of the person who used to do the job.

[SLOW]

Fifth: fix the house.

We cannot protect working families from a demand depression while their single largest expense keeps climbing faster than their pay. We need to build. Millions more homes, of every kind, in the places where the jobs are. That means changing the local rules that make it illegal to build modest homes and apartments in much of America. It means public investment in housing that working people can afford.

And it means preparing now for what happens if AI layoffs collide with mortgage payments. We learned in two thousand eight how slowly and grudgingly our system modified loans for families in trouble. We should have a plan ready this time, to keep families in their homes during a period of mass displacement, rather than letting foreclosures spread from street to street.

[PAUSE]

Sixth: invest in the places, not just the people.

When the factories closed, we offered workers retraining vouchers and told them to move. It was not enough. The record of those programs was, in my judgment, disappointing, because it treated a community crisis as a series of individual problems.

This time, we should invest in the regions that are hit, directly and early. Public works. Infrastructure. Care for the young and the old, which is work that people need and machines do poorly. Support for the hospitals and schools and local businesses that hold a community together. A town that keeps its anchor institutions can survive the loss of an employer. A town that loses them does not.

[SLOW]

Seventh: ask the companies that profit most to share the cost of the transition.

The companies deploying AI are not villains. They are doing what the market rewards. But the market does not count the cost of a hollowed-out town or a generation locked out of work. Those costs fall on the public.

We should ask the firms that benefit most to contribute to the institutions that cushion the people they displace, through taxes, through transition funds, through obligations to give notice and support when they replace large numbers of workers. That is not punishment. It is the same principle as asking a factory to clean up its pollution. If your business model imposes costs on others, you should help pay them.

[PAUSE]

Eighth: protect the first rung.

The clearest early warning we have is that the entry-level jobs are vanishing first. If the young cannot get a start, everything else in this speech will follow: the stalled careers, the delayed homes, the lost wealth, the hollow towns.

So we should treat the first job as a public priority. Support apprenticeships and paid training positions in fields that are growing. Encourage companies to keep hiring and training the young even when a machine could do the first draft, because the person who writes the first draft today is the person who will supervise the machine tomorrow. A company that stops hiring juniors will one day find it has no seniors.

Ninth, and finally: measure what is happening, honestly and in public.

Think about where the clearest early evidence in this speech came from. The study of young workers was built on records from a private payroll company. The layoff counts come from a private outplacement firm, based on what companies choose to announce. The spending split between rich and poor comes from a private analytics firm and a private bank.

Our public statistical agencies are among the best in the world. But they were not built to answer the question in front of us: which jobs are being replaced by machines, where, and how fast. We should ask them to answer it. Fund the Bureau of Labor Statistics and the Census Bureau to track AI adoption and displacement by occupation, by industry and by region, every quarter. Require large employers to report when they replace significant numbers of workers with automated systems, the way they already must give notice of mass layoffs.

[PAUSE]

You cannot steer by a map you refuse to draw. If the optimists are right, good data will prove it, and I will be the first to say so. If I am right, good data will tell us where to send help before the spiral turns, not after.

And there is a deeper reason for this. A democracy cannot make wise choices about a transformation it cannot see. Right now, the people who know the most about how fast human work is being replaced are the companies doing the replacing. That knowledge should belong to the public whose livelihoods are at stake.

[BUILD]

None of this is radical. Every one of these ideas has been tried somewhere, in some form, by governments of the left and the right. What would be radical is to do nothing. To watch the canaries fall silent one by one and tell ourselves it is a special case. To wait for the depression before building the defenses against it.

Demosthenes told his city that the danger was not that it lacked resources. Athens had ships and money and men. The danger was that it would not use them until it was too late.

We are the richest nation in the history of the world. We have the tools. We have the knowledge. We have the warnings, from the economists, from the data, from the very people building these machines.

What we lack is the will to act before we are forced to.

## XIV. The Years the Locust Has Eaten

[SLOW]

There is a line in the Book of Joel, in the old King James translation, where the Lord promises to restore to his people the years that the locust hath eaten.

The years that the locust has eaten. Years of plenty in the fields that were devoured before the harvest could be gathered. Years that could have been, and were not.

Every great economic catastrophe in our history has left behind a generation of eaten years.

[PAUSE]

The brakemen of eighteen seventy-seven, whose wages were cut three times while the dividends went up. Their years were eaten.

The farmers of nineteen thirty-two, watching corn fall to thirty-one cents a bushel. Their years were eaten.

The stockingers of Nottinghamshire, hanged at York, and their children who lived through half a century of pause. Their years were eaten.

The steelworkers of Youngstown, who walked out of the Campbell Works on Black Monday and never walked back in. Their years were eaten, and their children's years, and too many of their grandchildren's lives.

[BUILD]

And now I look at that young woman I told you about at the beginning. The one who did everything right. The one who took the loans and learned the skills and sent out the hundred applications, and found the bottom rung sawed off.

I look at her, and I ask: will her years be eaten too?

Will she rent at forty and at fifty because she never got the start that would have let her save?

Will she watch the town she grew up in empty out the way Youngstown emptied out?

Will she carry her student loans and her credit card balance into a downturn that nobody prepared for, and learn, as Irving Fisher learned, that the more you pay, the more you owe?

[PAUSE]

She does not have to.

That is the whole point of everything I have said tonight. None of this is fate. The machines do not decide who benefits from them. We do. The laws decide. The tax code decides. The safety net decides. The balance of power between those who own and those who work decides.

In eighteen twelve, the British Parliament decided that breaking a machine was a hanging offense and that a starving worker was nobody's concern. That was a choice.

In nineteen twenty-nine, the leaders of this country decided to wait for the economy to heal itself. That was a choice.

In the decades after Youngstown, we decided that displaced workers could fend for themselves with a voucher and a bus ticket. That was a choice.

[SLOW]

And now it is our turn to choose.

We can choose to treat this technology as what it is: one of the most extraordinary tools human beings have ever made, capable of producing abundance beyond anything our ancestors dreamed of. Or we can let that abundance pool at the top while the foundations of the house give way beneath it.

We can choose to build the stabilizers, the insurance, the ownership, the housing, the investment in places, before the downturn. Or we can wait, as we always have, and build them afterward from the rubble, at ten times the cost.

We can choose to give the young their first rung. Or we can tell them, as we told the steelworkers, that the market will sort it out.

[BUILD]

I am not asking you to fear the future. I am asking you to claim it.

I am not asking you to smash the machines. I am asking you to rewrite the bargain.

I am not asking you to believe that a depression is certain. I am asking you to make sure that it is impossible.

[PAUSE]

Demosthenes' Athens did not listen in time. Three years after the Third Philippic, its army was defeated by Philip of Macedon at Chaeronea, and the independence of the city was lost.

We do not have to end that way. We have the warnings in our hands tonight. The canaries are singing more softly every month. The ledger is heavy. The house is out of reach. The K is pulling apart. And the money, seven hundred billion dollars of it this year, is moving faster than our institutions have ever moved.

[SLOW]

So let this be the generation that acted before the fire instead of after it.

Let this be the generation that heard the alarm and did not call it a false one.

Let this be the generation that learned from the brakemen and the stockingers and the steelworkers, and said: never again will the costs of progress fall on the people least able to bear them.

And let it be said of us, when our grandchildren look back on these years, that the machines came, and the work changed, and the country grew richer than it had ever been, and that no one's years were eaten.

[LET IT LAND]

Thank you.
