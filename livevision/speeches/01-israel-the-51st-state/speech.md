# Make It Official: Israel, the Fifty-First State

## I. A Modest Proposal

Friends, neighbors, fellow taxpayers. Thank you for coming out tonight. I know it is a weeknight, I know gas is expensive, and I know some of you came only because the flyer promised a solution. I intend to deliver one.

I want to begin with a household problem, because every great question of state is a household problem wearing a suit.

Imagine a bill that comes to your house every year. It comes on time. It comes in a lump sum, at the start of the year, before almost anyone else gets paid. You pay it. Your parents paid it. If you are young, your children will pay it.

Now imagine that the bill is for a house you do not live in, in a neighborhood you cannot vote in, governed by a city council that has never once asked your opinion. And imagine that when the neighbors next door to that house start a fight, it is somehow your sons and daughters who are sent over the fence.

[PAUSE]

Most of us would call that a bad arrangement. A few of us would call it a scam. I am here tonight to call it something more hopeful. I am here to call it a merger that nobody has had the courage to finalize.

So let me state my proposal plainly, so that no one leaves confused and no one can say I hid it in the fine print.

[SLOW]

I propose that the United States of America formally admit the State of Israel as the fifty-first state of the Union.

[LET IT LAND]

Now, I can see some of you reaching for your coats. Please, sit down. I have not lost my mind. I have only lost my patience, and I promise you that before the evening is out, the proposal will sound less like madness and more like bookkeeping.

Because here is the thing. We are already most of the way there. We fund it. We arm it. We defend it. Our Congress writes laws about its military edge, our Pentagon stores weapons on its soil, and our bombers have flown over its enemies. Our pilots and sailors have died in the wars it fights. Its prime minister has addressed our Congress more times than Winston Churchill did.

The only things we have not done are the things that would give us any say. We have not given ourselves a vote on its decisions. We have not given its people a vote on ours. We have not given the arrangement a name, an audit, or a set of rules that the rest of the country has to live by.

We have taken on all of the obligations of ownership and none of the privileges. We have the mortgage and not the deed.

I am a reasonable person. I have looked at this arrangement from every side, and I have concluded that there are only two honest ways out of it. Either we make it official, or we make it conditional. Tonight I am going to argue, with a perfectly straight face, for the first. And by the end, I suspect you will understand why I would happily settle for the second.

Let me tell you what this speech is not. It is not about any people's worth, any faith, or any ancestry. I will not traffic in old slanders about secret control or divided loyalty, and if you came to hear those, you are at the wrong meeting and you may leave by the side door. The people of Israel are not the problem tonight. Neither are the people of America. The problem is an arrangement: a policy, a budget, a set of habits in Washington that nobody voted to create and nobody is allowed to examine.

This speech is about money, war, law and accountability. It is about whether a republic should spend its treasure and risk its children on decisions it does not make. And it is about whether the people who sign the checks should ever be permitted to read the statement.

Jonathan Swift once suggested, in perfect calm, that the poor of Ireland might solve their problems by selling their babies to the rich as food. He did not mean it. He meant that the policies of his day were already treating people that way, and that the only thing missing was the honesty to say so. His proposal was monstrous because the reality was monstrous, and he wanted his readers to feel the shock they had trained themselves not to feel.

My proposal is gentler. Nobody gets eaten. Nobody even gets annexed against their will, because, as I will explain, the annexation has already happened in every way but the paperwork. I am only asking that we write down what we are already doing, and see if we can stand to read it.

[PAUSE]

A word about how to listen to satire, since it is an old art and we have gotten out of practice. The satirist says something outrageous with a straight face, and then he builds the case for it with every real fact he can find. If the facts hold, the outrage moves. It stops being attached to his proposal and starts being attached to the reality that makes his proposal sound reasonable. That is the trick. That is the whole trick. So as I go, I want you to check my facts. Every number I give you tonight comes from a public source, our own government's where possible. If I am wrong, I want to be corrected. If I am right, then the joke is not on me.

[PAUSE]

So here is the plan for our evening together. First, we will look at the bill: what we have paid, and on what terms. Then we will look at the wars: the one we joined last year and the one we have been fighting since February. Then we will look at the new law that Congress is quietly drafting to tie our defense industries together. Then we will go back in time, to a ship called the Liberty, to a factory in a small Pennsylvania town, and to a reactor in the desert that our government still pretends it cannot see.

Then we will talk about lobbying, as a question of money and law, the way we would talk about the oil lobby or the drug lobby. Then we will read the fine print of statehood. And then I will give the strongest argument against everything I have said, and I will answer it.

By the end, I hope you will agree with at least one thing. Whatever this relationship is, it should not stay what it is now: unlimited in its costs, undefined in its terms, and unaccountable to the people who pay for it.

Let us begin, as all serious matters do, with the money.

## II. The Bill We Already Pay

The Congressional Research Service is the research arm of Congress. It is not an advocacy group. It does not hold rallies. It writes long, careful reports full of tables, and members of Congress from both parties rely on it. If you want to start an argument about money, you could not start with a more boring and more trustworthy source.

According to the Congressional Research Service, in its report updated in May of last year, the United States has provided Israel roughly one hundred and seventy-five billion dollars in bilateral assistance and missile defense funding since the end of the Second World War. That is in current dollars, the dollars of each year as they were spent.

Adjusted for inflation, measured in the dollars of twenty twenty-four, the Research Service estimates the total at about two hundred and ninety-eight billion dollars.

[LET IT LAND]

Nearly three hundred billion dollars. Israel has been the largest cumulative recipient of American foreign aid since its founding. Not one of the largest. The largest.

Of that one hundred and seventy-five billion, about one hundred and twenty-four and a half billion dollars has been military aid. About thirty-four billion has been economic aid, most of it in the decades before two thousand and eight, when the economic grants were phased out. And about sixteen billion has gone to missile defense.

Now, I want to be fair, because fairness is the only weapon a satirist cannot afford to lose. For most of those decades, Americans across both parties believed this money bought something real: a stable democratic partner in a dangerous region, a counterweight to the Soviet Union, intelligence we could not get on our own, and weapons tested in conditions we hoped our own soldiers would never face. Some of that was true. Some of it is still true. I will return to it before the night is out.

But a fair accounting is still an accounting. And the first rule of accounting is that you write down what you spend.

Here is where my modest proposal begins to look less like a joke and more like a cost-saving measure.

Consider the states we already have. Every year, Washington sends money to Wyoming, to Vermont, to Mississippi and to Maine. And every year, Wyoming and Vermont and Mississippi and Maine send people to Congress who must answer for how it is used. They file reports. They are audited. Their governors can be sued in federal court. Their citizens pay federal income tax. Their soldiers serve under the same flag and the same law.

That is the deal with a state. Money goes in, and accountability comes back out.

Now consider the deal we have. Money goes in, a great deal of it, and what comes back out is a press release.

Israel is a sovereign country, and as a sovereign country it is entitled to make its own decisions. I respect that. But the more money a sovereign country takes from you, the stranger it is that you should have no say in how it is spent, and no recourse when it is spent in ways that drag you into a war.

If you lent your brother-in-law three hundred thousand dollars over your lifetime, you might not ask for it back. You love him. But you would, at the very least, expect him to call before he set fire to the neighbor's garage. And you would definitely expect him not to send you the bill for the fire department.

[PAUSE]

Let me put that three hundred billion in a few other frames, not to inflame, but to measure.

It is money that has come from American paychecks, through a federal budget that every year argues bitterly over a few billion here and there for schools, for veterans, for highways, for research. When Congress fights over a food program, it fights in the hundreds of millions. When Congress funds this relationship, the debate is usually over in an afternoon.

That is not because Americans have carefully weighed the trade-offs and made a choice. It is because the question has been placed, by long habit, outside the realm of ordinary budget politics. It is treated as settled. And anything that is treated as settled for long enough stops being examined at all.

I am a great believer in examination. I believe the Founders were, too. They built a system in which the power of the purse sits with the House of Representatives precisely so that the people's money would be spent by the people's representatives, in the open, subject to argument.

So I ask a plain question, the sort of question a plain man might ask at a town meeting. If we are going to spend as though Israel were one of our states, why should we not govern as though it were one?

Either the money is foreign aid, in which case it should come with the conditions we attach to foreign aid. Or it is something closer to a domestic obligation, in which case it should come with the accountability we attach to domestic spending. What it should not be is neither.

Right now it is neither. And neither is the most expensive thing a government can buy.

## III. The Terms of the Account

But totals are abstractions. The real story is in the terms. And the terms of this account would make any banker blush.

The current arrangement runs on a ten-year Memorandum of Understanding signed on September fourteenth, twenty sixteen, in the final months of the Obama administration. It covers fiscal years twenty nineteen through twenty twenty-eight. Under it, the United States pledged thirty-eight billion dollars in military aid, subject to appropriation by Congress.

That breaks down to thirty-three billion dollars in grants called Foreign Military Financing, which is about three point three billion a year, plus five billion for missile defense, which is five hundred million a year.

And Congress, not content merely to appropriate, went further. In the defense authorization act for fiscal year twenty twenty-one, it wrote into law that Israel should receive "not less than" three point three billion dollars a year in Foreign Military Financing through twenty twenty-eight.

Not less than. A floor, set in statute. I would love to see the farmers of Iowa get a floor like that. I would love to see the veterans' hospitals get a floor like that.

[PAUSE]

Here is my favorite detail. Since nineteen ninety-one, Israel has received its yearly Foreign Military Financing in a lump sum, in the first month of the fiscal year. Most countries that receive American military aid draw it down over time, as they buy things. Israel gets it all up front.

And where does it go? According to the Research Service, into an interest-bearing account at the Federal Reserve Bank.

[SLOW]

Let me say that again, slowly, for the people in the back who have ever paid interest on a credit card. The United States borrows money, because the United States runs a deficit. It hands billions of dollars in a single payment to another country at the start of the year. And that money sits in an account earning interest, while American taxpayers pay interest on the debt that financed it.

I do not say that Israel has done anything improper here. It is an arrangement Congress chose. I say only that if a state government asked for its federal highway money as a lump sum in October, to be deposited in an interest-bearing account, its senators would be laughed out of the committee room. And if they kept asking, someone would open an investigation.

There is more. For years, Israel was the only recipient permitted to spend a large share of its American military aid inside its own defense industry, rather than buying American. This was called off-shore procurement. To its credit, the current agreement phases it out, ending entirely in fiscal year twenty twenty-eight. For fiscal year twenty twenty-five, the amount was still about four hundred and fifty million dollars.

And in fairness, let me add something the Research Service also reports: as of twenty twenty-four, Israel owed no outstanding sovereign debt to the United States government. Earlier in the relationship, much of the help came as loans. Those were paid or converted long ago. So nobody is defaulting on anybody. The grants are simply grants, given freely, year after year. That is precisely why the terms matter so much: when you give money away, the terms are the only leverage you will ever have.

I will give credit where it is due. The phase-out was a good idea. It is the kind of condition a serious partner should be glad to accept. I only note that it took sixty years to think of it.

How big is this compared with everyone else? According to a former American ambassador quoted by the Research Service, at the closing level of the previous agreement, Israel received roughly forty percent of the entire global Foreign Military Financing budget. Forty percent of all the military grants America gives to every country on earth, to one country of about ten million people.

At the same time, the Research Service notes, American military grants make up about fourteen percent of Israel's overall defense budget. That is a substantial share, but it also tells you something important: Israel is a wealthy country. It spent nearly nine percent of its economy on defense in twenty twenty-four. It is not a poor nation that would collapse without us. It is a prosperous one that we have chosen to subsidize.

And then there are the extras. Because the Memorandum, generous as it is, is only the baseline.

In April twenty twenty-four, eleven days after Iran launched its first direct attack on Israel, Congress passed the Israel Security Supplemental Appropriations Act. It included three and a half billion dollars in additional Foreign Military Financing, four billion dollars for missile defense, and one point two billion dollars for a laser defense system called Iron Beam. That was the first dedicated American funding for that system.

According to the Research Service's own table, in the war that began on October seventh, twenty twenty-three, Congress provided about six point eight billion dollars in additional military financing, four and a half billion in missile defense, and one point two billion for Iron Beam. That is on top of the regular three point eight billion a year.

[PAUSE]

There is also a stockpile. The United States keeps a War Reserve Stockpile in Israel, American munitions on Israeli soil, available in an emergency. Congress has authorized additions to that stockpile of up to five hundred million dollars a year, through fiscal year twenty twenty-seven.

And there is a law. Since two thousand and eight, under a statute called the Naval Vessel Transfer Act, the United States has been legally required to assess and protect Israel's "qualitative military edge," and to make sure that no American arms sale to any other country in the Middle East adversely affects it.

Think about that law for a moment. Congress has written into our own statutes a guarantee about the military position of a foreign country relative to its neighbors. We do not have that law for Japan. We do not have it for Poland. We do not have it for the United Kingdom, a country whose soldiers have bled beside ours in every war of the last century.

We have it for one country.

Now, I ask you, ladies and gentlemen. When a nation's legislature writes laws to guarantee another nation's military superiority, keeps its weapons stockpiled there, pays a floor of its defense costs in a lump sum, and tops that up in every emergency, at what point does the polite word "ally" stop describing the relationship?

I would suggest the word we are looking for is "dependency." Or, if you prefer a warmer word, "family." And families, in my experience, eventually have to sit down and talk about the will.

But money, as anyone with a teenager knows, is only the beginning of what family costs you. Let us turn to the more serious cost. Let us turn to war.

## IV. The War We Are Already In

I will not dwell on the war. I will not remind you that it has now lasted the better part of eight months. I will not mention that American service members have died in it, or that hundreds more have come home wounded. I will not bring up the price of gasoline, or the oil tankers burning in the Strait of Hormuz, or the missile defense stockpiles our own generals say will take years to rebuild.

[PAUSE]

I will not mention any of that, because Cicero taught us that the surest way to make a point is to promise not to make it. And so, having promised, let me now go through it carefully.

Start with last summer. In June of twenty twenty-five, Israel and Iran fought what came to be called the Twelve-Day War, from the thirteenth to the twenty-fourth of June. On June twenty-second, the United States joined in directly. American B-two bombers struck three Iranian nuclear sites: Fordow, Natanz and Isfahan. They dropped fourteen of the largest conventional bombs in the American arsenal, the thirty-thousand-pound bunker busters, on targets buried deep in the mountains.

Two days later, under American pressure, there was a ceasefire. It was announced from Washington. Iran had fired missiles at an American base in Qatar in retaliation. And for a while, we were told the matter was settled. The program had been set back. The war was over.

It was not over.

On February twenty-eighth of this year, twenty twenty-six, the United States and Israel launched joint strikes on Iran. The American operation was called Epic Fury. The Israeli operation was called Roaring Lion. Israel's defense minister called it a pre-emptive attack. The President of the United States said the purpose was regime change.

In the opening strikes, Iran's Supreme Leader, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, was killed, along with other senior officials.

[LET IT LAND]

I hold no brief for the government in Tehran. It is a cruel regime that has killed its own protesters, armed militias across the region, and chanted for our destruction for forty years. Nobody in this room should mistake an argument about American war powers for sympathy with the Islamic Republic.

But you can despise a government and still ask who decided to go to war with it, on whose timetable, and at whose expense.

Here is what followed. Iran struck back, at Israel, at American bases across the region, and at our partners in the Gulf. Saudi Arabia, the Emirates, Kuwait and Bahrain all reported people killed. Iran moved to choke the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow channel through which a large share of the world's oil passes. Within a day of the first strikes, according to reports, around one hundred and fifty freight ships, tankers among them, sat stalled in the water.

On March sixth, the President said there were "no time limits" on how long the war could last.

On April eighth, a conditional ceasefire was declared. Talks in Islamabad failed within days. The United States imposed its own naval blockade. On June seventeenth, the American and Iranian presidents signed a memorandum to end the fighting and lift the dual blockade. By July eighth, it had collapsed after new attacks on shipping in the Strait. In August it formally expired. And as of last month, according to the Congressional Research Service, the two countries were again trading limited strikes.

So when someone tells you the war ended, ask them which time.

Now let me tell you what it has cost us, in the numbers our own government reports.

In October, the Pentagon revised its count: twenty-one American service members killed and eight hundred and sixty-five wounded. Contractors were killed too. Dozens of American aircraft have been destroyed or damaged. One of our naval bases in Bahrain was reported inoperable.

[PAUSE]

Twenty-one families. I want to stop on that number, because numbers like that have a way of sliding past us. Twenty-one people who signed up to defend the United States, who are now buried in American soil, because of a war that Congress never declared.

In money: the Department of Defense estimated the war had cost about forty-three point six billion dollars as of September. The Congressional Budget Office, in mid-September, put the cost at no less than thirty-eight billion dollars, rising by roughly three billion dollars a month, and warned that our defensive missile stockpiles were being drained to the point that rebuilding them would take about five years.

How drained? According to data from the Center for Strategic and International Studies, about sixty-five percent of the roughly two thousand three hundred and thirty Patriot interceptors America had at the start of the conflict were used between February and July. Fewer than eight hundred and fifty were left.

Think about what that means. The shield that is supposed to protect American troops and American allies all over the world, in Europe, in Asia, in the Pacific, was spent down by two-thirds in five months.

And then there is the cost you paid at the pump. The Center for American Progress estimated that by mid-July, Americans had paid more than sixty-eight billion dollars extra for gasoline and diesel because of the war. Oil went above one hundred dollars a barrel after the Houthis declared a blockade on Saudi shipping in July. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve fell to its lowest level in four decades.

And because a speech about accountability has to count everyone, let me count the others too.

Israel has suffered in this war. By its own government's figures, forty-one Israeli service members and thirty-one civilians have been killed, and more than seven thousand eight hundred people injured. Iranian missiles have struck positions across the country despite one of the best missile defense systems in the world. Israeli families have spent months running to shelters. Their grief is real, and nothing in my proposal makes light of it.

In Iran, the World Health Organization has counted more than three thousand three hundred and seventy-five civilians killed and more than thirty-three thousand injured. Iran's government reports more than three thousand five hundred dead in total; an independent human rights group puts the figure higher still. Whatever you think of the regime, most of those people did not choose it, and many of them had marched against it.

And in the Gulf, in Saudi Arabia, the Emirates, Kuwait and Bahrain, civilians and soldiers who had nothing to do with the decision to begin this war have been killed by the missiles fired in response to it.

[PAUSE]

I list these not to assign blame by body count. I list them because the people who decide on wars should have to look at all of the numbers, and because in our system, the people who are supposed to look at them on our behalf are the members of Congress. Hold on to that thought.

So for those of you keeping score at home: forty-some billion in direct military costs, sixty-eight billion extra at the pump, two-thirds of our interceptors, twenty-one lives.

[SLOW]

And here is the cruelest part of my modest proposal. If Israel were the fifty-first state, all of that would make sense.

If Iranian missiles were falling on an American state, of course our Patriots would be there. Of course our sailors would be in the Gulf. Of course our taxpayers would cover the cost. That is what a union is. When Hawaii was attacked in nineteen forty-one, it was not even a state yet, only a territory, and still nobody in Ohio asked whether Hawaii was worth it. Hawaii was ours, its people were our people, and it was on its way to a seat in our councils.

But Israel is not a state. Its cabinet does not answer to our voters. Its decisions to strike, and when, and how hard, are made in Jerusalem, as is its right. And yet the consequences land on us as if we were one country.

We are carrying the risks of a union without any of the rights of one. We are being drawn into wars by decisions we did not make, and then told that questioning those decisions is disloyal, or naive, or worse.

Now I want to be careful here, because careful is the only way to be honest. I cannot tell you from public sources exactly who argued for what in the rooms where this war was decided. The American President authorized the operation, by the public record, on the afternoon of February twenty-seventh. He owns that decision. American presidents always do. I am not telling you that some other country made him do it.

What I am telling you is that the American system of government was never supposed to let one man make that decision alone. Which brings us to the next part of our story: the part where we discover that nobody in Washington seems to be in charge of the war except the person who started it.

And how did the American people feel about all this? An Ipsos poll in March and April found that seventy-one percent of Americans agreed their country should avoid involvement. By July, a majority opposed the war outright. The only country in that global survey where majority opinion supported the war was Israel.

The people who pay for the war did not want it. The people who wanted it do not pay for it. And the people who were supposed to decide, the Congress of the United States, were busy deciding not to decide.

## V. Who Declares the War

Let me make the argument the way Abraham Lincoln might have made it, plainly, in short words, one step at a time.

The Constitution says Congress shall have power to declare war. That is the first premise. It is written in Article One, Section Eight, in language a schoolchild can understand.

Congress has not declared war on Iran. That is the second premise. It is a matter of public record.

Therefore, if we are at war with Iran, we are at war without the consent that the Constitution requires.

[PAUSE]

That is not a radical argument. It is the argument our system was built on. The Founders had lived under a king who could send men to die on his own word, and they decided that in this country, that power would rest with the people's representatives, who would have to face the people afterward.

Nor did it declare war last June, when American bombers struck Fordow, Natanz and Isfahan. And this February, according to the public record, the order for Operation Epic Fury was given on the afternoon of February twenty-seventh, while the President was traveling to Texas. The strikes began the next morning, Iranian time. There was no vote in Congress before them. There was no debate. Members of Congress learned of the war, for the most part, the way you did: from the news.

I do not say that to mock the President. Every modern president has claimed this kind of power. I say it because it is the plain fact, and plain facts are where plain logic has to start.

Now, I know the response. Presidents of both parties have stretched this power for decades. Korea was never declared. Vietnam ran on a resolution passed after a murky naval incident. Libya, Syria, Yemen, Somalia: the list goes on. This war is not the first time a president has acted alone.

All true. But a bad habit repeated for seventy years is not a constitutional amendment. It is just a bad habit, grown old.

So what did Congress do this time? Let me give them their due: they did vote. Several times.

On June third, the House of Representatives passed a resolution directing the President to remove American forces from hostilities with Iran. The vote was two hundred and fifteen to two hundred and eight.

On June twenty-third, the Senate adopted it, fifty to forty-eight. Four Republican senators joined almost all the Democrats.

And what happened? Nothing, legally. Because the measure was what is called a concurrent resolution. It does not go to the President. He does not sign it or veto it. It carries no force of law. It is, in the words of one news report, a symbolic rebuke.

[LET IT LAND]

Symbolic. The constitutional branch charged with deciding whether we go to war, after months of war, finally mustered a majority in both houses to say "stop," and it said so in a form designed to have no effect.

Then, in September, they tried again. The House passed another war powers measure in mid-September, with two hundred and twenty votes and a record seven Republicans joining Democrats. And on September twenty-fourth, the Senate took up a House-passed resolution to withdraw American forces.

It failed. Forty-nine to fifty. One vote.

One senator, who had seemed undecided, cast the deciding vote against it. And the war went on.

I want you to sit with that for a moment. The whole constitutional machinery of the world's oldest written republic, built to restrain the power of one man to make war, came down, in the end, to a single vote on a resolution that would not have bound anyone even if it had passed.

Now, this is where my modest proposal shows its real elegance.

If Israel were a state, it would have two senators and about thirteen representatives. They would have voted on these resolutions. Their votes would have been recorded. Their constituents would have been able to reward or punish them. Their governor would have had to coordinate with the Pentagon through the ordinary channels of American law, subject to American courts.

In other words, the people most directly involved in the war would have had to argue for it in the open, on the floor of the American Congress, where the rest of us could hear them and answer them. Their case would have been part of our debate, rather than something that happens before our debate begins.

Instead, we have the worst of both worlds. A foreign government's security decisions shape our wars. Our President makes the final decision alone. And our Congress, the branch that should stand between them and our children, passes resolutions that are symbolic by design.

[PAUSE]

Lincoln, as a young congressman, opposed the war with Mexico. He wrote to a friend that allowing a president to invade a neighboring nation whenever he judged it necessary would place the power of war in one man's hands, the very thing the Constitution was written to prevent. I am paraphrasing him, but the logic is his, and it is as sound today as it was then.

The question is not whether Iran's government is bad. It is. The question is not whether Israel has the right to defend itself. It does. The question is who, in our system, decides when Americans go to war. And the answer the Constitution gives is: Congress, in the open, by vote, with consequences.

Right now the answer in practice is: whoever moves first, and whoever has the most influence on the man who moves second.

If that is how we are going to run our wars, then at least let the people whose wars they are come and sit in our Congress and cast their votes in daylight. Make it official. Or make Congress do its job.

## VI. The Engagement Ring: Section Two Nineteen

Every merger begins with a quiet agreement between the lawyers. And while the public was watching the war, the lawyers in Congress were drafting one.

It is called Section Two Nineteen of the House version of the National Defense Authorization Act for fiscal year twenty twenty-seven. That is the giant annual bill that sets policy for the Pentagon. It is enormous, it passes every year, and it is where Washington tucks the things it does not want to debate on their own.

Now, I want to be precise here, because there has been a lot of loose talk about this provision, and loose talk is the enemy of a good case.

Section Two Nineteen establishes what it calls the United States-Israel Defense Technology Cooperation Initiative. It directs the Secretary of Defense to designate a senior Pentagon official as an "executive agent" whose job is to coordinate joint research, development, testing and production of defense technologies with Israel. The areas named include missile defense, counter-drone systems, cybersecurity and artificial intelligence. The point, as the trade press put it, is to encourage co-development and co-production between the two countries' defense industries, which until now has mostly been limited to defensive systems like Iron Dome.

Here is what it does not do. It does not merge the two militaries. It does not put American troops under Israeli command or the reverse. It is not a mutual defense treaty. It does not by itself spend a single dollar; the money comes through appropriations. Those points come from a pro-Israel advocacy group, the American Jewish Committee, defending the provision, and on those facts they are right. I would rather lose an argument honestly than win it on an exaggeration.

So it is not a wedding. It is something subtler. It is an engagement ring.

[PAUSE]

The House passed the bill on July twenty-second, two hundred and sixteen to two hundred and twelve. A close vote, but not over this provision alone; the defense bill is always a fight. Before final passage, the House accepted by voice vote an amendment from Representative Anna Paulina Luna that struck one word from the section. The word was "integration."

I love that detail. They did not change what the section does. They took out the word that described it.

Meanwhile, Representative Thomas Massie of Kentucky, a Republican, offered an amendment to delete the section entirely. The Rules Committee never allowed it to come to a vote. Representative Ro Khanna, a Democrat from California, tried to strike it in committee and failed. Opposition, you will notice, came from both ends of the political spectrum, which is usually a sign that something real is going on.

The Senate's version contains a parallel provision, Section Twelve Seventeen. As of the latest reports I could find, the Senate bill has been stalled, in part because several Democratic senators said they would not move forward until this measure was debated. So it is not law yet. A compromise bill, analysts expect, will likely include some version of it.

It is also not alone. In February of this year, a senator introduced a separate bill, the United States-Israel FUTURES Act, short for Framework for Upgraded Technologies, Unified Research, and Enhanced Security. You will notice that somebody in Washington spent real time on that acronym. It was referred to committee, where it sits. But the pipeline is full. When one vehicle stalls, another is waiting.

And the Senate's own defense bill hit a wall in July, when a motion to advance it failed fifty to forty-six, amid objections over Iran policy. So the fate of this engagement ring now rests, as so many things in Washington do, with a handful of negotiators in a conference room, deciding in private what the country will be bound to in public.

Now, why does a modest engagement ring matter?

The supporters, led by the chairman of the House Armed Services Committee, Mike Rogers of Alabama, argue that it actually improves oversight by naming one official responsible for these programs. That is a real argument, and I will grant it the dignity of a reply.

The critics, including analysts at the Stimson Center and at the Center for Civilians in Conflict, worry that co-production is ill-defined, that it may allow less congressional oversight than traditional military aid, which at least has to pass through the appropriations process and the arms export laws, and that once production lines are intertwined, they become very hard to untangle. One former State Department official, Josh Paul, described the purpose as entrenching the relationship deep in America's own defense industrial base.

[SLOW]

That is the heart of it. Aid can be cut. It is a line in a budget. Congress can change it next year. But a factory in Alabama that makes half of a weapon, and a factory near Tel Aviv that makes the other half, cannot be separated without layoffs, lawsuits and a delegation of angry congressmen. Co-production turns a policy into a constituency.

And here is the irony that makes my proposal almost irresistible.

In the same season, Israel's own prime minister said he wants his country to end its reliance on American military aid within ten years, because, as he put it, Israel has come of age.

So at the very moment when the recipient is talking about independence, the donor is drafting permanence. At the very moment the son announces he is ready to move out, the father is quietly adding his name to the deed.

Ladies and gentlemen, that is not foreign policy. That is a family business. And I say, if we are going to run a family business, let us incorporate it properly. Let us admit the partner to the firm, give him a seat on the board, and make him subject to the same audit and the same law as every other partner.

Or, alternatively, if Israel has truly come of age, as its prime minister says, then let us take him at his word. Let us congratulate him, help him pack, and stop building a permanent apartment for him in our basement.

What we should not do is both: subsidize a nation's independence and then pass laws making sure it never needs to be independent of us.

## VII. The Liberty

Every family has a story it does not tell at dinner. This family's story is about a ship.

On the eighth of June, nineteen sixty-seven, in the middle of the Six-Day War, an American intelligence ship called the USS Liberty was sailing in international waters in the eastern Mediterranean, off the coast of the Sinai Peninsula. It was a slow, lightly armed vessel, crowded with antennas, flying the American flag. Its job was to listen.

That afternoon, Israeli jets attacked it. They strafed the ship with cannon fire and dropped napalm on its decks. Then Israeli torpedo boats arrived and opened fire with their guns. One torpedo struck the starboard side and tore a hole roughly thirty-nine feet across. That single torpedo killed twenty-five men.

The whole attack lasted about twenty-three minutes.

[PAUSE]

When it was over, thirty-four Americans were dead. Among them were two Marines and a civilian employee of the National Security Agency. One hundred and seventy-one were wounded. The ship stayed afloat only because its crew refused to let it sink.

Israel said the attack was a tragic mistake: that its forces had misidentified the Liberty as an Egyptian vessel. Israel apologized. Many survivors have never accepted that explanation, and they have spent the rest of their lives saying so.

Now, I want to be honest with you about where the evidence stands, because the Liberty has attracted its share of people who are more interested in a villain than in the truth.

There are serious people who believe it was a mistake, made in the fog of a fast war. A judge named Jay Cristol spent years researching the case and went to court to force the National Security Agency to release recordings of Israeli communications from that day; he concluded it was an error. Israeli inquiries concluded the same.

And there are serious people who believe otherwise. Dean Rusk, who was Secretary of State at the time, wrote years later that he never believed the Israeli explanation, and he called the attack outrageous. A report prepared for President Johnson by Clark Clifford, then chairman of the President's Foreign Intelligence Advisory Board, described it as a flagrant act of gross negligence for which Israel should be held completely responsible. And in two thousand and three, a private panel chaired by Admiral Thomas Moorer, a former chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, concluded that Israel was culpable and suggested the attack was deliberate.

I cannot settle that argument from this stage, and I will not pretend to. What I can do is tell you what the United States government did, and did not do, about the deaths of thirty-four of its own.

[SLOW]

The Navy convened a Court of Inquiry. By the record, it heard about four days of testimony. Critics say the whole process took about ten days, and that the officer presiding over it reportedly said a proper inquiry would have taken six months. No Israeli personnel testified.

The commanding officer of the Liberty, Commander William McGonagle, wounded himself, kept his ship fighting and afloat through the attack. He was awarded the Medal of Honor, the highest award for valor this country gives. It is customarily presented by the President, at the White House. McGonagle's was presented by the Secretary of the Navy, at the Washington Navy Yard, in an unpublicized ceremony.

Think about that. A man earns the Medal of Honor defending an American ship, and his government hands it to him quietly, as though his courage were an embarrassment.

And Congress? According to the historical record, there has never been a full congressional investigation of the attack on the Liberty. It came up as an aside in Senate hearings that summer. It appeared in a House report a few years later that was about something else. That is all.

Now I have to correct something, and I want to do it out loud, because a speech that asks for accountability had better practice it.

You may have heard it said, perhaps even by people who share my frustration, that Israel never paid reparations for the Liberty. That is not true.

[PAUSE]

In May of nineteen sixty-eight, Israel paid the United States about three point three two million dollars for the families of the thirty-four men who were killed. In March of nineteen sixty-nine, it paid about three point five seven million dollars to the wounded. And in December of nineteen eighty, it paid six million dollars as a final settlement for the damage to the ship. Money was paid. If anyone tells you otherwise, they are wrong, and you should tell them so.

But notice what the money settled and what it did not. It settled the claims. It did not settle the questions. A check is not an investigation. An apology is not a record. And thirty-four families were given compensation without ever being given a full public accounting from their own Congress of how their sons died.

The National Security Agency released recordings in two thousand and three, and hundreds more documents in two thousand and seven. As late as two thousand and six, the record suggests, it still held boxes of undeclassified material. When the first recordings came out in two thousand and three, two linguists who had worked on the intercepts said at least two more tapes had been withheld. I cannot tell you whether that is so. I can tell you that a government confident in its account would have nothing to lose by releasing everything.

Fifty-nine years have passed. Most of the officials are dead. Most of the survivors are old men now.

This past June, on the fifty-ninth anniversary of the attack, Representative Massie spoke about the Liberty and brought survivors to the Capitol to ask for a new investigation. I do not know what a new investigation would find. Neither does he. That is precisely the point of having one.

Here is why the Liberty belongs in a speech about statehood.

The instinct of the American government in nineteen sixty-seven was to protect the relationship. Not the truth, not the families, not even the reputation of the Medal of Honor. The relationship. Fifty-nine years later, that instinct is still in charge.

And that is what a dependency does to a republic. It teaches the republic to look away from its own dead in order to avoid an awkward conversation with its friend.

[BUILD]

If Israel were a state, the Liberty would be a matter for American courts, American investigators and an American Congress with full jurisdiction. There would be no diplomatic delicacy, no question of a foreign government's sovereignty, no reason to present a hero's medal in a side room. There would simply be the law, and the law would ask what happened.

I do not actually want to annex anyone to get an honest answer about thirty-four dead sailors. I would settle for the honest answer.

But if the only way this government will ever give the Liberty's crew a full public hearing is to make the matter domestic, then by all means, let us make it domestic. Those men deserve at least that much.

## VIII. Apollo, Pennsylvania

Let me take you now to a small town on the Kiskiminetas River, northeast of Pittsburgh. The town is called Apollo. In the nineteen fifties and sixties, it was home to a company called the Nuclear Materials and Equipment Corporation, known by its initials as NUMEC.

NUMEC processed highly enriched uranium for the American government. Highly enriched uranium is the kind of material that, in sufficient quantity, can be made into a nuclear weapon. And at NUMEC, a great deal of it went missing.

How much? The estimates vary, and I will give you the range rather than pick the scariest number. The FBI's case concerned about two hundred and six pounds, or ninety-three kilograms. Department of Energy records list a loss of about two hundred and sixty-nine kilograms before nineteen sixty-eight, and seventy-six kilograms afterward. That was the largest such loss at any commercial site in the country.

[PAUSE]

The company's president, Zalman Shapiro, was a respected chemist with business ties to Israeli officials, including a contract to build equipment for Israel. In nineteen sixty-five, the Atomic Energy Commission discovered the shortfall. NUMEC paid the government eight hundred and thirty-four thousand dollars for the missing material.

Eight hundred and thirty-four thousand dollars. For uranium of the kind that goes into bombs. That is less than some people in this room paid for their house, and they did not have to worry about where the house went.

The FBI investigated Shapiro, on and off, from nineteen sixty-five to nineteen eighty. No charges were ever filed.

And yet. In September of nineteen sixty-eight, four Israeli visitors toured the NUMEC plant. One of them was Rafi Eitan, listed on the paperwork as a chemist for Israel's defense ministry. He was, in fact, one of Israel's most senior intelligence officers. He would later run the operation that recruited Jonathan Pollard, whom we will meet shortly. The defense analyst Anthony Cordesman later said there was no conceivable reason for Eitan to have gone except for the nuclear material.

In February of nineteen seventy-six, the CIA told staff at the Nuclear Regulatory Commission that it believed the uranium had gone to Israel. President Carter's national security staff later described the CIA's case as persuasive, though not conclusive.

[SLOW]

Now. Here is where honesty requires me to slow down.

There is another explanation. The journalist Seymour Hersh, who is not known for going easy on anyone, argued in nineteen ninety-one that Shapiro diverted nothing, and that the missing uranium ended up in the air and water of Apollo itself, through sloppy handling and leaks. One of the CIA's own leading investigators reportedly said he knew of nothing to indicate Shapiro was guilty. And in two thousand and nine, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, asked to clear Shapiro's name, found no documents showing a diversion, but said it could not "unequivocally conclude" that none had happened.

So I will not stand here and tell you that a man stole uranium for a foreign country. The record does not prove that, and he was never charged. What the record does show is something almost as troubling. It shows that the United States lost track of a bomb's worth of its most dangerous material, that its own intelligence agency believed the material had gone abroad, and that the government never resolved the question.

A study by the Government Accountability Office, declassified in twenty ten, concluded that a timely, concerted effort would have greatly aided, and possibly solved, the NUMEC questions. That effort was not made. Documents declassified in twenty fourteen suggested that the Attorney General of the United States in the nineteen seventies believed federal officials may have broken the law by failing to report a possible felony.

[PAUSE]

And the town of Apollo? The Army Corps of Engineers is still overseeing the cleanup of contaminated land at the old NUMEC waste site. It was supposed to be finished in twenty fifteen. It was delayed when far more contamination was found. Last I could find, the work was scheduled to begin around now, with a timeline of about six years.

So whichever explanation is true, the people of a small Pennsylvania town are still living with what happened. Either their uranium went abroad and their government decided not to find out, or it went into their soil and water and their government took sixty years to dig it up.

Here is the statehood argument, and I make it almost sincerely.

When a question like NUMEC touches a foreign government, every instinct in Washington says: classify, delay, do not embarrass the friend. The investigation stalls not because the answer is unknowable but because the answer might be inconvenient.

If the parties to this story were all inside one country, under one law, there would be no friend to protect. There would only be a crime or an accident, and a duty to find out which.

That is the pattern, you see. The Liberty and Apollo are two very different stories, but they rhyme. In both, Americans were harmed or put at risk. In both, serious officials suspected something more than an accident. In both, the government chose not to look too hard. And in both, the reason was not evidence. It was the relationship.

## IX. The Bomb We Do Not Mention

Which brings us to the largest unmentioned object in American foreign policy.

Israel is widely believed to possess nuclear weapons. Estimates vary enormously, from around ninety warheads at the low end to several hundred at the high end. Israel does not confirm this. It does not deny it either. For decades it has said only that it will not be the first country to introduce nuclear weapons to the Middle East, a sentence it reportedly interprets to mean it will not test them or formally acknowledge them.

Israel is not a party to the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty, the agreement that nearly every nation on earth has signed.

Its reactor near the desert town of Dimona was built with French help beginning in the late nineteen fifties. American inspectors visited once a year in the nineteen sixties, under terms that, by most accounts, fell short of what President Kennedy had demanded.

[PAUSE]

Then, according to the historian Avner Cohen, in September of nineteen sixty-nine, President Nixon and Prime Minister Golda Meir reached a secret understanding at the White House. Israel would keep its program quiet and would not test. The United States would not press it to sign the treaty, and would stop asking questions out loud. The American inspections ended that same year.

That understanding, in substance, is still the policy of the United States more than half a century later. American officials do not acknowledge Israel's arsenal. Federal employees with clearances are barred from discussing it.

In nineteen eighty-six, a former technician at Dimona named Mordechai Vanunu gave a British newspaper details of the program. His information implied an arsenal of one to two hundred devices. Israeli agents lured him out of Britain to Italy and brought him back to Israel, where he spent eighteen years in prison.

Now hold all of this in your mind next to the war we discussed earlier.

[SLOW]

The United States went to war this year, and bombed Iran last year, in large part to keep Iran from acquiring a nuclear weapon. Our government says, and I agree, that the spread of nuclear weapons in the Middle East is one of the gravest dangers in the world.

And yet our government cannot bring itself to say, in public, that the one country in the region believed to actually have them has them.

Consider the strange diplomacy this produces. Iran signed the Non-Proliferation Treaty decades ago, and we have spent years, rightly, demanding that it live up to it, with inspectors, sanctions and finally bombs. Israel never signed, and so, technically, it has broken no promise. The treaty that we use as the legal basis for confronting one country simply does not reach the other.

You can believe, as I do, that this asymmetry has real reasons behind it. Israel is a small country that has been invaded and that faces enemies sworn to its destruction, and its leaders have concluded that ambiguity keeps it alive. But you cannot pretend the asymmetry is invisible to the rest of the world. Every government in the region sees it. Every one of them factors it into its calculations. And when American officials are forbidden even to name it, the only people fooled are Americans.

I am not arguing that Israel's arsenal is the moral equivalent of Iran's ambitions. Reasonable people can believe that a democracy with a long record of not using such weapons is different from a regime that chants for its enemies' destruction. I am arguing something narrower and, I think, harder to deny. A foreign policy that cannot name the most important fact about the region it is fighting in is not a foreign policy. It is a superstition.

And every American diplomat who stands at the United Nations to talk about non-proliferation knows that everyone in the room knows. That costs us. It makes our arguments sound like special pleading, because they are.

My modest proposal, as ever, solves this neatly. If Israel became the fifty-first state, its weapons would become American weapons. They would be under American command, subject to American law, counted in American treaties and overseen by American civilians. The ambiguity would end overnight. Nobody would have to pretend anymore.

Of course, nobody in Jerusalem wants that, and I do not blame them. A nation's deterrent is the last thing it would hand to someone else. But that is exactly my point. If Israel's most important security decisions are, rightly, its own, then it is strange for America to carry the diplomatic cost of protecting a secret it does not control.

[PAUSE]

One more story, because it shows how even good friends treat each other when one of them is far bigger than the other.

In November of nineteen eighty-five, the FBI arrested a civilian intelligence analyst for the United States Navy named Jonathan Pollard, just after he left the Israeli Embassy in Washington, where he had tried to seek asylum. He had been passing classified American material to Israeli handlers. He pleaded guilty in nineteen eighty-six to conspiracy to deliver national defense information to a foreign government, and in March of nineteen eighty-seven he was sentenced to life in prison.

Israel acknowledged part of its role and apologized. In nineteen ninety-five, while Pollard was still in an American prison, Israel granted him citizenship. In nineteen ninety-eight, Prime Minister Netanyahu acknowledged that Pollard had been an Israeli agent. Pollard was released in twenty fifteen, and when his parole ended, he moved to Israel at the end of twenty twenty.

Now, allies spy on each other. Everyone knows it. Americans have spied on friends too, and been caught. I raise Pollard not to suggest that Israel is uniquely treacherous; it is not. I raise it because it reminds us of something the sentimental version of this relationship forgets.

Israel is a separate country with separate interests. It acts on them, sometimes against ours, as any nation would. That is normal. What is not normal is for the United States to keep treating the relationship as though our interests and theirs were identical, writing them blank checks and guarantees as if they were one of our own states, while they, quite reasonably, behave like a foreign country.

If they are a foreign country, let us treat them as one, with the conditions and scrutiny that implies. And if we insist on treating them as family, then let us bring them inside the house, where the house rules apply.

## X. The Lobby, Lawfully

Now we come to the part of the evening where some speakers lower their voices and start talking about shadowy forces. I am going to do the opposite. I am going to raise the lights.

Let me say clearly, before I say anything else, what I am not saying. I am not saying that Jewish Americans control the government. That is an ancient lie, it has been used to justify terrible crimes, and it is false. Americans of every faith and background stand on every side of this debate. I am not saying that anyone who supports Israel is disloyal to the United States. That is another old slander, and it is also false. People can love two countries, and most immigrants and their children do.

And I will point out one more fact that the conspiracy-mongers always leave out. According to Gallup's survey this February, the group of Americans most sympathetic to Israel is not defined by religion or ancestry. It is defined by party. Seventy percent of Republicans said they sympathize more with the Israelis, compared with seventeen percent of Democrats. This is a political coalition, and like every political coalition, it can be argued with in the open.

What I am saying is simple and lawful and boring. There is a lobby. It raises money. It spends money. It lobbies Congress and it elects candidates. And it is very, very good at it.

[PAUSE]

The American Israel Public Affairs Committee, known as AIPAC, was founded in nineteen fifty-four and took its current name in nineteen fifty-nine. It is an American organization, registered as a domestic lobbying group, organized as a nonprofit under Section five-oh-one c four of the tax code. It says it receives no financial assistance from Israel or any foreign group, and I have seen no evidence otherwise. It has been argued over the years, by Senator J. William Fulbright among others, that it ought to register as a foreign agent. It has never been required to.

So let us treat it exactly as we treat the National Rifle Association, the pharmaceutical lobby, the oil industry, the teachers' unions or the realtors. As Americans exercising their right to petition their government, whose influence we are equally free to criticize.

For most of its history, AIPAC did not give money directly to candidates. That changed recently. In late twenty twenty-one, it formed a political action committee. In January twenty twenty-two, it formed a super PAC called the United Democracy Project, which can spend unlimited sums on elections.

In the twenty twenty-four cycle, according to public reporting, AIPAC and its affiliated committees spent a record forty-five point two million dollars to defeat two members of Congress in their primaries: Jamaal Bowman of New York and Cori Bush of Missouri. Both lost.

In this cycle, the United Democracy Project had raised, by June, about one hundred and four million dollars, and spent about fifty-two and a half million, according to FactCheck.org. It spent millions in Democratic primaries in Illinois, New Jersey, California, Maryland and Michigan. Reporting from Illinois described additional millions from AIPAC donors flowing through newly created committees with names that did not reveal who was behind them.

[SLOW]

And then there was Kentucky.

You have heard me mention Thomas Massie several times tonight. He is a Republican congressman, an engineer, a stubborn libertarian who votes against nearly everything, including, frequently, his own party. He opposed this year's war without congressional authorization. He opposed Section Two Nineteen. He brought the Liberty survivors to Washington.

On May nineteenth of this year, Massie lost his primary to a challenger named Ed Gallrein. It was, by reported ad spending of more than thirty-two million dollars, the most expensive House primary in American history. Pro-Israel groups accounted for more than nine million dollars of the spending against Massie, with the United Democracy Project alone spending more than three million. Massie called the race a referendum on whether Israel gets to buy seats in Congress.

Now, fairness again. Massie's opponent had the complete and total endorsement of the President of the United States, who rallied against Massie in Kentucky in March. In a Republican primary, that may well have been the decisive factor. I am not telling you that the pro-Israel money alone defeated him. I am telling you that it was spent, in large amounts, and that every member of Congress noticed.

And that is the real point. The direct effect of the money is not the main thing. The main thing is the lesson. Every member of Congress who is thinking about voting against the next aid package, or for the next war powers resolution, or to strike Section Two Nineteen, has watched what happened to Bowman, to Bush and to Massie. Two Democrats on the left. One Republican on the right. The message is bipartisan, and it is received.

[PAUSE]

The influence also shows up in our law. As of twenty twenty-four, thirty-eight states had passed laws or executive orders designed to discourage boycotts of Israel. In Texas, a speech pathologist named Bahia Amawi, who worked with children in a public school district, was told she had to certify that she would not boycott Israel in order to keep her contract. A federal judge ruled in twenty nineteen that the Texas law was an unconstitutional, viewpoint-based restriction on speech.

Think about what that case means. A woman who helps children learn to talk was asked to sign a political pledge about a foreign country in order to keep her job in an American public school. We do not ask teachers to pledge not to boycott France. We do not ask contractors to swear allegiance to Canadian lumber.

It is worth noticing that the politics of this are changing inside Congress itself. Last year, several Democratic members of the House, including Deborah Ross and Valerie Foushee of North Carolina, Morgan McGarvey of Kentucky and Seth Moulton of Massachusetts, said they would no longer accept AIPAC's donations. These are mainstream Democrats, not insurgents. They simply decided that the money had become a liability with the voters they serve.

That is how campaign finance is supposed to work in a democracy. Money is offered. Voters notice. Politicians decide whether the money is worth what the voters think of it. The answer to a powerful lobby is not to whisper about it. It is to talk about it in the open until voters can weigh it like anything else.

And then there is the matter of who addresses our Congress. According to the House's own historians, the foreign leader who has addressed the United States Congress more times than any other is Benjamin Netanyahu, four times: in nineteen ninety-six, twenty eleven, twenty fifteen and July of twenty twenty-four. Winston Churchill, who led Britain through the Second World War, made three addresses.

I do not begrudge any prime minister a speech. But I notice that our Congress has heard from the leader of one foreign country more often than from any other foreigner in our history, and that it often greets him with more enthusiasm than it greets its own President.

[BUILD]

Here, my modest proposal rises to its full and shining height.

Lobbying is what you do when you do not have a vote. The farmers lobby because there are not enough farmers to win elections alone. The oil companies lobby because barrels of oil cannot vote. Every lobby is, in a sense, an admission that the people it speaks for cannot simply walk into the polling place and get what they want.

Make Israel a state, and its people would not need a lobby. They would have two senators and about thirteen representatives, elected by their own voters, sitting in the open, accountable for every vote. Their influence would be exactly as large as their population, which is the American way of measuring influence. No more, no less.

And the rest of us would finally have what we lack now: a clear view. We would know who was speaking for whom. Their arguments would be made on the floor of the House, not in a fundraiser. And when they lost a vote, as every state's delegation sometimes does, they would lose it.

Of course, there is a less drastic solution, and I suspect you can see it coming. We could simply pass laws that apply to every lobby equally. Full and fast disclosure of who funds every super PAC. An end to committees with names designed to hide their donors. A rule that a public employee's job can never depend on her political opinion about any foreign country.

Those reforms would not single out anyone. They would apply to the gun lobby and the crypto lobby and the pharmaceutical lobby and every lobby that has learned to frighten a congressman with a seven-figure primary. That is the beauty of equal rules. They are not aimed at anyone. They are simply aimed at the problem.

## XI. The Fine Print of Statehood

Now let us be practical. Suppose that tomorrow morning, Congress woke up, read the transcript of this speech, and said: you have persuaded us. Let us draw up the papers. What would we actually get?

First, population. Israel's population this year is estimated at a little over ten million. That would make it, roughly, a mid-sized American state, roughly the population of Michigan. With the average congressional district at around seven hundred and sixty thousand people, it would be entitled to about thirteen seats in the House of Representatives, and of course two senators. That is fifteen electoral votes. In a close presidential election, a swing state.

[PAUSE]

I will let the political consultants in the room absorb that for a moment.

Second, money. Israel's economy this year is estimated at about seven hundred and forty-seven billion dollars, with an income per person of over seventy-two thousand dollars. That is wealthier per head than many of our current states. As a state, its citizens and businesses would pay federal income tax, just like everyone in Texas or Ohio.

In other words, for the first time in seventy years, money would flow in both directions. The fifty-first state would contribute to the federal treasury that has been supporting it. It would help pay for its own missile defense, and for everyone else's highways. I call that progress.

Third, the military. Israel's armed forces would become a National Guard, under the governor in peacetime and the President in war. Its decisions to strike foreign countries would no longer be its own. They would go through the Pentagon, the President and, if our Constitution meant anything, the Congress.

Here, I admit, the proposal begins to strain. I cannot imagine a single Israeli general agreeing to it, and I cannot blame them. But again, notice what that tells us. The arrangement we have now gives Israel the independence of a foreign nation and the backing of an American state. Statehood would take away the independence. The status quo lets it keep both. No wonder nobody in the current arrangement is eager to change it.

[SLOW]

Fourth, and here the fine print gets very fine indeed: the Constitution.

A state of the Union does not just get money and votes. It gets the whole Constitution, all of it, binding on its government. The First Amendment. Due process. The Fourteenth Amendment's guarantee that no state shall deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.

Any person. Within its jurisdiction.

And so we arrive at the question that every serious person on every side of this debate knows is at the center of it, and that our current arrangement lets American politicians avoid. What about the millions of Palestinians who live in the territories Israel has controlled since nineteen sixty-seven, in the West Bank, and in Gaza, which has been devastated by the war of the last three years? Under our Constitution, could a state govern people within its control who are not its citizens and do not vote?

[LET IT LAND]

Abraham Lincoln faced a version of this question, about a different people in a different century. His answer, stripped to its logic, was that a republic cannot permanently govern some people as citizens and others as subjects. It must, sooner or later, become one thing or the other. He was right, and it cost a war to prove it.

I am not going to tell you how the Israelis and Palestinians should resolve their future. That is their question to answer, and people far wiser than I have broken their hearts on it. Two states, one state, some confederation not yet imagined. I do not know.

But I do know this. If we tried to bring that situation inside the American Union, our own Constitution would not allow it to continue as it is. American courts would have to answer the question that American diplomacy has spent decades declining to ask.

And that is the deepest point of my modest proposal. It is not really about Israel. It is about us. We are funding, arming and defending an arrangement that our own founding law would never permit within our own borders. We have decided, as a matter of policy, that what we could never do at home we may pay for abroad, so long as it is called foreign.

[PAUSE]

There is a final irony in the fine print, and I would be a poor satirist if I did not mention it.

The United States already has places that want to be states, or at least want a vote. Puerto Rico, home to more than three million American citizens, has no voting member of Congress and no electoral votes. The District of Columbia, with roughly seven hundred thousand residents, pays federal taxes and has no senators. Its license plates complain about taxation without representation.

Americans in San Juan serve in our military and die in our wars and cannot vote for the commander in chief who sends them. Americans in Washington, D.C., live within sight of the Capitol and cannot elect a senator to it.

And yet, this year, the Congress has been far busier drafting a technology cooperation initiative with a foreign country than doing anything about either. It is easier, apparently, to write laws binding our defense industry to a nation of ten million that does not want to join us than to hold a serious vote on the political future of three million citizens who already belong to us.

So if anyone objects to my proposal on the grounds that statehood is a serious matter that requires careful thought, I agree entirely. I only ask that we start with the Americans who have been waiting longest. Let Puerto Rico and the District go first. Israel can take a number.

## XII. Conditions Have Worked Before

Before I take on the strongest objection, let me answer a quieter one, the one that people murmur rather than say out loud. It goes like this: "It has always been this way. The relationship has always been unconditional. To change it now would be to break something sacred."

That is simply not true. And the history of how it was not true is one of the most encouraging stories I know.

[PAUSE]

Begin at the beginning. On May fourteenth, nineteen forty-eight, David Ben-Gurion proclaimed the State of Israel, and President Harry Truman recognized it the very same day. He did so over the objections of his own State Department, which had recommended a United Nations trusteeship instead, and which worried about Soviet influence in the Arab world and about Arab oil.

So the relationship began with an American president making a hard choice against the advice of his own diplomats. It was a decision, not a destiny. And in the early years, the help that followed was modest. For a time, the main American assistance was food aid. The great flood of military financing came much later.

Now jump forward to nineteen fifty-six. Israel, together with Britain and France, invaded Egypt during the Suez Crisis, and Israeli forces occupied the Sinai Peninsula and the Gaza Strip. President Dwight Eisenhower, a Republican, a war hero, a man nobody could call soft, was furious. He pressed all three countries to withdraw. When Israel held on to Gaza and to Sharm el-Sheikh, Eisenhower sought economic sanctions through the United Nations to force a full withdrawal.

In March of nineteen fifty-seven, Israel withdrew.

[LET IT LAND]

I want to be fair to the whole record. Eisenhower later told a supporter, in nineteen sixty-five, that he greatly regretted forcing Israel out of the Sinai. History does not hand out clean morals. But the point stands: an American president once decided that American policy would be set in Washington, applied real pressure to a friend, and the friendship survived. Within a generation it was stronger than ever.

Jump forward again to October of nineteen seventy-three. Egypt and Syria launched a surprise attack on Israel on Yom Kippur. Israel's situation was dire. And the United States responded with one of the largest airlifts in its history. From October fourteenth to November fourteenth, American aircraft flew five hundred and sixty-seven missions and delivered about twenty-two thousand tons of materiel. Ships carried tens of thousands of tons more.

That was American generosity at its most dramatic, and I do not regret it. But I want you to notice what it cost. Arab oil producers declared an embargo against the United States. Oil prices rose sharply, fuel became scarce, and Americans sat in lines at gas stations until the embargo was lifted in March of nineteen seventy-four.

Some of you are old enough to remember those lines. Some of you sat in similar lines this past summer. The lesson of nineteen seventy-three is not that America should never help. It is that when America helps, Americans pay, and they deserve to know it and to have a say.

[PAUSE]

Now nineteen seventy-five. After the war, Henry Kissinger was trying to negotiate a further Israeli pullback in the Sinai. Israel's government rejected the American proposal. And on March twenty-first, nineteen seventy-five, President Gerald Ford wrote to Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin that his administration would "reassess" its relations with Israel. Arms shipments were halted.

Gerald Ford. The most mild-mannered man ever to occupy the Oval Office. He paused the weapons.

And what happened? Did the alliance collapse? Did the region burn? No. That September, Israel and Egypt signed a disengagement agreement. Within a few years came Camp David and a peace treaty between Israel and Egypt that has held, through every storm since, for more than four decades. I will not claim that Ford's letter caused the peace. Many hands made it. But it plainly did not prevent it.

Then the nineteen eighties. In nineteen eighty-one, Israel bombed Iraq's Osirak nuclear reactor, and President Ronald Reagan, a man beloved by the friends of Israel to this day, suspended a shipment of military aircraft and criticized the strike. In nineteen eighty-two, during the war in Lebanon, the Reagan administration suspended shipments of cluster munitions to Israel and reminded its government that American weapons were provided for defensive purposes.

Ronald Reagan. Conditions.

[BUILD]

And in nineteen ninety-one, President George Herbert Walker Bush faced a request from Prime Minister Yitzhak Shamir for ten billion dollars in American loan guarantees, largely to help absorb immigrants from the collapsing Soviet Union. Bush asked, as a condition, for a freeze on settlement construction in the occupied territories. The Shamir government would not agree, and a political showdown followed between the two governments.

The next year, after an Israeli election brought a new coalition to power, the new government approved a partial freeze on housing construction in the territories.

Do you see the pattern? Truman, a Democrat, made his own decision. Eisenhower, a Republican, applied pressure. Ford, a Republican, paused arms. Reagan, a Republican, paused aircraft and cluster bombs. Bush, a Republican, set conditions on loan guarantees.

For most of the history of this relationship, American presidents of both parties treated it as exactly what it was: a close friendship between two separate countries, each with its own interests, in which the larger and richer partner sometimes said no.

[PAUSE]

What changed? I think several things did. The aid was locked into ten-year agreements that run on autopilot. Congress wrote floors and guarantees into statute. The lobbying, as we discussed, moved from persuasion to primaries. And somewhere along the way, a habit of saying no, which every healthy friendship needs, came to be treated as a betrayal.

So when people tell you that conditioning aid would be unprecedented, tell them it would be the most traditional thing in the world. It would be a return to the way Eisenhower and Ford and Reagan and the first President Bush ran American foreign policy. It is the unconditional era, the era of floors and lump sums and symbolic resolutions, that is the historical oddity.

My modest proposal, you will recall, was to make Israel a state. But the history suggests an even more modest proposal: make America a superpower again. Not the kind that shouts. The kind that sets its own terms.

## XIII. The Strongest Objection

I promised you that I would give the strongest argument against everything I have said, and answer it. A speech that only argues with weak opponents is just a pep rally, and you did not come out on a weeknight for a pep rally.

So here is the case against me, made as well as I can make it.

[SLOW]

First: Israel is a sovereign nation. Its people have fought for their independence through war after war. They have their own language, their own history, their own democracy, their own elections, loud and contentious ones. They do not want to be an American state, and the very suggestion is insulting. A proposal to annex a democracy, even as a joke, plays with the language of empire.

Second: the alliance pays for itself. Israel shares intelligence that has saved American lives. It has developed technologies, in missile defense and drones and cybersecurity, that our own forces now use. Some of the aid money comes back to American factories and American workers, because much of it must be spent on American weapons. Israel has been the testing ground for systems that protect our troops. And it is a democracy in a region with very few of them.

And the numbers on the alliance's side are real. From twenty eleven to twenty twenty-two, the United States contributed about two point six billion dollars to Iron Dome, a system built to knock down rockets fired at civilians. The two countries jointly fund the Arrow missile defense program. Defenders of the relationship can fairly say that this is some of the most effective defensive spending America has ever done, measured in lives saved per dollar.

Third: Israel was attacked. On October seventh, twenty twenty-three, it suffered the deadliest day in its history. It faces enemies who say openly that they want it destroyed, and a regime in Tehran that has armed militias on its borders and fired missiles directly at its cities. To talk about conditions and accountability at such a moment is to give comfort to those enemies.

Fourth: deterrence. If America is seen to waver in its support, Iran and its proxies will take it as a sign of weakness, and the region will become more dangerous, not less. American credibility is on the line.

[PAUSE]

Those are serious arguments. Many decent people hold them. Let me answer each, honestly.

To the first, on sovereignty: you are exactly right, and that is the whole joke. Of course Israel should not become an American state. Of course its people would not want it. That is why the proposal is absurd. But the arrangement we actually have is also absurd, in the opposite direction. We behave as if Israel were part of us, in our budgets, our statutes and our wars, while agreeing that it is entirely separate whenever the question of accountability comes up. My proposal is not that Israel should lose its sovereignty. It is that we should stop acting as if we had no sovereignty of our own.

Respect for a nation's independence cuts both ways. If Israel is independent, then America is independent too, and entitled to set the terms of its own aid, decide its own wars, and investigate the deaths of its own sailors.

To the second, that the alliance pays for itself: some of it does, and I said so at the beginning. Intelligence sharing is real. Co-developed missile defense is real. American jobs that depend on these contracts are real. But a relationship that genuinely benefits both sides does not need special treatment to survive. It does not need a lump sum paid in advance, a statutory floor, an exemption from scrutiny or a law guaranteeing one party's military superiority. It can stand on its own merits, negotiated, reviewed and renewed like any other partnership.

Look at our other close allies. Britain, Japan, Germany, Australia, South Korea. They share intelligence with us. They co-develop weapons with us. They host our bases. And, by and large, they pay for their own militaries. We bargain with them, sometimes hard. Nobody thinks the alliance with Britain is in danger because Congress refuses to give it a guaranteed annual check.

Israel today is a wealthy country, with an income per person higher than that of many American states. Its own prime minister has said that it has come of age and should wean itself off our aid within a decade. When the recipient himself says the subsidy is no longer necessary, the case for an unconditional one becomes hard to make.

[PAUSE]

To the third, that Israel was attacked: yes, it was, and the attack of October seventh was an atrocity. Nothing I have said tonight excuses it, and nothing I have said denies Israel's right to defend its people. But the right to self-defense is not the same thing as a right to American funding without conditions. America has laws, the Leahy laws and the Arms Export Control Act among them, that set conditions on the use of American weapons by every recipient. Asking that those laws be applied to everyone equally is not giving comfort to an enemy. It is the rule of law. A friend who asks for help defending his house can be helped. A friend who asks you never to look at what is done with your help is asking for something else.

To the fourth, on deterrence: I would say that nothing has weakened American credibility in the Middle East more than wars launched without a plan for how they end, and commitments made without the consent of the American people. Credibility does not come from blank checks. It comes from clear policies, openly debated, that the country is willing to sustain. A war that seventy-one percent of Americans wanted to avoid, fought without congressional authorization, with two-thirds of our interceptors gone in five months, is not a show of strength. It is a show of drift.

Consider, too, how the deterrence argument is used. It is used to justify the war, and then the extension of the war, and then the supplemental for the war, and then the co-production law, and then the next agreement. There is no point at which the deterrence argument says, "Enough; we are now sufficiently credible." An argument that can justify any amount of spending and any length of war is not an argument. It is an appetite.

And there is one more thing the deterrence argument misses. The country is changing its mind.

[SLOW]

Since two thousand and one, Gallup had found more Americans sympathizing with Israelis than with Palestinians. This February, that lead was gone. Thirty-six percent sympathized more with the Israelis; forty-one percent with the Palestinians. Gallup itself notes that the five-point gap is within the margin of error, so call it a tie, but a tie where there used to be a thirteen-point lead. Among Americans under thirty-five, a majority sympathized more with the Palestinians. Even among Republicans, sympathy for Israelis had fallen ten points since twenty twenty-four. And this was not a one-off. Last August, a Quinnipiac poll found sixty percent of Americans opposed sending more military support to Israel.

I do not cite that poll to say the public is right. Polls are not arguments. I cite it to say that a policy which is drifting further and further from the people who pay for it cannot survive by refusing to be examined. The surest way to lose public support for a relationship is to insist that it can never be questioned. The surest way to keep it is to make it accountable.

[PAUSE]

There is one more objection, and it is the one I take most seriously of all, because it is not about policy. It is about what speeches like this one can do in the world.

It goes like this. Antisemitism is rising, in America and around the world. Jewish Americans have been harassed, threatened and attacked. Synagogues need armed guards. And when a speaker stands up and talks about lobbies and money and influence and foreign loyalties, even carefully, even with disclaimers, he gives cover to people who are not careful at all. The words "Israel" and "lobby" and "Congress" in the same breath are, for some listeners, a dog whistle, whatever the speaker intends.

I hear that. I have tried all evening to earn the right to answer it.

My answer is this. The way to starve bigotry is not to make a public policy unspeakable. When a subject cannot be discussed in the open by responsible people, it does not disappear. It goes underground, to the places where the irresponsible people are waiting, and they get to frame it however they like. Silence is not a firewall against conspiracy theories. Silence is their fertilizer.

The answer to the bigot who says that a people secretly controls the government is a citizen who says: no, here is exactly how the policy is made. Here is the law, here is the vote, here is the money, here are the names of the committees, and here is the public record of who decided what. It is all ordinary, it is all American, and it can all be changed by ordinary American means. That is what I have tried to do tonight. Not to whisper, but to read the ledger out loud.

And I would add one thing more. Criticizing a government's policy, or our own government's policy toward it, is not hatred of a people. Americans criticize the policies of Britain and Mexico and Saudi Arabia and China without anyone imagining that they hate the British or the Mexicans. Israel, a proud and argumentative democracy whose own citizens criticize their government more fiercely than I ever could, deserves to be treated the same way: as a normal country, with a normal government, whose policies can be judged on their merits.

Treating Israel as a normal country is not an insult. It may be the most respectful thing we can do.

So I say to the friends of Israel in this room, and I hope there are many: the best thing you could do for the relationship you care about is to support the reforms I am about to propose. A partnership that can survive scrutiny is a partnership that can survive.

## XIV. Make It Official, or Make It Conditional

[BUILD]

So here we are, at the end of a long evening, and I return to the bill that arrives at your house every year.

If we are going to pay for it like a state, let us govern it like a state.

If we are going to fight its wars like a state, let us give its people a vote, and ourselves a vote, like a state.

If we are going to write its military edge into our statutes, stockpile our weapons on its soil and tie our factories to its factories, let us at least call the thing by its name.

And if we are not willing to do any of that, and of course we are not, then let us stop pretending that the present arrangement is anything other than what it is: the costs of a union with none of its accountability.

[PAUSE]

I have made you a modest proposal tonight. Annex Israel. Admit the fifty-first state. Seat its senators, tax its citizens, command its army and bring its every decision under our Constitution.

It is absurd. You know it is absurd. I know it is absurd. The Israelis know it is absurd, and would rightly reject it. But I hope you can now see why I made it. Because the only thing more absurd than my proposal is the arrangement it describes, and that arrangement is not a joke. It is our policy.

So let me close by putting down the satire and picking up the plain words, because Lincoln was right that plain words are the ones that last.

[SLOW]

We do not need to make it official. But we do need to make it conditional. And here is what that means.

It means that no American president should take this country to war with Iran, or anyone else, without a vote of Congress, cast in the open and binding in law. Not a symbolic resolution. A real one, with the President's signature or a veto to override.

It means that American military aid to Israel should come on the same terms as aid to everyone else. Paid as it is spent, not in a lump sum to earn interest. Subject to the same human rights laws and the same arms export laws that apply to every recipient, enforced the same way, and reported to the public.

It means that before the current agreement expires in twenty twenty-eight, Congress should hold real hearings, with real debate, on what the next one should look like, and whether there should be one at all, rather than renewing it by habit in an afternoon.

It means that if Section Two Nineteen becomes law, it should come with a sunset, a public report to Congress every year, and a clear definition of what co-production commits us to, so that a policy does not quietly become a permanent constituency.

[PAUSE]

It means that the United States Congress should, at long last, hold a full public investigation into the attack on the USS Liberty, and that every remaining document should be declassified while some of the survivors are still alive to read it. Thirty-four men died. Their country owes them a hearing.

It means that the remaining NUMEC files should be released, so that the people of Apollo, Pennsylvania, and everyone else, can know what the government knew.

It means that American officials should be allowed to speak honestly about the nuclear facts of the Middle East, so that our arguments about non-proliferation stop sounding like special pleading.

And it means equal rules for every lobby in America: full, fast disclosure of who funds every super PAC; no hidden donors behind committees with invented names; and no American losing her job for refusing to sign a political pledge about any foreign country.

[BUILD]

Before I finish, I want to bring back the people we met tonight, because abstractions do not ask for justice. People do.

Remember Commander McGonagle, bleeding on the bridge of a burning ship, keeping it afloat, and then receiving the highest honor his country could give in a side room at the Navy Yard, as though his courage were something to be kept quiet. And remember the thirty-four men who did not come home, whose families got a check and never got a hearing.

Remember the people of Apollo, Pennsylvania, still waiting, six decades on, for their land to be cleaned, and for their government to tell them plainly what happened to the uranium that passed through their town.

Remember Bahia Amawi, the speech pathologist, who wanted only to keep helping children find their words, and who was told she had to sign a pledge about a foreign country first.

Remember Thomas Massie, whatever you think of his politics, who asked for a vote on a war and a hearing for the Liberty, and who will be leaving Congress in January because he lost his primary.

[PAUSE]

And remember the twenty-one American service members killed in a war Congress never declared, and the families who received a folded flag from a government that could not muster a binding vote on whether they should have been there at all.

None of those people asked for annexation. None of them asked to merge anything. Every one of them asked, in one way or another, for the same thing: that their own government act as if it answered to them.

[BUILD]

None of these proposals singles out anyone because of who they are. Every one of them is a rule we would want applied to any foreign government, any lobby and any president. Every one of them is something the friends of Israel could support without betraying their friendship, and the critics of Israel could support without betraying their principles. Every one of them simply asks the United States to act like a self-governing republic.

That is all I am really asking for. Not annexation. Self-government. Ours.

[PAUSE]

The men who wrote our Constitution had a word for a people who pay for decisions they do not make, fight in wars they did not choose and are told that asking questions is disloyal. They called it subjection. They had just fought a revolution to escape it.

We are not subjects. We are citizens. And citizens have the right to read the bill before they pay it.

So go home tonight and do the most radical thing an American can do. Call your representative. Call both your senators. Ask them three plain questions.

Did you vote on this war?

Do you know what we are paying, and on what terms?

And would you accept, for any other country on earth, the arrangement you accept for this one?

If they cannot answer, then tell them you have a modest proposal. Tell them either to make it official, or to make it accountable.

[SLOW]

And if they ask you which, tell them you will settle for accountable.

[LET IT LAND]

Thank you, and good night.
