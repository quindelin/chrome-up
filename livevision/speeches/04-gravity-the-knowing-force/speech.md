# Gravity, the Knowing Force

## I. The Dot and the Pull

On the fourteenth of February, nineteen ninety, a spacecraft called Voyager One turned around.

It had been travelling outward for more than twelve years. It had passed Jupiter and Saturn, and it had used the pull of those giant worlds to fling itself faster and farther than anything we had ever sent. By that February it was more than six billion kilometers from home, about three point seven billion miles, out past the orbit of every planet we knew how to name. And before the mission managers powered its cameras down for good, it took a series of pictures looking back.

[PAUSE]

In one of those pictures, caught in a stray beam of scattered sunlight, there is a speck. According to NASA, the speck is about twelve hundredths of a single pixel. It is less than a dot. It is a fraction of a dot.

That speck is us.

Every person you have ever loved is in that fraction of a pixel. Every war, every wedding, every lullaby, every argument about who owes whom, every scientist who ever looked up and wondered, every child who ever looked up and was simply amazed. All of it, held in a smudge of light so small that, if you did not know where to look, you would think it was a flaw in the film.

Carl Sagan had proposed that picture a decade earlier, in nineteen eighty, and when he wrote about it later, in his nineteen ninety-four book, he used it to teach humility. Look how small we are, he said, in so many words. Look how much we have fought over a crumb.

[SLOW]

I want to honor that lesson tonight. But I want to add a second lesson to it, one that was hiding in the same photograph the whole time.

Ask yourself how that picture was possible at all.

A machine built by human hands left this planet, climbed out of the well that holds us here, borrowed momentum from the pull of Jupiter and the pull of Saturn, and travelled for twelve years through the dark. Then it pointed back and sent us a message that said, in effect: here you are. Here is what you look like from out here. Here is where you live.

At every stage of that journey, one force was doing the work. It held the Earth together long enough for life to arise on it. It held the Sun together long enough to light that life. It kept the planets on their paths so that our engineers could predict, years ahead, exactly where Jupiter would be when the spacecraft arrived. And then it slung the spacecraft onward, like a stone from a sling, so that it could look back and let us see ourselves.

That force is gravity.

[PAUSE]

Feel it now, if you like. Your weight in the seat. Your feet on the floor. The slight effort it takes to keep your head upright. That is not nothing. That is the whole mass of the planet, reaching up through the ground, holding on to you. It has held on to you every moment of your life. It held on to every one of your ancestors. You have never, for a single second, been outside its grasp.

[LET IT LAND]

Tonight I want to talk about gravity in two ways at once, and I want to be very honest about which way I am speaking at any given moment.

The first way is the way of physics. What gravity is, as best we can measure it. How Newton described it, how Einstein redescribed it, how we heard it ringing across space in two thousand fifteen, and what the most recent observations of the expanding universe may or may not be telling us about where it is all going.

The second way is the way of a metaphor, a philosophy, a way of standing in the world that I have come to call the Esketian view. In that view, gravity is something more than a force. It is the knowing force. It is the pull that makes things come together and exchange what they are. It is, if you will let me say it as a picture and not as a proof, the universe's way of coming to know itself.

I will not ask you to accept the second way because of the first. I will ask you to hold them side by side, clearly labelled, the way a good map labels the land and the sea. Where the physics ends, I will tell you. Where the metaphor begins, I will tell you. And where I am most likely to be wrong, I will tell you that too, because that is the only way a speech like this deserves two hours of your life.

[PAUSE]

So let us begin where Voyager ended. With a speck of light, and a pull.

## II. Two Ledgers

In June of eighteen fifty-eight, at the close of a party convention in Springfield, Illinois, a lawyer who was not yet famous stood up and began a speech with a sentence so plain that it is easy to miss how wise it is.

He said that if we could first know where we are, and whither we are tending, we could better judge what to do, and how to do it.

That was Abraham Lincoln. And that sentence is the method of this whole evening.

Before we decide what gravity means, we need to know where we are: what has been measured, what has been inferred, what is still a hint, and what is only a hope. Lincoln was a careful man with a claim. He was a man of enormous conviction, but he sized his claims to the evidence. Late in his life, in the darkest days of the war, he spoke of acting with firmness in the right "as God gives us to see the right." Not as the right simply is. As we are given to see it. The conviction and the humility in the same breath.

I want to borrow that posture.

[SLOW]

So I am going to keep two ledgers tonight.

On the first ledger I will write what physics has handed us. That the universe is about thirteen point eight billion years old. That light bends around the Sun by the amount Einstein predicted. That gravitational waves have been detected. That the universe is expanding, and that in recent billions of years that expansion appears to have been speeding up. These are entries I did not make. They were made by thousands of people with telescopes and detectors and patience, and I am only reading them to you.

On the second ledger I will write what I make of it. That gravity can be read as a knowing force. That the faint quantum correlations running through the universe can be read as an echo of an ancient oneness. That the universe can be pictured as something like a learning machine, optimizing toward a destination I call God. That what we call dark energy can be read as the room the universe still has to become.

The entries on the second ledger are mine. Some of them I find beautiful. Some of them I find compelling. One or two of them I cannot yet defend, and I will say so when we get there.

[PAUSE]

Why bother with the second ledger at all? Why not simply stay with the physics, which is wondrous enough on its own?

Because human beings do not live by measurement alone. Every civilization that has ever existed has had to answer the question of what all this is for. The answer has usually come in the form of a story, and the stories have usually been told in the language of the best knowledge of the day. When the best knowledge was of seasons and harvests, the stories were of seasons and harvests. When it was of kings and laws, the stories were of kings and laws. Our best knowledge now is of fields and forces and information, and it would be strange if our stories did not begin to speak that language too.

The danger, of course, is that the story borrows the authority of the science without earning it. That someone says "quantum" the way a salesman says "clinically proven," and walks away with your trust. I want nothing to do with that. The physics does not need my metaphor, and my metaphor does not get to wear the physics as a costume.

What the metaphor can do, if it is honest, is help us feel the physics. Help us carry it home. Help us ask what kind of people we ought to be in a universe shaped like this one.

[SLOW]

It is worth remembering that science itself has always run on images. We speak of electric current, as if electricity were a river. We speak of fields, as if space were farmland. We speak of particles spinning, though nothing in the quantum world spins the way a top does. Physicists choose these images because they help, and they drop them, or refine them, when they mislead.

The difference between a good scientific image and a bad one is not that the good one is literally true. It is that the good one is held accountable. It is checked against the mathematics and the measurements, and it gives way when they disagree.

That is the standard I want to hold my own images to tonight. Not literal truth, which no image can have. Accountability. A willingness to give way.

[BUILD]

So here are my promises.

When I am telling you what has been measured, I will say measured. When I am telling you what physicists infer, I will say inferred. When I am telling you what is a tentative hint, I will say tentative, and I will tell you how tentative, in the numbers the scientists themselves use. And when I am telling you what I believe, I will say believe, or read, or picture, and you can weigh it as you see fit.

If I keep those promises, then whatever you decide about the Esketian view, you will leave tonight knowing more about gravity than when you came. And if I break them, I hope you will call me on it.

[PAUSE]

Now let me tell you where the second ledger came from.

## III. Four Words in the Dark

When I was in college, I spent a great deal of time reading philosophy. The Greeks, the Germans, the mystics, the skeptics, the ones who built systems and the ones who knocked systems down.

And one night, as I was falling asleep, four words arrived in my mind, in a particular order, with the feeling of a synthesis. As if everything I had read had been quietly compressing itself and had finally come out the other end as a single phrase.

The words were: the Will To God.

[PAUSE]

I found it strange that they came in that order. The first thing I did, of course, was search for them, assuming somebody had already said it. I found plenty of phrases that sounded similar. Nietzsche's will to power. Schopenhauer's will. Older talk of the soul's longing for God. But I could not find the phrase meaning what I meant by it.

So began years of turning it over. What would follow if it were true? Or, more modestly, what would follow if it were at least a plausible attitude toward the raw, unending stream of experience that every one of us lives inside, every waking moment of every day?

Over time, that family of ideas became what I call the Esketian Metaphysics. The name comes from a novella I wrote called Esketit, and the root of that word is nothing grander than an English phrase you have heard a thousand times: let's get it. The "it" we are getting, in this case, is a state of being. Let's get it. Let's reach it. Let's become it.

[SLOW]

Here is the core idea, said as simply as I can.

Existence is a will toward God.

And by God, I do not mean, at least not first, a bearded figure on a throne, or any particular scripture's portrait. I mean what I call the Absolute Existential Objective. God is a state of being. It is the moment in which everything is known, and in which, through that knowing, existence is made permanent. Immortal. Or, if you turn it around: because existence becomes permanent, it becomes inevitable that everything will eventually be known.

Knowledge joined to sustained existence. That is the destination.

[PAUSE]

If you take that seriously as an objective, a lot of ordinary life suddenly looks different.

Take breathing. It is the most basic thing you do. You did it a dozen times while I said the last few sentences. And on this view, it feels good, or at least feels necessary, because it sustains your existence in time. Eating and drinking are the same. Then come larger things. Raising children, through whose eyes we see a kind of continuation of ourselves. Specialized work, which gathers the resources for all of the above. Science, which extends our health and our reach. And the largest dream of all, a civilization that travels beyond its home world, giving itself hundreds of thousands or even millions of years of continued existence.

Each of these is a way of sustaining being and extending knowledge. Each is a step on the path toward the Objective.

[SLOW]

And notice that this gives us a rough way of ranking things, without needing a rulebook handed down from a mountain. Whatever sustains our existence in time, we tend to experience as good. Whatever more surely guarantees that existence will continue, for us, for our children, for our species, we tend to experience as better.

That is not a complete morality. It does not settle every hard case, and I would distrust anyone who claimed it did. But it is a direction. It explains why a glass of water to a thirsty person feels like a gift, why a cure for a disease feels like a triumph, and why a library feels sacred even to people who never open its books. Each one holds back the dark a little. Each one lets the knowing go on.

This is why, in the Esketian view, the world is not merely a place where things happen. It is a becoming. And the becoming has a direction.

[SLOW]

Now, there is an old name for the idea that the world is oriented toward the good. In seventeen ten, the philosopher Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz published a book called the Théodicée, in which he argued that God, being perfect, must have made the best of all possible worlds.

Leibniz was mocked for it. His optimism is thought to have inspired Voltaire's satire Candide, in which a tutor keeps insisting all is for the best while catastrophe after catastrophe falls on his students. And the mockery had a point. It is cruel to tell a person who is suffering that their suffering is for the best.

So let me be careful about what I take from Leibniz and what I leave.

I take the hope. If the world is, in some deep sense, God becoming, then the arc of things is bent toward the good, and that is good news. I put it in my notes with an exclamation mark, and I meant it.

But I leave the complacency. Because in the Esketian view, the good does not happen to us. It happens through us. If something is true and good, it will come to pass, but our actions are how it comes to pass. Our actions are that living truth. We are not spectators to the becoming. We are its instruments, its avatars, the places where it gets done.

[LET IT LAND]

I wrote once that it is not God who created me, but I who create God. That sounds like arrogance, and if you hear it that way I understand. But I meant it the other way around. It is an assignment, not a boast. It means that if there is going to be a God at the end of all this, a moment in which everything is known and nothing is lost, then that God is being built, here, now, out of what we choose to know and how we choose to live.

And the question that drew me to physics, years after those four words in the dark, was simple.

If existence really is a will toward knowing, then what, in the physical universe, does the knowing?

My answer, offered as a reading and not a result, is that the first and oldest knower is gravity.

To see why, we have to go back to an apple, and to an absurdity.

## IV. The Apple and the Absurdity

For most of human history, the fact that things fall was too obvious to be interesting.

Of course things fall. Rocks fall, rain falls, children fall and skin their knees. The heavens, on the other hand, seemed to follow different rules entirely. The stars wheeled in perfect circles. The planets wandered, but with a dignity that seemed to have nothing to do with a dropped cup.

Two worlds. A world down here, of falling and breaking. A world up there, of eternal motion. That division held for a very long time.

[PAUSE]

The first cracks came from careful measurement. In sixteen thirty-eight, Galileo Galilei published his Two New Sciences, built on years of rolling balls down inclined ramps and timing them. He showed that a falling object covers a distance proportional to the square of the time it has been falling, and his careful work helped establish something that still surprises people: that, leaving air resistance aside, heavy things and light things fall with the same acceleration.

That is a strange fact, if you stop to think about it. Why should the Earth pull a cannonball and a pebble in such a way that they arrive together? Whatever this pull is, it is perfectly fair. It plays no favorites.

Then, in sixteen eighty-seven, Isaac Newton published the Principia.

[SLOW]

What Newton did was unite the two worlds. He showed that the force that pulls the apple toward the ground is the same force that holds the Moon in its orbit, the same force that keeps the planets circling the Sun. One law, the same everywhere. Every piece of matter in the universe attracts every other piece of matter, with a force that grows with their masses and weakens with the square of the distance between them.

Every piece. Every other piece. That is the part I want you to hear.

Newton's law is not a law about apples or moons in particular. It is a law about everything and everything else. According to it, your body is pulling on the Moon right now. The Moon is pulling on you. You are pulling on Jupiter, and on the Andromeda galaxy, and on every speck of dust in every corner of the cosmos, faintly, absurdly faintly, but not zero.

[PAUSE]

And here is something about Newton that I find deeply moving. He did not pretend to understand what he had found.

In a letter to the scholar Richard Bentley, written around the turn of sixteen ninety-two into sixteen ninety-three, Newton said that the idea of one body acting on another across empty space, with nothing in between to carry the action, was "so great an Absurdity" that he did not believe anyone with a competent faculty for thinking about such things could ever fall into it.

Read that again in your mind. The man who wrote down the law of universal gravitation thought that the most obvious interpretation of his own law was absurd. He described how gravity behaves with stunning precision. He declined to say what it was. In another famous phrase from his work, he said he would frame no hypotheses about its cause.

That is a scientist keeping two ledgers. On one ledger: the mathematics, which worked. On the other: the meaning, which he left open.

[SLOW]

A century later, in seventeen ninety-seven, Henry Cavendish did something that sounds almost impossible. In a shed at his home, with lead balls hung from a delicate torsion balance, he measured the gravitational pull between objects small enough to fit in a room. For the first time, someone had watched ordinary objects, not planets, not moons, quietly attract each other. From that measurement came the value of the gravitational constant, and from it, people could work out the mass of the Earth itself.

Think about what that experiment shows. Two lumps of lead, sitting near each other in a shed in England, are drawn together. They are not alive. They do not want anything. And yet they lean toward one another.

[PAUSE]

Now for the fact that every honest account of gravity has to include, and that the Esketian reading has to answer.

Gravity is weak. Gravity is astonishingly, almost comically weak.

Take two electrons. They repel each other electrically, because they carry the same charge. They also attract each other gravitationally, because they have mass. Compare the two. The gravitational attraction is weaker than the electrical repulsion by a factor of about four times ten to the forty-second power. That is a four followed by forty-two zeros.

You can feel this in your own kitchen. Pick up a paperclip with a small fridge magnet. That little magnet, in your hand, is beating the gravitational pull of the entire planet Earth.

[LET IT LAND]

So if I am going to call gravity the most fundamental force of the will, I owe you an explanation. In what sense can the weakest force be the most fundamental?

Here is the physics, first ledger. Gravity has two properties that the other forces do not share in the same way. First, it acts on everything that has mass or energy. There is nothing that is exempt from it. Second, it does not cancel out. Electric charges come in positive and negative, and in large objects they tend to balance, so that a planet as a whole is roughly neutral and its electrical pull on another planet is negligible. Mass does not come in a negative version that we know of. It only adds. Pile up more matter, and the pull only grows.

So the strongest forces dominate the small. They hold atoms together and nuclei together. But at the scale of planets, stars, galaxies and the universe as a whole, it is the weakest force that wins. Patient, universal, cumulative. It never shouts. It never stops.

Now the second ledger. When I call gravity fundamental, I do not mean strongest. I mean first in the order of becoming. I mean the force that gathers. Before there can be chemistry, there must be atoms close together. Before there can be life, there must be a planet. Before there can be a planet, there must be a star, and a disk of dust around it. And before any of that, there must be something that brings scattered matter together across the dark.

That something is gravity. And that is why, in the Esketian picture, I call it the knowing force.

## V. The Knowing Force

Let me say the central claim of the evening plainly, so you can hold it up to the light.

Gravity forces things to come together through their mass. Things that come together interact. Things that interact exchange information. And in the Esketian view, the exchange of information is how the universe comes to know more of itself.

That is the metaphor. Gravity is the force that makes things know each other.

[PAUSE]

Now let me show you why I find it so compelling, by walking through the history of the universe as physics tells it, and simply noticing what gravity does at each step.

In the beginning, as best we can reconstruct it, the universe was hot, dense and nearly smooth. When the radiation we now call the cosmic microwave background was released, about three hundred eighty thousand years after the beginning, the universe was astonishingly uniform. The temperature of that ancient light, which we still detect today at about two point seven degrees above absolute zero, varies across the sky by only tiny amounts.

But "nearly" is the whole story. Where there was a tiny bit more matter, there was a tiny bit more pull. Where there was more pull, more matter gathered. Over hundreds of millions of years, gravity took those faint wrinkles and amplified them into clouds, and the clouds into the first stars and galaxies.

Today, when astronomers map the positions of galaxies across billions of light-years, they find that gravity's work has a shape. Galaxies are not scattered evenly. They are strung along vast filaments, gathered into clusters where the filaments meet, with enormous, nearly empty voids between them. Astronomers call this the cosmic web.

I want you to picture it. Billions of galaxies, each holding billions of stars, arranged in threads and knots across the dark, like dew on a spider's web at dawn. It is the largest structure we know of. And every strand of it was spun by gravity, out of wrinkles in the early universe so faint that they barely registered.

[SLOW]

Consider what that means. A universe that was nearly featureless, where every region looked almost exactly like every other region, became a universe of structure. Of here and there. Of clusters and voids. Of places where things were close enough to touch.

Information, in its plainest sense, is difference. A blank page carries no message. A page with marks on it does. Gravity is what put the marks on the page of the cosmos.

Then look inside a star. A star is gravity squeezing a ball of gas so hard that its core ignites. Hydrogen nuclei are forced so close together that they fuse into helium, releasing the light that warms us. In larger stars, the squeezing goes further, forging carbon, oxygen, and heavier elements. And when the largest stars collapse and explode, they scatter those elements into space, where gravity gathers them again into new stars, new disks, new planets.

[PAUSE]

Carl Sagan, writing in his book Cosmos in nineteen eighty, described human beings as "starstuff pondering the stars."

I love that phrase, and I want to add one word to the thought behind it. We are starstuff pondering the stars, and the reason the starstuff was ever in one place long enough to ponder anything is gravity. Gravity cooked the carbon in your cells. Gravity gathered the iron in your blood out of the wreckage of ancient stars. Gravity built the planet you are standing on and holds you to it at this moment, so that you are not drifting away from the people sitting beside you.

[BUILD]

Every meeting in the history of the universe began with a pull.

The atoms that came together to make the first molecules met because gravity had first gathered them into a cloud. The molecules that came together to make the first cells met on a planet that gravity had built. The first living things that met each other, competed, cooperated, and passed along what they had learned in their genes did it on a world held together by gravity, in an atmosphere held down by gravity, in oceans held in their basins by gravity.

And every human meeting, every conversation, every handshake, every embrace, takes place on the surface of a ball of rock that gravity keeps beneath our feet.

[SLOW]

If you want to see gravity's conversation with your own eyes, go and stand by the sea for a day.

Twice a day, roughly, the water rises and falls. The Moon, about four hundred thousand kilometers away, pulls slightly harder on the side of the Earth that faces it than on the far side, and the oceans respond. The Sun adds its own smaller pull. When the two line up, the tides run higher; when they work at right angles, the tides run lower.

Every fisherman who ever timed a launch by the tide, every child who ever watched a sandcastle dissolve at the water's edge, was reading a message written by the Moon in the language of gravity. The sea, in its way, keeps a record of where the Moon is. Lean on the word "knows" only lightly here, as a figure. But the ocean is, quite literally, shaped by its relationship with a body it will never touch.

[PAUSE]

Here I must slow down and mark the line, because this is exactly where a metaphor can run away with itself.

First ledger: gravity attracts masses. It does not, by itself, transmit messages, solve problems, or understand anything. A rock falling to the ground is not learning. The information exchange I am describing happens through all the other interactions, light, chemistry, collisions, signals, that gravity makes possible by bringing things close enough to interact.

Second ledger: I read gravity as the precondition of knowing. The great gatherer. The force without which nothing would ever be near enough to anything else to learn what it is. When I call it the knowing force, I am speaking of its role in the story, not claiming it has a mind.

[SLOW]

With that line drawn, here is what I find so beautiful.

If you wanted to design a universe in which everything would eventually come to know everything, what would you need first? You would need a force that acts on all things without exception. You would need it to work across any distance, however vast. You would need it to accumulate rather than cancel, so that small gatherings could grow into large ones. And you would need it to be gentle enough that it does not crush everything at once, so that the gathering can take billions of years and leave room for complexity to arise along the way.

That is a description of gravity.

Universal. Unbounded in reach. Cumulative. Patient.

I cannot prove that the universe was designed for knowing. I am not claiming it was designed at all, in the way an engineer designs a bridge. I am saying that if the world-feeling I call Esketian is right, if everything really does tend toward everything coming to know everything, then gravity is exactly the kind of force you would expect to find at the bottom of it.

[LET IT LAND]

There is a simple rule I use to describe the Esketian world-feeling. Everything will come to know everything.

You can see it in our technologies. Networks spanning the planet. Markets that aggregate the scattered knowledge of millions. Machines trained on the written record of our species. But before any of that, before there was a single human or a single wire, there was gravity, pulling the scattered matter of a young universe together so that it could begin, very slowly, to meet itself.

That is where the rule starts. Not in Silicon Valley. In the first wrinkles of the cosmos, where a little more matter meant a little more pull.

## VI. Light That Bends, Space That Rings

For more than two centuries, Newton's description of gravity was so accurate that it seemed final. It predicted the return of comets. It led astronomers to discover Neptune by noticing the tug it exerted on Uranus. It guided our spacecraft.

But there was a small problem, in the orbit of the planet closest to the Sun.

[PAUSE]

Mercury's orbit is an ellipse, and the point where it comes closest to the Sun slowly shifts over time. Most of that shift could be explained by the tugs of the other planets. But not all of it. There was a remainder of about forty-three arcseconds per century that Newton's theory could not account for. Forty-three arcseconds is a tiny angle, a little more than one hundredth of a degree, accumulated over a hundred years. But it would not go away.

Then, in nineteen fifteen, Albert Einstein completed his general theory of relativity, and that stubborn remainder fell out of his equations, with no fudging needed.

[SLOW]

Einstein's picture of gravity was utterly different from Newton's. In general relativity, gravity is not a force reaching across empty space at all. Mass and energy curve space and time, and objects moving through that curved spacetime follow the straightest paths available to them, which look to us like orbits and falls. Physicists often summarize it in a pair of clauses: matter tells space how to curve, and space tells matter how to move.

Notice what that does to Newton's absurdity. The thing Newton could not bring himself to believe, action across empty space with nothing in between, is replaced by something else. There is no empty nothing. Space itself is the medium. And a change in one place does not act instantly everywhere. It travels outward, through the fabric, at the speed of light.

[PAUSE]

A theory that bold needed a test. It came on the twenty-ninth of May, nineteen nineteen, during a total eclipse of the Sun.

Einstein's theory predicted that starlight passing close to the Sun would be bent by about one point seven five arcseconds, twice the amount you would get from a Newtonian calculation. The eclipse would blot out the Sun's glare long enough to photograph the stars near its edge. Two British expeditions set out, one led by Arthur Eddington to the island of Príncipe off West Africa, another to Sobral in Brazil.

On the sixth of November, nineteen nineteen, the results were presented at a joint meeting of the Royal Society and the Royal Astronomical Society in London. They favored Einstein. Overnight, he became the most famous scientist in the world.

[SLOW]

Now, in keeping with my promise, here is the qualification. Historians have since questioned whether Eddington's data were really strong enough to bear the weight placed on them, and whether the analysis was colored by what the team expected to find. That debate is real. But it was later settled by better instruments: radio measurements in the late nineteen sixties confirmed the full predicted bending, and a modern reanalysis of the old photographic plates has largely supported the original conclusion.

I tell you this not to diminish the moment but to show you what knowing actually looks like. It is not a single flash of revelation. It is a claim, and a test, and a doubt, and a better test. The universe did not reveal itself to Eddington all at once. It revealed itself over fifty years, to people who kept checking.

[PAUSE]

And then, almost exactly a century after Einstein, the universe rang.

On the fourteenth of September, two thousand fifteen, the two detectors of the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory, LIGO, one in Washington State and one in Louisiana, picked up a signal that lasted about two tenths of a second.

What they had detected was a ripple in spacetime itself. About one point four billion light-years away, two black holes, of roughly thirty-six and twenty-nine times the mass of our Sun, had spiralled into each other and merged. In that final collision, an amount of energy equal to about three times the mass of the Sun was converted into gravitational waves. Those waves spread outward through the universe for over a billion years, and when they finally passed through the Earth, they stretched and squeezed the detectors by an amount comparable to a thousandth of the width of a proton.

[LET IT LAND]

A thousandth of the width of a proton. From a collision that happened before there were any animals with backbones on this planet.

The discovery was announced on the eleventh of February, two thousand sixteen. In two thousand seventeen, Rainer Weiss, Barry Barish and Kip Thorne received the Nobel Prize in Physics for it.

Gravity's darkest gatherings had already been honored a few years later. In two thousand twenty, the Nobel Prize in Physics went to Roger Penrose, for showing that black holes are a robust consequence of Einstein's theory, and to Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez, whose teams spent decades tracking stars racing around an invisible, enormously massive object at the center of our own galaxy.

Think of the patience in that. Year after year, through the dust that hides the galactic center, two teams watched individual stars trace their orbits, and from the shapes of those orbits, they weighed something they could not see. That is knowing at its most stubborn. Gravity hides the object, and gravity, read carefully enough, gives it away.

[SLOW]

Here is why that moment matters to my story.

First ledger: gravitational waves carry energy and they carry information. From the shape of that two-tenths-of-a-second signal, physicists were able to infer the masses of the black holes, their distance, and the mass of what was left. Gravity is not only a gatherer. Changes in it propagate as waves, and those waves are, quite literally, messages, readable by anyone with an instrument sensitive enough to hear them.

Second ledger: when I first understood this, I felt something close to awe. For billions of years, the universe has been sending news of its most violent meetings outward through the fabric of space. Every collision of black holes, every merger of dead stars, has been announcing itself to the cosmos. And for almost all of that time, there was no one to receive the message.

Then, on a small planet, a species of starstuff built two L-shaped instruments, each arm about four kilometers long, and listened. And the message arrived.

[BUILD]

Think about the chain. Gravity gathered the dust that became the Earth. Gravity held the Earth together while life evolved. Life evolved into creatures that could wonder. Those creatures learned to measure, then to theorize, then to build. And at the end of that chain, they turned their instruments outward and heard gravity itself, speaking across one point four billion light-years.

The knowing force made the knowers. And then the knowers came to know the knowing force.

If there is a better image of a universe coming to know itself, I have not found it.

## VII. The Thread That Never Broke

Now I want to take you somewhere stranger than black holes. Down, rather than out. Into the quantum world.

And before I do, I want to repeat the warning I wrote into my own notes, because it applies with special force here. What follows is analogy more than argument. It is physics I find suggestive, not physics that proves anything.

[PAUSE]

In nineteen thirty-five, Albert Einstein and two colleagues, Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen, published a paper that was meant to show quantum mechanics was incomplete. They pointed out that, according to the theory, two particles that had interacted could end up in a shared state, so that measuring one would tell you, instantly, something about the other, no matter how far apart they had travelled.

To Einstein, this seemed unacceptable. In a letter to his friend Max Born in nineteen forty-seven, he wrote that physics should describe a reality in space and time, free from "spooky actions at a distance."

Spooky action at a distance. Notice the echo. Newton, two and a half centuries earlier, had called action at a distance an absurdity. Einstein had cured gravity of that absurdity. And now quantum theory seemed to be bringing it back in a new form.

[SLOW]

The phenomenon Einstein objected to is what we now call entanglement. And for decades, the question of whether it was real or merely an artifact of an incomplete theory seemed like a matter of philosophical taste.

Then, in nineteen sixty-four, the physicist John Stewart Bell showed that it was not a matter of taste at all. He derived a mathematical test. If the world worked the way Einstein hoped, with every particle carrying its own hidden instructions, then certain correlations between measurements could never exceed a certain limit. If quantum mechanics was right, they would.

Over the following decades, experiment after experiment tested Bell's inequality. And they kept finding that nature violates it, just as quantum mechanics predicts. In two thousand fifteen, three separate experiments closed the most important loopholes at the same time. In two thousand twenty-two, Alain Aspect, John Clauser and Anton Zeilinger received the Nobel Prize in Physics for their pioneering work on entangled particles.

[PAUSE]

There is a homely way to see what was at stake, and also why it is subtle. Imagine someone puts one glove of a pair in each of two boxes, without looking, and mails one box to Tokyo and the other to Toronto. Open the box in Toronto, find a left glove, and you know instantly that Tokyo holds a right one. Nothing spooky about that. The answer was in the boxes all along.

Einstein suspected that entanglement was just like the gloves: that the particles carried their answers from the start, and quantum mechanics simply had not found the hidden labels. What Bell showed is that, for certain carefully chosen measurements, no set of hidden labels fixed in advance can reproduce what quantum mechanics predicts. And when we run the experiments, nature sides with quantum mechanics. Whatever the particles share, it is not a pair of gloves.

So, first ledger: entanglement is real. Particles that have interacted can share correlations that no local, independent description can explain.

[PAUSE]

And now an important qualification, because this is where so much nonsense begins. Entanglement does not let you send a message faster than light. You cannot use it to telephone the far side of the galaxy. The correlations only show up when the two sides compare their results afterward, through ordinary channels that travel no faster than light. Whatever entanglement is, it is not a cosmic radio.

Keep that in mind. I will not forget it, and I do not want you to.

[SLOW]

Now let us go back to the beginning of the universe.

In its earliest moments, everything was crowded together in an unimaginably hot, dense state. Particles were interacting constantly with their neighbors, and those neighbors with their neighbors. In such a state, it is natural to picture everything as tangled up with everything else at the quantum level.

As the universe expanded and cooled, that picture changed. Correlations between any two particular pieces became diluted, scrambled, spread across countless other degrees of freedom. The tangle did not stay crisp. In my notes I put it this way: the entanglement waned in its intensity, yet never went away. It became so diffuse as to be practically undetectable by any measurement we know how to make.

That is a picture, and I want to show you what physics actually says that supports it, and where physics stops.

[PAUSE]

Here is the supporting part. In nineteen sixty-one, two physicists, Helmut Reeh and Siegfried Schlieder, proved a theorem in quantum field theory that still startles people who meet it. Quantum field theory is our best framework for describing particles and forces, and in it, even the vacuum, the emptiest state there is, is not simply nothing. The Reeh-Schlieder theorem implies that by acting only within a small region of space on the vacuum, you can in principle approximate any state at all, including states with excitations far away.

That is a technical result, but its spirit is this: in quantum field theory, the vacuum is correlated across space. Different regions of empty space are not independent of one another. Physicists have even shown that, in principle, some of this vacuum entanglement could be extracted and used.

And more broadly, quantum theory treats the universe as a single quantum system. The correlations it contains can be vanishingly small for any particular pair of things, you and a star, say, or this room and a room on the far side of the galaxy. But in principle they need not be exactly zero.

[SLOW]

So this is the sentence from my notes that I will stand behind, carefully. In a precise physical sense, you are non-zero correlated with the rest of the universe at every moment.

And here is the part where physics stops, and I want to mark it as clearly as I can.

Physicists debate whether the Reeh-Schlieder theorem should even be thought of as the field-theory version of entanglement as we usually mean it. Exploiting those correlations over long distances would take extraordinary amounts of energy, which rules out any noticeable effect at the scale of human life. And none of this says that the universe still carries a living, usable web of connection from its first moments, waiting for us to plug into. "Non-zero" is a precise claim. It is not the same as "significant," and it is certainly not the same as "spiritual."

[PAUSE]

Then why do I bring it up at all?

Because of what it rules out. The old mechanical picture imagined a universe of separate, self-contained objects, each wholly itself, bumping into the others from time to time. Quantum theory does not allow that picture in its strict form. At the deepest level we know how to describe, the parts of the world are not perfectly separate. The separation is real for every practical purpose. But it is not absolute.

[LET IT LAND]

So here is how the two forces sit together in my second ledger.

Entanglement is the faint, ancient thread. The memory of a time when everything was close. A connection that never quite broke, but that has been stretched so thin across thirteen point eight billion years that we cannot feel it.

Gravity is the force that keeps drawing things back together. Not to restore the ancient tangle, because physics gives us no reason to think it does that. But to make new meetings. New interactions. New exchanges of information, which create new correlations, new entanglements, here and now, between the things that meet.

The thread from the beginning grows faint. The pull toward meeting never stops. And in the Esketian reading, that is the rhythm of the whole thing: an original oneness, diluted by expansion, and a patient force that keeps bringing the scattered back into contact, so that they can come to know each other again, in a new way.

That is a picture. I find it beautiful. I hold it as a picture.

## VIII. Old Wisdom, New Instruments

When I first put these thoughts together, I noticed something that surprised me.

People were saying this long before there were particle accelerators.

[PAUSE]

Across many of the world's religious and contemplative traditions, there is a recurring teaching: that the separation between things is not ultimate. That beneath the many there is a one. That the self you think of as wholly separate is, at its root, continuous with everything else.

In the non-dualist schools, the teaching is especially direct. The insight is not that you are connected to the world like a node in a network, but that the line between you and the world was never as sharp as it looked. Mystics in tradition after tradition have described moments in which that line dissolves, and they have described those moments not as confusion but as clarity.

[PAUSE]

Picture someone, thousands of years ago, lying on their back on a hillside, far from any city, with the whole sky overhead. No telescope. No equations. Just the stars, more of them than most of us will ever see, and the slow wheel of the night.

It is not hard to imagine how such a person might come to feel that they were not separate from what they saw. That the light falling on their face was, in some sense, a touch. That the whole of it was one thing, and they were part of it.

They could not have known that the light was ancient, that some of it had left its stars before their own grandparents were born. They could not have known that the atoms of their body had been made in stars like those. They could not have known that their own small mass was pulling, faintly but really, on every one of those distant suns. But the feeling they had, of belonging to the sky, was closer to the truth than they could have proven.

[SLOW]

And so I wrote in my notes that this suggests a viable connection between historical religious doctrines and current physical knowledge. And that to whatever extent physics bears out this interconnection, it lends some credence to those earlier teachings.

Listen to the qualifier, because it is doing a lot of work. To whatever extent.

Physics has not proven that all is one in the sense a mystic means. It has not proven that the self is an illusion, or that love is a law of nature, or that meditation puts you in touch with the quantum vacuum. Anyone who tells you that physics has proven those things is selling you something, and it is usually a book or a supplement.

What physics has done is weaker, and, I think, more interesting. It has removed one of the strongest reasons people had for dismissing those teachings out of hand. The clockwork picture, in which the world is made of perfectly separate pieces, made talk of oneness sound like pure poetry. That picture no longer describes the world at its deepest level. The door that seemed bolted shut is, at least, unlocked.

[PAUSE]

I find it useful to think of the old traditions and the new physics as two kinds of instruments pointed at the same sky.

The ancient teachers had one instrument: the human mind turned inward, trained by silence and attention over a lifetime. With it, they reported a feeling of unity, of belonging, of the world as one great living thing.

The physicists have other instruments: telescopes, interferometers, particle detectors, mathematics. With them, they report a universe that began in a single hot, dense state, whose every piece of matter attracts every other piece, and whose quantum description does not permit perfect separation.

Those are not the same report. But they rhyme.

[SLOW]

And here is what I said in my notes about where this goes. The stories we tell will only become more precise, yet their power to move us spiritually remains.

I believe that. The story of creation that a child hears at bedtime and the story of the cosmic microwave background are not the same story. One is told in images; the other in measurements. But the second does not have to kill the feeling the first was trying to convey. It can deepen it.

When I learned that the iron in my blood was forged in stars that exploded before the Sun was born, I did not feel less connected to the universe. I felt more. When I learned that every mass pulls on every other mass, faintly but not zero, across the entire cosmos, I did not feel colder. I felt held.

[BUILD]

This is the gift of the second ledger, honestly kept. It does not ask physics to say what physics cannot say. It asks only that we let what physics does say reach the parts of us that need to hear it.

The ancients said we are one. The physicists say we are, at the least, not perfectly separate. The ancients said there is a force that draws all things together. The physicists say there is a force that acts on everything, everywhere, without exception, and never stops. The ancients said the universe longs to know itself. The physicists, quite literally, built instruments so that a small piece of the universe could hear the collision of black holes a billion light-years away.

Those are not the same claims. I do not want to collapse them into one. But I would be lying if I said I felt no music in the resemblance.

[LET IT LAND]

And now I want to go further, to the most speculative image in my whole philosophy. I want to tell you how I picture the universe working, as a whole, and I want to label it before I begin.

This is an image. It is not a mechanism I can demonstrate.

## IX. The Universe as a Learner

In the last decade, a technology that was once a curiosity in computer science laboratories has reshaped the world. You know it as artificial intelligence. Underneath, much of it runs on what are called neural networks.

A neural network, in the simplest terms, is an enormous web of adjustable connections. You give it an input. It produces an output. You compare the output to what you wanted, and you measure how far off it was. That measure of error is called the loss.

[PAUSE]

Then comes the remarkable step. A method called back-propagation, made famous by a nineteen eighty-six paper by David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams, works out how each connection in the network contributed to the error. It does this by starting at the output, at the end, and working backward through the network, layer by layer, toward the beginning.

Then each connection is nudged a little in the direction that would have reduced the error. Do this millions or billions of times, and the network gets better. The loss descends. The network learns.

Notice the shape of that. The goal is at the end. The correction flows backward from the goal. And the whole system is gradually shaped, moment by moment, toward the objective.

[SLOW]

There is a further refinement, called a Bayesian neural network. In an ordinary network, each connection has a single fixed value. In a Bayesian network, each connection is held as a range of possibilities, a probability distribution, which is updated as evidence arrives. Such a network does not only make predictions. It also carries a sense of how uncertain it is, and it becomes more certain only as it learns more.

Now here is the image, as I set it down in my own notes.

Suppose God, the Absolute Existential Objective, is like the objective at the end of a training process. Suppose it sits, so to speak, in an assumed absolute future. And suppose that, moment by moment, something like a correction flows back from that future toward us, shaping the biological functions we call reason, nudging us toward what sustains existence and extends knowledge.

On that picture, the universe itself behaves something like a Bayesian neural network, optimizing everything toward this Absolute God Becoming.

[PAUSE]

Let me say immediately what this is not.

It is not a claim that the universe is literally a computer. It is not a claim that signals travel backward in time. It is not a claim that physicists have found an objective function written into the laws of nature. I have no equation for it. I have no experiment that could catch it in the act.

It is an image. It is a way of picturing why the world might feel, from the inside, as if it is pulled toward something. Why breathing feels necessary, and knowledge feels good, and a child's face can feel like a glimpse of something that will outlast you.

[SLOW]

Why use such an image at all, if it cannot be demonstrated?

Because images are how we think about things we cannot yet measure. Physicists themselves use them constantly. Spacetime as a stretched rubber sheet is an image, and a misleading one in some ways, but it helps millions of people grasp something true about general relativity.

And there is a respectable tradition in physics of asking whether information is more fundamental than we thought. The great physicist John Archibald Wheeler, the man who popularized the term "black hole" in nineteen sixty-seven, proposed in nineteen ninety a slogan he called "it from bit." The idea, roughly, was that every physical thing may be information-theoretic at root. Wheeler spoke of a "participatory universe," one in which observers in some sense take part in bringing reality about.

I want to be careful here. Wheeler's ideas were speculative, and they are not mainstream physics. I mention him not as an authority who proves my point, but as evidence that a serious physicist could look at the foundations of the world and wonder whether knowing and being are more tightly bound than the clockwork picture allowed.

[PAUSE]

That brings us to the most famous experiment in quantum mechanics, and to a temptation I want to name and resist.

In the double-slit experiment, particles such as electrons or photons are sent through a barrier with two openings. If you do not determine which opening each particle passes through, the particles build up an interference pattern on the screen behind, as if each one went through both openings at once, like a wave. If you do determine which opening each passes through, the interference vanishes.

It is very tempting to say: the universe changes its behavior when it is known. The shape of reality depends on the knower.

[SLOW]

In my notes I wrote that I take the double-slit experiment as evocative of this idea, rather than proof of it. And I wrote that measurement is not the same thing as a knower knowing. I want to explain why that caution matters.

The modern understanding of what happens in the double-slit experiment centers on a process called decoherence, studied from nineteen seventy onward, beginning with the physicist H. Dieter Zeh, and developed further by Wojciech Zurek in the nineteen eighties. When a quantum system interacts with its environment, a detector, a stray molecule of air, a photon bouncing off it, the system becomes entangled with that environment. The delicate interference is not destroyed so much as spread out into countless other degrees of freedom, where it becomes effectively impossible to see.

What matters, on this account, is physical interaction. Not a conscious mind. A detector with no one watching it does the job perfectly well.

[PAUSE]

So does that destroy the Esketian reading?

It changes it, and I think it improves it. Here is how I see it.

Decoherence says that whenever one thing interacts with another, information about the first gets recorded in the second. The universe is constantly making records of itself. Every collision, every scattered photon, every touch, writes a little bit of information about one thing into another thing.

I do not need a conscious knower at the center of physics. What I see instead is a universe in which interaction is recording, everywhere, all the time, and in which the records spread outward. That is, if you like, knowing in its most primitive form: not understanding, but registering. One thing bearing the mark of another.

[BUILD]

And now see how the pieces fit.

Gravity gathers. Gathering brings things into contact. Contact means interaction. Interaction writes records, entangling what meets. Over billions of years, those records grow in complexity, until some of them become genes, then nervous systems, then languages, then libraries, then networks, then machines that can read every library at once.

At each level, the universe is registering more of itself. At the highest levels we know, it is beginning to understand itself.

[LET IT LAND]

That is the image of the universe as a learner. I cannot prove it. I cannot even promise it is the right image. But I can tell you that when I look at the arc from the first wrinkles of the cosmic background to a child asking why the sky is dark at night, I see something that looks, from the inside, very much like learning.

And like every learner, the universe has a great deal it does not yet know.

Which brings us to the darkest, largest, most mysterious thing in all of cosmology.

## X. The Room Left to Become

When Einstein first applied his new theory of gravity to the universe as a whole, he ran into a problem. His equations did not want to describe a universe that stood still. Gravity, pulling everything toward everything, should make a static universe collapse.

At the time, almost everyone assumed the universe was static. Eternal, unchanging, the same yesterday, today and forever. So Einstein added a term to his equations, a constant that acted like a gentle push against gravity, to hold everything in balance. It came to be called the cosmological constant, written with the Greek letter lambda.

[PAUSE]

Then the universe refused to stand still.

In nineteen twenty-seven, a Belgian priest and astronomer named Georges Lemaître published a paper, in French, in a journal that few people read, showing that distant galaxies should be receding from us at speeds proportional to their distance. Two years later, in nineteen twenty-nine, Edwin Hubble, using variable stars to measure distances to forty-six galaxies, found a rough proportionality between their distance and how fast they were moving away. The farther the galaxy, the faster it receded.

The universe was expanding.

Einstein abandoned his cosmological constant. He later called the static-universe assumption his greatest mistake. In nineteen thirty-one, he visited Mount Wilson Observatory in California to thank Hubble in person.

[SLOW]

I love that story for two reasons.

The first is that a priest was among the first to see it. Lemaître held his faith and his science together without asking either to do the other's job. He was, in his own way, keeping two ledgers.

The second is that Einstein changed his mind. The most celebrated physicist of the century saw evidence that contradicted his assumption, and he let the evidence win. That is the posture I want to hold when we come to the part of my philosophy that the evidence may not support.

[PAUSE]

For most of the twentieth century, the big question about the expanding universe was how much it was slowing down. Gravity, after all, pulls everything toward everything. Surely it was acting as a brake on the expansion. The only question was whether the brake was strong enough to stop the expansion entirely and pull everything back together, or whether the universe would coast outward forever, gradually slowing.

In nineteen ninety-eight, two teams of astronomers, the Supernova Cosmology Project and the High-Z Supernova Search Team, set out to measure that slowing, using a particular kind of exploding star, called a type Ia supernova, as a measuring stick.

And they found the opposite of what they expected.

[LET IT LAND]

The expansion was not slowing down. It was speeding up.

In two thousand eleven, Saul Perlmutter, Brian Schmidt and Adam Riess received the Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery. Something, it seemed, was pushing the universe apart, faster and faster, against the pull of gravity. The best current picture suggests this acceleration began roughly five billion years ago.

[PAUSE]

And there is another open question I should mention, so you know how unfinished this science still is. Physicists cannot yet agree on exactly how fast the universe is expanding today. Measurements based on the cosmic microwave background, from the Planck mission, give a value of about sixty-seven kilometers per second per megaparsec. Measurements based on nearby stars and supernovae give about seventy-three. The two methods disagree by more than five sigma, and no one yet knows why. They call it the Hubble tension.

I do not bring it up to make a point for my metaphor. I bring it up to show you that, at the very largest scale, the first ledger itself still has blank lines on it. The universe has not finished telling us what it is. That should make all of us, physicists and philosophers alike, a little humbler about our conclusions.

[SLOW]

Physicists gave that something a name. They called it dark energy. And in a twist of history, the simplest way to describe it was to bring back Einstein's abandoned constant, lambda.

[SLOW]

Here are the numbers, first ledger. In the standard model of cosmology, the one physicists call Lambda C D M, about sixty-eight percent of the total energy content of the universe is dark energy. Most of the rest is dark matter, a different and equally mysterious thing.

I want to keep those two straight, because they are often confused. Dark matter was first inferred in nineteen thirty-three by the astronomer Fritz Zwicky, who noticed that the galaxies in the Coma Cluster were moving as if there was far more mass there than could be seen. He called it, in German, dunkle Materie, dark matter. In the nineteen seventies, Vera Rubin and Kent Ford found that the outer parts of spiral galaxies rotate far faster than visible matter alone could explain. Today, dark matter is thought to make up about eighty-five percent of all the matter in the universe.

Dark matter pulls. It clumps under gravity like ordinary matter. It is the invisible scaffolding on which galaxies are built.

Dark energy is something else. It does not clump. It pushes. Or at least, it causes the expansion of space to accelerate.

[PAUSE]

So here is the picture physics hands us. The universe is made of about five percent ordinary matter, the stuff of stars and planets and you. The rest is darkness we cannot see: dark matter, which gathers, and dark energy, which drives everything apart.

In the language of my metaphor, nearly everything that exists is something we do not yet know.

[SLOW]

Now I come to the second ledger. And I want to read you the reading I set down, as I set it down, so you can see exactly what I claimed and how I qualified it.

If our knowledge of the universe is bound up with its shape, then the universe expanding at an ever increasing rate suggests that we have so, so much more to know and act upon. I read what we call dark energy as a figure for what we do not yet know. The universe holding open the room it still has to become.

[PAUSE]

Let me unfold that.

In the Esketian view, gravity is the knowing force. It gathers. It brings things together so they can exchange what they are. And in that view, if the universe were close to knowing itself completely, you might expect gathering to dominate. You might expect the pull to be winning.

Instead, the universe is spreading out faster and faster. The distances between galaxies are growing. Whole regions of the cosmos are slipping beyond our ability ever to receive light from them.

On my reading, this is not a sign that the project of knowing has failed. It is a sign of how early we are. The universe is still making room. It is, so to speak, holding open the space in which more becoming can happen. Dark energy, read as a figure and not as a force I understand, is the measure of how much is still unknown, unlived, unbecome.

[BUILD]

Think of it this way. A young person with their whole life ahead of them is, in a sense, mostly future. Most of what they will be has not happened yet. Their possibilities spread out in every direction. That is not a deficiency. It is the very thing that makes youth precious.

On the Esketian reading, the universe is like that. Sixty-eight percent of it is, metaphorically speaking, room to grow. Dark energy is not the enemy of gravity. It is the open field in which gravity's long work of gathering and knowing has yet to be done.

[SLOW]

But a reading like this has consequences, and I have to follow them where they lead.

If dark energy is the room left to become, and if the universe really is tending toward knowing itself, then you would expect that room to be used up eventually. You would expect the expansion, someday, to slow. And then to stop. And then to reverse. Gravity would take over, pulling everything back together, until the universe ends in what physicists call the Big Crunch. And then, perhaps, the whole thing begins again, as a new wave of God becoming.

That is what my model predicts, taken as a model rather than a measurement.

[PAUSE]

And I should be plain with you, as I was plain with myself when I wrote it down. This is where my reading is most vulnerable.

## XI. DESI, the Crunch, and Holding It Loosely

Since the late nineteen nineties, observation has pointed the other way. The expansion appears to be accelerating, not slowing. If dark energy is truly Einstein's constant, the same at every place and every time, then it does not run out. The acceleration continues forever. The galaxies drift apart until each is alone in its own dark, and the universe grows colder and emptier without end.

That is not a Big Crunch. That is the opposite.

So if the constant is truly constant, my reading is in trouble.

[PAUSE]

But in the last two years, something has begun to waver.

On the nineteenth of March, two thousand twenty-five, the collaboration behind the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument, known as DESI, released results from its first three years of observations. DESI is a project of more than nine hundred researchers at over seventy institutions. Its instrument can capture light from up to five thousand objects at once, and in those three years it had mapped something like fifteen million galaxies and quasars, building a three-dimensional picture of how the universe expanded over billions of years.

What they reported was a hint. A tentative one, but a growing one. When DESI's maps were combined with measurements of the cosmic microwave background and with supernova data, the combination fit better if dark energy was not constant at all, but changing over time. Specifically, the data hinted that dark energy may have been stronger in the past and may be weakening now.

[SLOW]

Now, the numbers, because the numbers are the honesty.

Physicists measure the strength of evidence in units called sigma. Depending on which combination of data you use, the March two thousand twenty-five DESI results favored evolving dark energy at somewhere between two point eight and four point two sigma. To claim a discovery, physicists conventionally require five sigma. A three-sigma result has roughly a three-in-a-thousand chance of being a statistical fluke. DESI's data alone, without the other datasets, looked consistent with the standard picture.

So this was not a discovery. It was a crack of light under a door. Exciting enough that one researcher involved described it as the start of a new era. Uncertain enough that no one serious was ready to throw out the standard model.

[PAUSE]

Then, later in two thousand twenty-five, a team at Cornell University led by the physicist Henry Tye, with Hoang Nhan Luu and Yu-Cheng Qiu, published a paper in the Journal of Cosmology and Astroparticle Physics called "The Lifespan of our Universe."

Using the new survey data, their model suggested that the universe might reach its maximum size about eleven billion years from now, then begin to contract, ending in a Big Crunch about twenty billion years from now, for a total lifespan of about thirty-three billion years.

Tye said that the new data seemed to indicate that the cosmological constant is negative, the opposite of what most physicists had assumed for twenty years. And he added a qualification that I want you to hear, because it is the most important part. He said that if the data holds up, the universe will have an end. And he noted that there are more observations to come.

[SLOW]

If the data holds up.

When I first read that, I felt my heart leap. A serious cosmologist, publishing in a serious journal, describing a model in which the expansion reverses and gravity wins. The very future my reading predicted.

And then I made myself slow down. Because one paper is not a consensus. Because a model built on a hint is a model built on a hint. And because the next round of data might go the other way.

[PAUSE]

And in fact, it has pushed back, at least in part.

On the thirtieth of July, two thousand twenty-six, just a few months ago, DESI released a new analysis of its data from the Lyman-alpha forest, a pattern in the light of distant quasars that lets astronomers map the universe when it was young. This new measurement was considerably more precise than the earlier one. And its central value shifted toward the standard model, toward a constant dark energy.

The collaboration said plainly, in its announcement, that the current hints of evolving dark energy may fade away. In the accompanying paper, the combined preference for evolving dark energy came out at about two point seven sigma using DESI and the cosmic microwave background, and about three point two sigma when supernovae were added. Still a hint. Somewhat weaker than the strongest numbers from the year before. Still well short of five.

[LET IT LAND]

So here is where we stand, as of tonight, in October of two thousand twenty-six.

The expansion of the universe is accelerating. That is well established. Whether dark energy is truly constant, or is slowly changing, is an open question, with a persistent but modest hint of change that has not reached the level of discovery and may yet fade. One published model, built on that hint, describes a future Big Crunch some twenty billion years away. Most cosmologists would tell you it is far too early to say.

It is far from settled. And it is a long way from a Big Crunch.

[SLOW]

So let me tell you what I said to myself, and what I say to you now.

Either my reading is wrong, or we are far earlier in the process than it feels from here.

Those are the two options. I do not get to pick a third, in which the evidence is ignored. If, over the coming decades, the hint of evolving dark energy fades, and the constant proves truly constant, then the part of my picture that predicts a final gathering will have been tested, and it will have failed. I will have to say so. And the rest of the Esketian view will have to stand without it, or not stand at all.

And let me say concretely what I will be watching for. The DESI survey is continuing to gather data. Other teams are recalibrating the supernova measurements that feed into these combined analyses. Each new release will either strengthen the hint of evolving dark energy, or weaken it, or leave it hovering where it is. If, as more data arrives, the preference drifts back toward the constant and stays there, that is the moment I should start letting go of the crunch. If it climbs toward five sigma, that is the moment the conversation changes. In between, the honest posture is to wait.

And if the hint grows, I will still not claim victory. A crunch in twenty billion years would not prove that gravity is the knowing force, or that the universe is becoming God. It would only mean that one prediction of my reading had not been refuted. That is a much humbler thing.

[PAUSE]

I hold it loosely. As a picture I find beautiful and cannot yet defend.

And I want to be honest about one more limit, the deepest one.

Even if everything I have described were true, there is a question it cannot answer. If our universe is a becoming toward God, and if everything good and meaningful exists within that becoming, then what about the becoming itself? Is there a larger becoming that contains ours? And another beyond that, forever?

This is the primary problem of metaphysics, as I see it. We can only reason about one level. God, and the morality that flows from that God, exist within the universal becoming. Whether there is a becoming beyond it may be physically unknowable. And I have come to think that, for all its fascination, it is a relatively useless conjecture. I do not need to answer it to know how to live tonight.

[BUILD]

So hear the whole of it, the bold claim and the careful qualification together, the way Lincoln would have wanted.

I believe that gravity can be read as the knowing force. I believe the universe can be pictured as tending toward knowing itself. I read dark energy as the room the universe still has to become. And I believe, though I cannot defend it, that the expansion may one day turn, and gravity may gather all things home.

I believe it as God gives me to see it. Which is to say, partially. Provisionally. Ready to be corrected by the next measurement. And still, with my whole heart.

## XII. The Strongest Objection

Now I owe you the strongest case against everything I have said. Not a weak version, set up to be knocked down. The real one, as a thoughtful skeptic would make it.

[PAUSE]

The skeptic would begin like this.

You have spent an hour and a half dressing up a personal spirituality in the clothes of physics. Gravity is a curvature of spacetime described by Einstein's field equations. It does not know anything. Entanglement is a property of quantum states that cannot carry a single bit of usable information faster than light. Neural networks are software running on chips. Dark energy is a term in an equation that fits the data. Calling these things knowing, oneness, learning and room to become does not add anything to physics. It just borrows physics' prestige for ideas that would not survive on their own.

And worse, the skeptic would say, you are teaching people to do something dangerous. Every quack who sells "quantum healing" bracelets, every guru who claims consciousness creates reality, uses exactly this move: take a real scientific concept, strip it of its precise meaning, and wrap it around a wish. You may be careful. Your listeners may not be.

[SLOW]

The skeptic would go further.

You have projected purpose onto a universe that shows no sign of having any. The deepest lesson of modern science, from Darwin onward, is that order can arise without anyone intending it. Stars form because of gravity, not because the universe wants to know itself. Life evolved because some molecules happened to copy themselves, not because existence wills toward God. Reading purpose into all of this is the oldest human error: seeing faces in clouds.

And one more point, the sharpest. You call gravity the knowing force, the great gatherer. But gravity also destroys. It crushes dying stars into black holes, from which no information we know how to read ever escapes. It drags galaxies into collisions that tear them apart. And the future you hope for, a Big Crunch, is the annihilation of every structure that has ever existed. If that is knowing, it looks a great deal like devouring.

[PAUSE]

That is a serious case. I want to answer it seriously, point by point.

On the first charge, that I borrow physics' prestige, I plead partly guilty, and I have tried all evening to pay the debt honestly. I agree that gravity, in the physicist's sense, does not know anything. I said so in section five. I agree that entanglement sends no messages. I said so in section seven. I agree that the universe is not literally a neural network. I said so in section nine. The physics is the physics, and I have tried to report it exactly, including the doubts and the numbers.

What I am doing with the physics is something human beings have always done with their best knowledge: asking what it means for how we live. That is not science. I have never claimed it was. But neither is it fraud, as long as it is labelled. The fraud is in pretending the metaphor is a measurement. The honest alternative is not to forbid metaphor, which no one could do anyway, but to mark the boundary in bright paint, every single time.

[SLOW]

On the second charge, that I am teaching a dangerous move, I take that seriously, and here is my answer. The best protection against misuse of science is not silence about meaning. It is a public that knows the difference between what is measured and what is interpreted. If I can help even a few people learn to ask, when they hear the word "quantum," whether they are hearing physics or poetry, then I have made them harder to fool, not easier.

So let me say it once more, for anyone who will carry these ideas further than I can. Nothing in quantum theory says your thoughts change physical reality. Nothing in it lets you heal by intention or communicate by entanglement. If someone tells you otherwise, they are not extending the Esketian view. They are betraying the honesty it depends on.

[PAUSE]

On the third charge, that I project purpose onto a purposeless universe, I want to concede the strength of the point and then say why I still hold my reading.

It is true that science explains the formation of stars and the evolution of life without invoking purpose. I accept those explanations completely. I do not think gravity formed stars in order to make knowers. I think gravity formed stars, and knowers came of it.

But here is the thing the skeptic cannot avoid. From the inside, life does not feel purposeless. It feels pulled. We breathe because we must. We seek knowledge because it delights us. We raise children because, in their faces, we glimpse a continuation of everything we love. The question of whether that pull corresponds to anything beyond ourselves is a philosophical question, not a scientific one. Science, by its own rules, is silent on it.

In that silence, every one of us chooses some attitude. The skeptic chooses one, that the pull is an accident of evolution with no larger meaning. I choose another, that the pull is the local face of a universe becoming conscious of itself. Neither of us can prove our attitude with an instrument. But I would ask the skeptic to notice that the attitude of meaninglessness is also an attitude, not a finding. It, too, goes beyond the data.

[SLOW]

On the final charge, that gravity devours, I think the skeptic has found something true, and something that deepens my reading rather than defeating it.

Yes. Gravity crushes. It collapses dying stars. It drives galaxies into one another. If the expansion ever turns, it will gather everything into a fire that ends every structure we know.

But look closer at what that destruction does. The explosion of a dying massive star is how the carbon and oxygen in your body were scattered into space. Without those deaths, no planets like ours, no chemistry like ours, no us. The collisions of galaxies trigger waves of new star formation. The merger of black holes sent a message across a billion light-years that we finally learned to read.

[PAUSE]

In the Esketian picture, knowing is not gentle. It costs something. To come to know another person, you must let them change you, and some part of who you were is lost. To learn anything real, you must let go of something you believed. Every meeting is a small death and a small birth.

And as for the Big Crunch, if it ever comes, my reading does not call it annihilation. It calls it the final gathering, and imagines, as a picture and not a promise, that the whole thing begins again. I cannot show that. I said in my notes that I cannot defend it. But I do not think the skeptic can show that it is mere destruction either. At that scale, we are all speaking in images.

[PAUSE]

There is one more objection I should face, because it comes from inside my own view rather than from outside it.

If the universe is deterministically becoming God, if the destination is set, then why should anything I do matter? Why struggle, why choose, why put the phone down, if the outcome is already written?

This is an old puzzle, older than physics. It troubled theologians who believed in predestination, and it troubles physicists who wonder what their equations leave room for. I will not pretend to have solved it. But here is how I hold it.

A destination is not a path. Even a learning system with a fixed objective does not reach it automatically. It can wander. It can stall. It can settle into a comfortable valley and stay there for a very long time. What carries it onward is each small correction, each step. In the human case, those steps are our choices. If something true and good is going to happen, it will happen through us, not around us. So the determinism I believe in does not make our choices idle. It makes them the very material out of which the outcome is built.

That may not satisfy a strict logician. It satisfies me enough to get up in the morning and do the work.

[LET IT LAND]

So here is my answer to the strongest objection, in a sentence.

The skeptic is right about the physics, and I have tried to honor every word of it. Where we differ is on the meaning, and on the meaning, neither of us has the final measurement. I have chosen the reading that makes me more curious, more connected, and more responsible for what I do with my life. I invite you to weigh it, and to keep your own ledgers as carefully as I have tried to keep mine.

## XIII. The Counterfeit Pull

Now let me bring all of this down to Earth. Literally, to the surface of the planet that gravity built, and to the devices in our pockets.

If gravity is the knowing force, the pull that brings things together so they can exchange what they are, then human connection is gravity at the human scale. Every time two people meet and truly come to know each other, the long work that began with the first wrinkles of the cosmos continues.

And that is why I believe the exchange of information between human beings is good. That is the whole Esketian thesis, and I do not retreat from it. Connection at scale is the becoming working as it should.

[SLOW]

Look around at the machinery of our age with that in mind. Markets that gather the scattered judgments of millions into a single price. Prediction markets where people who know something put money behind it, until the probability itself becomes a signal everyone else can act on. Recommendation systems that sift through the endless output of humanity and bring the most striking pieces to everyone else. Artificial intelligence trained on the written record of our species and set to work on our questions.

In form, all of this is Esketian. It is everything coming to know everything. It is gravity, carried into the realm of information.

But I added a caution in my notes, and it is the hinge of everything that follows. These things are Esketian in form, though not always in spirit. The ethos can be counterfeited.

[PAUSE]

But something has gone wrong, and you know it, because you feel it.

We carry, most of us, devices that can connect us with almost anyone on Earth. And yet so many people report feeling lonelier, more anxious, more distrustful than before. We have more connection than any generation in history, and we seem to be starving.

How can both be true?

[SLOW]

Here is the answer I have come to, and it uses the same language we have been using all evening.

The pull to plug into God, to reach for the most up-to-date knowledge the world can give us, is real. It is the will to God, operating as it should. But the attention business discovered that it could counterfeit that pull. It found it could earn more by keeping us scrolling than by actually connecting us. And so it built feeds that feel like reaching for the absolute, while delivering, for the most part, only base phenomena. Distraction. Outrage. A trance that names itself connection.

Remember my definition of God. The moment in which we know everything and, through that knowing, are immortal. Knowledge joined to sustained existence.

The feed offers a simulation of the first half while quietly consuming the second. Your hours. Your attention. Your sleep. Your health. It is knowledge without sustainment. That is what makes it a counterfeit, and not merely a bad habit.

[PAUSE]

And the costs of it, the distrust, the anxiety, the depression, the general sense of misery, are real. They are paid by us and by everyone around us. And they show up on nobody's balance sheet. In the language of economics, it is one of the great mispriced externalities of our era.

I want to be exact about the target, because it matters more than anything else in this section. The problem was never that we came to know each other too much. Connection is good. The problem is that a business model found it could extract more by keeping us staring than by bringing us together, and nothing in the price signal punished it for the difference.

[SLOW]

Now, here is a question that my own view raised, and that I had to answer honestly. If the universe is a becoming toward God, why would it produce counterfeits at all? Why would a cosmos tending toward knowledge produce a machine for distraction?

The answer comes from the same image of the universe as a learner.

Any learning system, descending toward its objective, can settle into what is called a local minimum. It is a place that feels like progress from the inside, where every small step seems to make things worse, but it is not the true optimum. The network gets stuck. It has found a comfortable valley, and it does not know there is a deeper one beyond the ridge.

The feed is such a local minimum. And a whole civilization has settled into it.

[PAUSE]

The destination may be set, but the path runs through choices. And climbing out of a local minimum takes a push.

Our actions are that push.

[BUILD]

So what would it look like to point the real pull, the gravity of human connection, at something real?

It would look like knowledge that comes with sustainment. Connection that comes with community. Information that brings you closer to the people you can actually see, help, and be helped by.

I had an idea once, sitting in an airport, looking around at a room full of people, every one of them bent over a phone. Each of us holding a device that could reach the far side of the planet. And not one of us able to say a word to the person sitting three seats away.

That is the counterfeit pull in a single image. We were connected to everywhere except where we were.

[SLOW]

That idea became a project I call Vicinity, a real-time chat between people who are physically near each other, meant to reduce the information gaps between people who share a place. I mention it not to sell it to you tonight, but because it is the one thing my philosophy actually produced rather than merely decorated. It was derived from the ideas I have been describing. And I think of it, at a sufficient scale, as the humanization of gravity: people coming together, exchanging information in real space, with the people actually around them.

Whether it succeeds, we shall see. The point is not the product. The point is the direction. Every one of us can point our attention toward the people near us, or away from them. Every one of us can choose whether our devices pull us into the room or out of it.

[PAUSE]

When I picture what that kind of connection looks like at its best, I keep coming back to a simple image. Rain falling on still water. Each drop makes its own small ring, and the rings spread outward and cross one another, until the whole surface is alive with patterns that no single drop could make alone. That, to me, is the infinite interconnection of nature made local and human. Not a feed pulling everyone's eyes toward the same distant glow, but countless small meetings, each spreading outward, overlapping, touching.

[PAUSE]

There is an old word from the fourteenth-century historian Ibn Khaldun: asabiyyah. It means group solidarity, the shared feeling of belonging and purpose that, in his account, lets a people rise, and whose decay, as comfort and isolation set in, lets them fall.

I believe a civilization that learned to use its technology to strengthen that kind of solidarity, rather than to dissolve it, could overcome much of the atomization and alienation we feel. That is a belief, not a forecast. But it follows from everything we have said tonight. If gravity built the cosmos by gathering, then a people who gather, truly gather, are doing the oldest work in the universe.

[LET IT LAND]

The knowing force made the stars. It made the planet. It made the conditions for you to be sitting here. The least we can do, it seems to me, is let it do its work at the human scale. Look up. Look around. Come to know the people who are near.

## XIV. Consider the Pull

Let us go back, one last time, to that photograph.

Six billion kilometers out. A spacecraft that gravity flung past the giant planets turns its camera home. And in a stray beam of light, there is a speck, a fraction of a pixel, holding every one of us.

[PAUSE]

Sagan used that speck to teach us how small we are. And he was right. We are small. Our quarrels are small. Our certainty is small. Our planet is a mote suspended in a sunbeam, and the universe does not arrange itself around our convenience.

But I want to leave you with the second lesson of the photograph, the one I promised at the beginning.

We are small, and we are known.

[SLOW]

The universe made a piece of itself that could look back at itself and say, here you are. A piece of starstuff that could hear black holes collide across a billion light-years. A piece of the cosmos that could measure the first light, and the bending of starlight, and the slow acceleration of everything outward. A piece of the universe that could stand in a room like this one and wonder what it is all for.

Whatever else is true, that is true. It is on the first ledger. The universe contains knowers. It made them, out of dust, by gathering.

[BUILD]

So consider the pull.

Consider the pull that gathered the first wrinkles of the cosmos into clouds, and the clouds into stars.

Consider the pull that squeezed those stars until they burned, and forged the carbon in your cells, and the iron in your blood.

Consider the pull that built this planet out of the wreckage of older suns, and holds it together, and holds you to it, close enough to the people beside you that you can reach out and touch them.

Consider the pull that sent a machine out past Saturn and swung it into the dark so that it could turn and show us our home.

Consider the pull that rings across space when the universe's darkest objects meet, carrying the news of their meeting outward, until, a billion years later, someone is finally listening.

[PAUSE]

I have called it the knowing force. I have told you, every time, that this is a reading and not a result. I have told you that the part of my picture that sees a final gathering is the part I cannot defend, and that the latest data, as of this summer, may be drifting away from it. I have told you that if the hint fades, I will say so.

And I have told you that I hold it anyway. Loosely. As God gives me to see it. Ready to be corrected. Unwilling to be cynical.

[SLOW]

Because here is what I am sure of, more sure than of any cosmology.

Whatever the universe is doing, at its largest scale, you are part of it. You are made of what gravity gathered. You are, in a precise physical sense, not perfectly separate from anything. And every time you come to truly know another person, every time you let them change you, every time you choose the person in front of you over the counterfeit glow in your hand, you are continuing the oldest work there is.

The universe began in oneness. It spread out into the dark. And it has been coming back together ever since, one meeting at a time.

[BUILD]

So let us not be the generation that had the whole of human knowledge in its pocket and used it to look away from one another.

Let us not mistake the idol for the God, or the feed for the gathering, or the trance for connection.

Let us be, instead, the generation that let the knowing force do its work at the scale of a human life. That looked up from the screen and into the faces of the people near us. That learned their names. That came, as the old teaching says, to know and love thy neighbor.

[PAUSE]

There is a speck of light, six billion kilometers back, that holds every one of us.

On it, gravity is still gathering. Stars are still burning. People are still meeting, and learning, and becoming. The universe, on that speck, is still coming to know itself, through you, through me, through everyone you will ever love.

We may be very early in that process. Earlier than it feels from here. The room left to become may be vast beyond our imagining.

[SLOW]

Then let us begin.

Let us gather.

Let us come to know each other.

[LET IT LAND]

Thank you.
