# Deaths of Despair

## I. The Ledger

Fellow citizens, I want to begin with a piece of good news, because it is true, and because it will help me tell you something that is harder to hear.

In January of this year, the National Center for Health Statistics reported that life expectancy at birth in the United States had reached seventy-nine years. It was the highest figure ever recorded in this country. After a pandemic, after a poisoning epidemic, after years of headlines about decline, the number went up.

[PAUSE]

You might expect a speech called "Deaths of Despair" to skip that number, or to explain it away. I will do neither. I will hold it up in the light, the way you hold up a banknote to see whether it is real.

It is real. And here is what you see when you hold it up.

An average is a kind of promise. It says: this is what a life in this country is worth, in years. But an average can rise while a whole class of people falls. It can rise because some of us are living longer than any Americans before us, while others are dying younger than their parents did. The average is a blanket thrown over a room. It tells you how many people are in the room. It does not tell you who is cold.

[SLOW]

So tonight I want to lift the blanket.

I want to talk about three kinds of death that have no business being as common as they are in the richest country in human history. Death by overdose. Death by suicide. Death by drink. Two economists gave these deaths a name a little more than a decade ago. They called them deaths of despair.

Some people dislike the phrase. They say it is too soft, or too vague, or that it turns a drug supply problem into a poem. I will take that objection seriously later in this speech, because it is the strongest one there is. But I will tell you now why I keep the word.

I keep it because it names a cause that a death certificate cannot record. The certificate will say poisoning, or liver failure, or an injury. It will not say that the mill closed, that the marriage ended, that the church emptied, that the pain in the back never went away and the pills were easy to get. It will not say that a man stopped expecting next year to be better than this one. Despair is not a medical term. It is a description of a life in which the future has gone dark.

[PAUSE]

Here is my thesis, and I will spend the next two hours earning it.

These deaths are not a mystery and they are not a natural disaster. They are the result of choices: choices made in boardrooms and in Congress, in pharmaceutical sales meetings and in trade negotiations, in state capitols and in our own neighborhoods. They follow a map we drew. They follow a line we drew through the middle of the country, between people with a four-year degree and people without one. And underneath the economics there is something older, something our grandparents would have called spiritual, a hollowing out of the institutions that once told ordinary people that they belonged and that their lives were needed.

If these deaths were caused by choices, then other choices can end them. That is the hope in this speech, and I want you to hold on to it through the hard parts.

[SLOW]

Because there are hard parts.

Before I go on, a word about one of them. I am going to talk about suicide. I will talk about it carefully. I will not describe how anyone died. And if you are listening tonight and that subject is close to you, closer than you would like, I want you to know now, at the beginning, not at the end: you can call or text nine eight eight, any hour of any day, and a trained person will answer. It is free. It is confidential. People do come through the darkest nights. Many of the people who answer those calls once needed that call themselves.

[PAUSE]

Now let me tell you how we came to know any of this. It begins, as many important American stories do, with someone looking at a chart and noticing that a line was going the wrong way.

## II. Two Economists and a Line on a Chart

In the fall of two thousand fifteen, two economists at Princeton published a short paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. Their names were Anne Case and Angus Deaton. They were married to each other. Deaton, a Scotsman born in Edinburgh, would win the Nobel Prize in economics that same year, for his work on consumption, poverty, and welfare.

The paper was short, and its tone was cautious, the way scientific papers are supposed to be. But what it described was a catastrophe.

Here is what they found.

For most of the twentieth century, death rates for Americans in middle age fell, year after year. Medicine improved. Smoking declined. Heart disease, the great killer, retreated. From the late nineteen seventies to the late nineteen nineties, the death rate for white Americans between forty-five and fifty-four fell by about two percent a year. That is what progress looks like in a mortality table. It is boring and steady and wonderful.

And then, around the turn of the century, it stopped. Not slowed. Stopped, and turned around.

[LET IT LAND]

Between nineteen ninety-nine and two thousand thirteen, the death rate for middle-aged white Americans rose by about half a percent a year.

Now, half a percent a year does not sound like much. So let me put it the way Case and Deaton did. If the death rate for that one age group had simply held where it was in nineteen ninety-eight, about ninety-six thousand people who died would have lived. If it had kept falling at the pace it fell before, the number is closer to half a million. Half a million Americans, in fifteen years, in one ten-year slice of middle age.

And this was happening nowhere else. They compared the United States with France, Germany, Britain, Canada, Australia, and Sweden. In every one of those countries, midlife death rates went on falling at about two percent a year. America had stepped off the escalator that the rest of the rich world was still riding.

It was not happening to every American, either. In that same period, death rates for Black and Hispanic Americans in midlife kept falling. Death rates for Americans over sixty-five, of every background, kept falling. The reversal was concentrated among middle-aged white Americans, and within that group, among those with the least education.

[PAUSE]

What was killing them? Not cancer, not heart disease, not the usual suspects. The rise was driven by poisonings from drugs and alcohol, by suicide, and by chronic liver disease. And alongside the deaths, the paper reported something else: people in this group said their health was getting worse. They reported more pain, more mental distress, more difficulty working. The deaths were the tip of something. Underneath them was a great sea of suffering that did not show up in the mortality tables at all.

I want to dwell on that sea of suffering for a moment, because it is the part of the story that is easiest to forget.

For every person who died, there were many more who were living in pain. Pain in the back and the knees and the shoulders, from years of hard physical work. Pain that kept them from working, which cost them their income, which cost them their insurance, which made the pain harder to treat. Pain of the other kind, too: the kind that does not show up on an X-ray but keeps a person awake at three in the morning, wondering what happened to the life they expected to have.

[SLOW]

When people in that condition are offered something that makes the pain go away, even for a few hours, a great many of them will take it. That is not a moral failing peculiar to one class of Americans. It is a human response to suffering. Any one of us, in enough pain, for long enough, with nothing else on offer, might do the same.

The question that Case and Deaton forced the country to face was not, why are these people so weak? It was, why are these people in so much pain, and why is nobody helping them carry it?

[PAUSE]

Case and Deaton were careful people, and they wrote carefully. They said that ties to economic insecurity were possible, and they presented that as a hypothesis, not a conclusion. But they also wrote that addictions are hard to treat and pain is hard to control, and they raised the possibility that this generation in middle age might become a lost generation.

[SLOW]

A lost generation. Economists do not usually write like that.

Two years later they followed up, in the Brookings Papers on Economic Activity, and the picture had grown darker and sharper. The trend had continued. It had spread beyond a few regions; it had become, as they put it, country-wide. And they offered a striking comparison. In nineteen ninety-nine, the death rate for middle-aged white Americans without a college degree was about thirty percent lower than the death rate for Black Americans of the same age. By two thousand fifteen, it was about thirty percent higher.

I want to be very careful about what that comparison means and what it does not mean. It does not mean that Black Americans were doing well. Black Americans had suffered higher death rates for generations, for reasons that run straight back through this country's history. What it means is that a gap that had been taken for granted, a gap rooted in centuries of injustice, had been closed, not by bringing one group up, but in part by a slice of another group falling down. That is not the kind of equality anyone should want.

[PAUSE]

In that second paper, Case and Deaton began to tell a story about why. They noticed that each new group of working-class Americans, born a few years after the last, did worse than the group before it at the same age. Worse in their jobs, worse in their health, less likely to be married, more likely to report pain. The damage was not a single blow. It was accumulating, cohort after cohort, like sediment at the bottom of a river.

They traced the beginning back to the early nineteen seventies, the end of the long postwar boom in blue-collar work, and they followed it forward through the financial crash of two thousand eight and the slow, uneven recovery after it.

In twenty twenty they published a book, with Princeton University Press, and they gave it a title that tells you how far their thinking had traveled: Deaths of Despair and the Future of Capitalism. In it they argued that the problem was not only drugs and not only jobs. It was a weakening of labor's position, a growth in corporate power, and a health care system that, in their view, takes money out of working people's wages and moves it upward.

[LET IT LAND]

I do not ask you to accept every part of their argument. I will push back on some of it before the night is done. But I ask you to sit for a moment with the fact that it took two economists, looking at a line on a chart, to tell the most powerful nation on earth that a part of its people had begun to die faster, and that the rest of the country had not noticed.

How could we not notice? The answer, I think, is the blanket. The average kept going up. The towns where it was happening were not the towns where the newspapers were written. And the people who were dying were, many of them, people we had already stopped looking at.

## III. What the Numbers Mean

Let me give you the numbers now, plainly, and then let me tell you why numbers are not enough.

In nineteen ninety-nine, about seventeen thousand Americans died of drug overdoses. That was already a terrible figure. By two thousand fifteen, it was more than fifty thousand. By two thousand nineteen, about seventy thousand. Then the pandemic came, and with it isolation and disruption and a drug supply growing more poisonous by the month, and the number leapt. More than ninety thousand in twenty twenty. More than one hundred and ten thousand a year at the peak, in twenty twenty-two.

[PAUSE]

One hundred and ten thousand people in a single year. It is a small city, emptied, every year.

The Centers for Disease Control counts about eight hundred and six thousand Americans who died of opioid overdoses alone between nineteen ninety-nine and twenty twenty-three. Eight hundred thousand. Think of one of our great American cities, and imagine it gone, not in one night, but slowly, house by house, over a quarter century, while the rest of the country went about its business.

And overdose is only one of the three.

Each year, the CDC estimates that about one hundred and seventy-eight thousand Americans die from the effects of excessive drinking. That figure is from twenty twenty and twenty twenty-one, and it was up twenty-nine percent from just four years earlier.

Each year, close to fifty thousand Americans die by suicide. In twenty twenty-four the number was forty-eight thousand, eight hundred and twenty-four. It has hovered near that level for years.

Measured as a rate, which accounts for a growing population, the change is just as stark. In two thousand two, about eight Americans out of every hundred thousand died of a drug overdose. By twenty twenty-two, it was more than thirty-two. The rate had roughly quadrupled in twenty years. Men died at more than twice the rate of women. And the age group with the highest rate was not teenagers, not the elderly, but adults between thirty-five and forty-four: people in the prime of life, people raising children, people who should have been the backbone of their communities.

[SLOW]

These categories overlap; some deaths fall into more than one. And they are measured in different ways, so you cannot simply add them together and announce a total. Case and Deaton, counting narrowly, put the deaths of despair at about one hundred and fifty-eight thousand in twenty eighteen, compared with sixty-five thousand in nineteen ninety-five. However you count them, the conclusion does not change. These deaths rose from a background hum to one of the great killers of American adults in a single generation.

Now listen to what that did to the national average, the blanket I spoke of.

According to Case and Deaton's publisher, life expectancy in the United States fell for three years in a row in the middle of the last decade, from two thousand fifteen through two thousand seventeen. That had not happened since nineteen eighteen, the year of the great influenza and the last year of the First World War. In nineteen eighteen we had a pandemic and a world war. In the twenty-teens, we had neither. We did it to ourselves.

[PAUSE]

Then came COVID, and life expectancy fell off a cliff, from about seventy-eight point eight years in twenty nineteen to seventy-six point four in twenty twenty-one. Much of that was the virus. But not all of it. Overdoses rose during the pandemic, and alcohol deaths rose with them.

And then, as I said at the start, the number came back. Seventy-eight point four in twenty twenty-three. Seventy-nine in twenty twenty-four, a record. The government's report that announced it also noted something worth hearing: one of the biggest reasons for the improvement was a sharp fall in deaths from unintentional injuries, the category where most overdose deaths are counted. Those deaths fell from about two hundred and twenty-three thousand in twenty twenty-three to about one hundred and ninety-seven thousand in twenty twenty-four.

[BUILD]

And the overdose numbers have kept falling. The CDC's provisional count for twenty twenty-five was just under seventy thousand overdose deaths, a drop of nearly fourteen percent from the year before, the third straight year of decline. Opioid deaths fell from about fifty-five thousand to about forty-five thousand. Almost every state saw a decrease.

Let me say this clearly, because a speech about despair can forget to say it: that is real progress, and real people are alive because of it. Somewhere tonight there is a young woman eating dinner with her family who would have died in twenty twenty-three. There are tens of thousands of her.

[SLOW]

But hear the other half of that sentence. Seventy thousand overdose deaths in a year is, according to the CDC's own release, about where we were in twenty nineteen. And in twenty nineteen, we called that number a national emergency. We have climbed down from a peak of horror back to a plateau of horror, and the danger is that we will call the plateau normal.

The same report that brought us the record life expectancy also reported that suicide had re-entered the list of the ten leading causes of death in America. It did not climb into that list because suicide grew more common. It climbed back because COVID fell out. And chronic liver disease, much of it driven by alcohol, sits just above it, at number nine.

[LET IT LAND]

So that is the shape of it. A fire that roared, and has been beaten back, but is still burning. A record average, and under the average, a widening gap between those who are thriving and those who are not.

Numbers are necessary. But I have learned that numbers can also be a kind of anesthesia. Past a certain size they stop meaning anything. One death is a funeral, the old saying goes, and a hundred thousand is a statistic.

So I want to do something different for the next part of this speech. I want to stop counting the dead for a while and start asking how they died, and who sold them the means, and who looked away. And I want to begin with a pill.

## IV. The Pill

In January of nineteen eighty, the New England Journal of Medicine published a letter to the editor. It was a single paragraph. It was written by a doctor named Hershel Jick, and it reported that among patients who had been given narcotic painkillers while in the hospital, very few went on to become addicted.

That was all it was. A short note about hospitalized patients, under supervision, for a limited time. It was not a study of people sent home with a bottle of pills. It was not a study of chronic back pain. It did not claim to be.

[PAUSE]

But over the following years, that little letter was cited, again and again, as if it were proof that opioid painkillers were safe for almost anyone, for almost any pain, for almost any length of time. Jick himself later said he never meant it to be used that way. In twenty seventeen, the journal itself attached a note to the letter, pointing out that it had been based only on patients in the hospital.

I tell you this story first because it shows how a catastrophe is built. Not usually by a single villain in a dark room, though there are villains in this story. Usually it is built out of small convenient half-truths, repeated by people who want them to be true, until they harden into common sense.

[SLOW]

In nineteen ninety-five, the Food and Drug Administration approved a new painkiller made by a company called Purdue Pharma. It was called OxyContin. It was a long-acting form of oxycodone, and the company argued that its time-release design made it less attractive to people who wanted to misuse it. The approval was granted without long-term studies of how addictive it might be. It went on sale in nineteen ninety-six.

What followed was one of the most aggressive marketing campaigns in the history of American medicine. Purdue held dozens of promotional conferences. It ran a campaign called Partners Against Pain. And, according to accounts that later emerged, its sales representatives were trained to tell doctors that the risk of addiction was less than one percent.

Less than one percent. Hold on to that number, because the company would later admit in a court of law that it had misled the public about the risk of addiction.

[PAUSE]

It was not only Purdue, and it was not only marketing. In those same years, the medical profession was being told, with good intentions, that it had been cruel to patients in pain, that pain was undertreated, that a good doctor took it seriously. There was truth in that. Many people in real agony had been turned away. But the correction overshot, and an industry was standing ready to profit from the overshoot.

The pills went out. Prescriptions for opioid painkillers rose from about seventy-six million in nineteen ninety to about one hundred and sixteen million by nineteen ninety-nine, making them the most prescribed class of drugs in the country. And they did not flow evenly. They flowed hardest into the places where bodies were already broken: the coal fields, the mill towns, the places where people had spent their working lives lifting, digging, hauling, and falling.

By twenty twelve, in some states, pharmacies were dispensing more opioid prescriptions in a year than there were people living in the state. In Alabama the figure was about one hundred and forty-three prescriptions for every hundred residents. In West Virginia, about one hundred and thirty-eight.

[LET IT LAND]

More prescriptions than people.

In West Virginia a reporter named Eric Eyre, working for the Charleston Gazette-Mail, spent years digging through shipment records to show just how many pills were being poured into small towns in that state. He won the Pulitzer Prize for investigative reporting in twenty seventeen. The prize committee praised his courage in the face of powerful opposition. Think about that phrase. It took courage, in America, to count pills.

[PAUSE]

Now, what happened to Purdue?

In May of two thousand seven, the company pleaded guilty to misleading the public about OxyContin's risk of addiction, and agreed to pay six hundred million dollars. Three of its executives pleaded guilty to misbranding and paid fines.

Six hundred million dollars sounds like a great deal of money. Against the sales of a blockbuster drug, it was a cost of doing business. The pills kept selling.

In two thousand nineteen, buried under lawsuits from states and cities and counties, Purdue filed for bankruptcy. In November of twenty twenty, it pleaded guilty again, this time to three federal felony charges. No member of the family that owned the company was charged with a crime.

And then came the long fight over what that family, the Sacklers, would pay, and what they would get in return. The proposed bankruptcy deal would have shielded them from future lawsuits by victims, even though they themselves had not declared bankruptcy. In June of twenty twenty-four, the Supreme Court, in a five to four decision, struck down that arrangement. The case went back to the lower courts for a new settlement.

[SLOW]

I am not going to stand here and tell you that all of this was one family's fault. That would be too easy, and it would let too many people off the hook. There were the distributors who shipped the pills and the pharmacies that filled the orders and the regulators who approved the labels and the doctors who wrote the prescriptions and the politicians who took the contributions and looked away.

There is a temptation, in a story like this, to find the one guilty party and pour all our anger on them, so that the rest of us may feel clean. Lincoln, speaking at the end of a far greater catastrophe, refused that temptation. He did not pretend that there was no wrong. He named the wrong plainly. But he would not let his own side pretend it had no share in it.

I want to hold that line tonight. A company lied, and it pleaded guilty to lying, twice. That is a fact and it should be said in plain words. But a lie only spreads where people are ready to believe it, and where a system rewards those who repeat it. We built a medical system that paid for pills more readily than for physical therapy, for prescriptions more readily than for time with a patient, for the fifteen-minute visit rather than the long conversation. We built a regulatory system that trusted the seller. And we built an economy that left a great many people with bodies in pain and very little else to look forward to.

[PAUSE]

The pill was the spark. The country was the dry grass.

And when, in the twenty-tens, the authorities finally began to clamp down on prescribing, when the pill mills were shut and the prescriptions fell, the fire did not go out. It jumped.

## V. The Waves

The Centers for Disease Control describes the opioid epidemic as coming in three waves. I want to walk you through them, because each wave teaches a different lesson.

The first wave began around nineteen ninety-nine. It was made of prescription pills, the oxycodone and hydrocodone that I have just described, along with methadone. These were deaths of people who had, many of them, started with a prescription from a doctor they trusted.

The second wave began around twenty ten, and it was made of heroin.

[PAUSE]

Here is how that happened. As prescribing tightened, as pills grew harder to get and more expensive on the street, people who were already dependent did not simply stop. Dependence does not work that way. They went looking for something cheaper, and they found that it was already waiting for them.

A journalist named Sam Quinones told part of this story in a book called Dreamland, published in twenty fifteen. He traced how traffickers from a small town in Mexico called Xalisco had transformed the way heroin was produced and sold in this country, and how their cheap black-tar heroin flowed into American towns that had never before had a heroin problem. It filled the gap that the pills had opened.

I want you to notice the shape of that. The legal market created the customers. The illegal market served them. One followed the other as surely as water follows a slope.

[SLOW]

The third wave began around twenty thirteen, and it was made of fentanyl.

Fentanyl is a synthetic opioid. It has legitimate medical uses; it is used in hospitals for surgery and severe pain. But it is enormously potent, roughly fifty to a hundred times as potent as morphine, and about two milligrams can be enough to kill most people. Two milligrams. A few grains of salt.

Because it is so potent, it is enormously profitable to smuggle. A small package can be cut into a vast number of doses. Investigators have described a supply chain in which chemical ingredients come largely from China, and the drug is made and packaged in Mexico and smuggled into the United States by cartels.

And because it is so cheap and so strong, it began to appear everywhere. In heroin. In counterfeit pills pressed to look like prescription painkillers or anti-anxiety medicines. In stimulants. Many of the people who died of fentanyl did not know they were taking it.

[LET IT LAND]

That is the terrible innovation of the third wave. In the first wave, the danger was addiction. In the third wave, the danger was that a single mistake, a single pill bought from the wrong person, could end a life the first time.

By twenty twenty-two, fentanyl and related synthetic opioids were involved in nearly seventy-four thousand deaths in a single year. In twenty twenty-two, the Food and Drug Administration also warned that an animal tranquilizer called xylazine was turning up more and more often in the illicit supply of heroin and fentanyl. The poison was being cut with another poison.

[PAUSE]

The government responded, as governments do, with law. In July of twenty twenty-five, the President signed the HALT Fentanyl Act, which permanently placed fentanyl-related substances in the strictest category of controlled drugs. It passed the Senate eighty-four to sixteen and the House three hundred and twenty-one to one hundred and four. That is a rare thing in this Congress: a bill both parties could vote for.

Some civil liberties groups opposed it. They warned that it would sweep in chemically similar compounds that might have medical uses, and that harsh drug penalties have a long history of falling hardest on Black communities. Supporters answered that the drug was killing tens of thousands of people a year and that the law needed to keep up with chemists who kept changing the molecule.

I will not settle that argument tonight. I will only say this. We have tried, for half a century, to arrest our way out of drug epidemics. Enforcement has a place; nobody who has seen what fentanyl does can be indifferent to the people who traffic it. But every wave of this epidemic has been answered first with enforcement, and every wave has been followed by another. When you squeeze one part of the supply, it bulges somewhere else. The pills were squeezed and heroin bulged. Heroin was squeezed and fentanyl bulged.

[SLOW]

The lesson of the three waves is this. As long as there are millions of people in pain, physical and otherwise, who want relief badly enough to risk their lives for it, someone will sell them relief. The supply matters enormously; I will argue later that it matters more than some of the despair theorists admit. But the demand is the ground the supply grows in. And the demand is made of human beings.

[PAUSE]

Before I leave the waves, I want to say a word about the language we use for the people caught in them, because language is policy before it becomes law.

For most of this epidemic, we had a word for people who used heroin, and it was not a kind word. We imagined them as other people: people from somewhere else, people who had chosen their fate, people unlike us. That picture was always false, and the first wave proved it. The first wave was made of people who had been given a prescription by a doctor in a white coat. Construction workers with crushed discs. Nurses with bad shoulders. Grandmothers after hip surgery. Teenagers after a football injury or a wisdom tooth.

[SLOW]

Shame is not a treatment. Shame is the thing that keeps a man from telling his doctor that the pills have taken hold of him. Shame is the thing that keeps a mother from asking for help because she fears she will lose her children. Shame is the thing that sends a person to use alone, behind a locked bathroom door, where there is no one to call for help and no one to carry the naloxone. If we want fewer people to die, we must make it easier, not harder, for people to say out loud that they are in trouble.

Addiction is a condition that people can and do recover from. Most of us know someone who has. Many of us love someone who has. The person in front of you who is struggling is not a word. He is somebody's son.

So we must ask: who are they? Why were so many of them in so much pain? And why was the relief they found so often fatal?

Before we answer, I want to turn to the oldest drug of all, the one that is legal, the one that is advertised during football games, the one that kills more Americans every year than the other two causes I am discussing tonight. I mean alcohol.

## VI. The Quiet Deaths

There are no cartels in the story of alcohol. There are no midnight raids, no congressional hearings about smuggling routes. Alcohol is sold in grocery stores, and gas stations, and in the stands at the ballpark. We toast our weddings with it and we pour it at our funerals.

And it kills, by the CDC's estimate, about one hundred and seventy-eight thousand Americans a year.

[PAUSE]

That figure comes from the years twenty twenty and twenty twenty-one, and it represents a jump of about twenty-nine percent from the years twenty sixteen and twenty seventeen, when the figure was about one hundred and thirty-eight thousand. The CDC put it another way. More than four hundred and eighty-eight deaths every day. About twenty an hour.

About a hundred and seventeen thousand of those deaths come slowly, from chronic diseases: cancers, heart disease, and above all disease of the liver. About sixty-one thousand come quickly, from drinking too much at one time: crashes, poisonings, falls, violence.

And the people who die from alcohol lose, on average, twenty-four years of life. Twenty-four years. A child born the day one of them died would be grown, graduated, perhaps a parent, by the time that person should have died.

[SLOW]

We call these the quiet deaths because they are so easy to miss. An overdose is sudden; it makes the news in a small town. A death from liver disease takes years. It happens in a hospital bed. The obituary says "after a long illness." The family knows, and the doctor knows, and nobody says it out loud.

Case and Deaton included chronic liver disease in their count of deaths of despair for a reason. Drinking to the point of destroying your own liver is, very often, a long, slow version of the same thing that happens in an overdose: a person reaching for something to dull a life they cannot bear, and not stopping.

Alcohol deaths rose gradually for years before the pandemic. Then, during twenty twenty and twenty twenty-one, they jumped. Isolation, fear, boredom, grief, lost jobs, closed churches, and liquor stores that stayed open as essential businesses. Since then the numbers have come down somewhat. But in twenty twenty-four, according to an analysis of federal data by the health research group KFF, deaths caused directly by alcohol were still about twenty percent higher than in twenty nineteen, and about fifty percent higher than a decade earlier.

[PAUSE]

I want to be honest about why alcohol gets less attention than opioids. It is not because it kills fewer people. It is because it is ours. It is woven into our celebrations and our commerce. It is a major industry with a powerful lobby. And it is, for most people who use it, a pleasure that does no lasting harm. Most adults who drink are not in danger. That is true and it matters.

But it is also true that a substance that most people can handle can still destroy those who are already vulnerable: the lonely, the grieving, the unemployed, the person in chronic pain, the person whose father drank and whose grandfather drank. The question is not whether alcohol is evil. The question is whether a society that leaves so many people in pain, and then surrounds them with an easy anesthetic, should be surprised when some of them use it to disappear.

[SLOW]

There is one more thing to say about alcohol. Its deaths fall heavily on people in middle age and later. In twenty twenty-four, among Americans aged forty-five and older, there were nearly thirty-seven thousand deaths caused directly by alcohol, and the rate among men was more than double the rate among women.

These are not the teenagers we warn in school assemblies. These are fathers and grandfathers, mothers and aunts, people who held on through decades of hard work and then, somewhere along the way, began to let go.

[LET IT LAND]

And this brings me to the subject I promised to approach carefully. Because when we talk about people letting go, we are coming close to the hardest subject of all.

## VII. The Hardest Subject

I am going to speak now about suicide, and I want to tell you how I am going to do it.

I am not going to tell you anyone's story in a way that turns it into a drama. I am not going to describe how anyone died. I am not going to suggest, even for a moment, that suicide is a reasonable answer to a hard life, or a statement, or a way out. It is none of those things. It is a tragedy, and most of all it is a preventable one.

[PAUSE]

If you are listening and you are struggling, I want to say it again, early, so you do not have to wait for it. Call or text nine eight eight. You can also chat online through the nine eight eight Lifeline. It is open every hour of every day of the year, and it is free, and it is confidential. You do not have to be sure that you are in crisis to call. You only have to be hurting.

Now, the facts.

In twenty twenty-four, forty-eight thousand, eight hundred and twenty-four Americans died by suicide. The year before, it was a little over forty-nine thousand. The age-adjusted rate, which lets us compare years fairly as the population grows and ages, was thirteen point seven per hundred thousand people in twenty twenty-four, down a little from fourteen point one the year before.

[SLOW]

That small decline is welcome. But look at the longer view. In twenty fourteen the rate was thirteen. By twenty seventeen it was fourteen. And since then it has stayed, stubbornly, on a high plateau. The CDC's own analysts say the twenty twenty-four figure was not meaningfully different from that plateau.

Suicide is now, once again, one of the ten leading causes of death in the United States.

Men die by suicide at roughly three to four times the rate of women. In twenty twenty-four, the rate for men was about twenty-two per hundred thousand; for women, under six. And here is a fact that surprises many people: the highest rate of all, for any group the CDC reported, was among men aged seventy-five and older. Forty per hundred thousand. Among women, the highest rate was in midlife, between forty-five and sixty-four.

[PAUSE]

We tend to imagine suicide as a problem of the young, and it is a terrible problem among the young; it is a leading cause of death for teenagers and young adults. But it is also a problem of the old man whose wife has died and whose friends are gone and whose children live a thousand miles away. It is a problem of the middle-aged woman caring for everyone and cared for by no one. It is a problem of the veteran, the farmer, the laid-off machinist.

Look at where the rates are highest. In twenty twenty-four the states with the highest suicide rates were Alaska, Wyoming, Montana, New Mexico, and South Dakota. The lowest were the District of Columbia, New Jersey, New York, Massachusetts, and Rhode Island.

[SLOW]

Think about what separates those two lists. The first is a list of vast, thinly populated places, beautiful places, where your nearest neighbor may be miles away, where winters are long, where the nearest psychiatrist may be a day's drive, and where self-reliance is not just a virtue but a way of life. The second is a list of dense places, crowded places, places where help is closer and people are harder to avoid.

I do not say this to blame the West, or to romanticize the cities. I say it because it tells us something about what protects people. Distance from help is dangerous. Isolation is dangerous. And so is easy access, in a moment of crisis, to the means of ending a life.

[PAUSE]

I will say one thing about that, and only one, because it is the most important thing public health has learned. More than half of all suicide deaths in this country involve a firearm. Suicidal crises are often brief. A person who is kept safe through the worst hours of a crisis very often comes through it. That is why suicide prevention experts urge families, when someone they love is in crisis, to put time and distance between that person and lethal means: to lock things away, or to ask a trusted friend to hold them for a while. This is not a political statement. It is what the evidence says saves lives.

[SLOW]

Now let me tell you what the deaths of despair framework adds to our understanding of suicide.

For a long time we thought of suicide almost entirely as an individual illness, a matter of brain chemistry and private suffering, to be treated one patient at a time. And it is that; mental illness is real, and treatment works, and nothing I say tonight should discourage anyone from seeking it.

But more than a century ago, a French sociologist named Emile Durkheim noticed something that individual explanations could not account for. Suicide rates were remarkably stable within societies from year to year, and remarkably different between them. Something about the society itself, its bonds, its rules, its sense of shared purpose, was shaping how many of its members chose to die. I will return to Durkheim, because I think he holds a key to this whole subject.

What Case and Deaton found is that suicide was rising, in the early two thousands, among the same people who were dying of overdoses and liver disease: middle-aged Americans without a college degree, in the places where work and community had been draining away. The three causes of death were moving together, as if they were three symptoms of one disease.

[LET IT LAND]

I do not want to overstate this. Every suicide is the end of a particular person, with a particular history, and no theory captures all of them. But when the same people, in the same places, start dying in three different ways at the same time, you are entitled to ask whether something larger than individual illness is at work.

[PAUSE]

And there is reason for hope here, real hope. In October of twenty twenty, Congress passed, and the President signed, a law designating nine eight eight as the national number for suicide and mental health crises. On July sixteenth, twenty twenty-two, it went live across the country. Three digits, easy to remember, like nine one one.

It is not a cure. A phone line cannot rebuild a town or bring back a job or heal a marriage. Its funding and its staffing have been the subject of real political fights, and they will need defending. But it is a door, and it is open, and every night people walk through it who would not otherwise have known where to go.

[SLOW]

Here is what I want every person in this room to take home. If someone you know is struggling, ask them directly. Asking someone whether they are thinking about suicide does not put the idea in their head; prevention experts are clear on this. It tells them that you see them, and that you are not afraid of their pain. Listen. Stay with them. Help them connect to nine eight eight, or to a doctor, or to someone they trust.

That is not a small thing. In a country where so many people have come to feel unseen, being seen can be the beginning of staying alive.

## VIII. A Map of Sorrow

I have mentioned places. Now I want to show you the map.

If you took a map of the United States and shaded each county by its rate of deaths from drugs, alcohol, and suicide, you would not see a random scatter. You would see patterns, and the patterns would tell a story about this country's history.

[PAUSE]

The first and darkest stain would fall across Appalachia: eastern Kentucky, southern West Virginia, southwestern Virginia, eastern Tennessee, southern Ohio, western Pennsylvania. In twenty sixteen, West Virginia had the highest overdose death rate in the nation, fifty-two deaths for every hundred thousand people. By twenty seventeen, the rates of deaths of despair were especially high across Appalachia.

Why Appalachia? It is not because the people there are weaker or worse than anyone else. Anyone who says that has never met them. It is because Appalachia had, in concentrated form, every risk at once. Hard physical work in mines and mills and timber that left people injured and in pain. The collapse of that work, as coal declined and factories closed. Distance from hospitals and treatment. A flood of prescription pills, aimed straight at the region by distributors and pharmacies. And a long history of being exploited by outside companies who took the coal and the timber and left the people behind.

[SLOW]

The second stain would spread across the old industrial heartland: the factory towns of Ohio, Michigan, Indiana, Pennsylvania, upstate New York, New England. Places whose names were once stamped on products sold around the world. Places where, for two generations, a young man or woman without a college degree could walk out of high school and into a job that paid enough to buy a house, raise children, and retire with a pension.

The third stain would spread across the rural West and the Great Plains, where, as I have said, suicide rates are the highest in the country.

[PAUSE]

And there are other stains on the map that the original story of deaths of despair did not emphasize enough, and I want to name them, because honesty requires it.

The first is Indian Country. American Indian and Alaska Native people have had some of the highest rates of overdose death in the nation. In twenty twenty-two, their overdose death rate was the highest of any racial or ethnic group the CDC reported, more than sixty-five per hundred thousand, double the national rate. Native communities also face some of the highest suicide rates in the country. These are communities that experienced, generations before the factories closed in Ohio, every form of dispossession Durkheim could have named: the loss of land, of livelihood, of language, of the institutions that held life together. If you want to understand what happens to people when the structures of meaning are taken from them, you could do worse than to start there.

[SLOW]

The second is Black America, and especially Black Americans in cities.

The first wave of the opioid epidemic hit white Americans hardest, partly, researchers have argued, because white patients were more likely to be prescribed opioid painkillers in the first place. But the third wave, the fentanyl wave, did not discriminate in that way. According to Case and Deaton's own later work, drug and alcohol deaths among Black Americans kept falling until about twenty thirteen. Then the trend turned. As fentanyl spread through the illicit supply, overdose deaths among Black Americans rose steeply. People who had lived for years with addiction, and survived it, found that the supply had changed under them, and that the margin for error had vanished.

In twenty twenty-four, two researchers at UCLA, Joseph Friedman and Helena Hansen, argued that deaths of despair among Black Americans had been extensive and overlooked: by policymakers, by the medical establishment, and by the media. I think they are right to say so.

[PAUSE]

Why does this matter? Because for a while, the phrase deaths of despair became associated, in our politics, with one particular group: the white working class of the Rust Belt and Appalachia. And that association was used, by people on every side, to tell a story about politics and grievance and identity.

I want to reject that move tonight, as plainly as I can. The suffering of a laid-off white steelworker in Ohio and the suffering of a Black grandfather in Baltimore and the suffering of a young Lakota woman in South Dakota are not rivals. They are not competing for a limited supply of sympathy. They are three windows into the same house.

[PAUSE]

And the map is not standing still. When the CDC released its provisional count for twenty twenty-five, almost every state had seen a decline in overdose deaths. Rhode Island, New York, North Carolina, Alabama, and Vermont had fallen by a quarter or more. But three states had moved the other way. In New Mexico, Arizona, and Colorado, overdose deaths rose by ten percent or more.

That is what an epidemic does when it is beaten back in one place. It moves. The fentanyl wave first broke hardest in the East and the industrial Midwest, and it has been rolling west ever since. The lesson for the states where deaths are still rising is that they do not have to learn everything the hard way. The East has already paid for the knowledge of what works. The West should not have to pay for it again.

And one more thing on the map, which runs through every county on it. An analysis of the twenty twenty-four federal data by researchers at the University of Minnesota found that the burden of overdose death fell most heavily on two groups: American Indian and Alaska Native people, and people who had not finished high school. Region by region, the map changes. That pattern does not.

[SLOW]

Frederick Douglass, standing in Rochester in July of eighteen fifty-two, asked his audience what their celebration of liberty could mean to people who had been denied it. He would not let them hide inside a comfortable national story. He made them look at who had been left out of it.

I want to ask a question in that spirit. What, to the man in the hollow in eastern Kentucky, is a record national life expectancy? What, to the woman on the reservation, is a falling overdose rate in the state capital? What, to the grandfather in the rowhouse, is the news that deaths are back down to where they were in twenty nineteen?

[BUILD]

To them, the national average is a story about someone else. The map tells the truth that the average conceals. And the truth is that, in this country, where you live and who you are and how much schooling you had now shapes how long you will live more than it has in generations.

Which brings me to the sharpest line on the map. It does not run between states or races or regions. It runs through every one of them. It is the line drawn by a piece of paper: a college diploma.

## IX. The Line Through the Diploma

Here is the single most important fact I know about deaths of despair.

In the early nineteen nineties, an American adult with a four-year college degree could expect to live a little more than two years longer than an American adult without one. That was a gap, but it was a modest one.

On the eve of the pandemic, that gap had grown to about five years. By the end of twenty twenty-one, it was more than seven.

[LET IT LAND]

Seven years. That is the difference a bachelor's degree now makes in how long an American adult can expect to live. It is a gap that has opened in one generation, inside one country, between neighbors.

Anne Case put it starkly. For Americans with a college degree, life expectancy had kept rising, decade after decade. But for those without one, she said, that progress had stopped cold around twenty ten. And for nearly a decade before the pandemic, life expectancy for adults without a bachelor's degree was actually falling. Then the pandemic hit both groups, and it hit those without a degree harder.

[PAUSE]

Think about what that means. The majority of American adults do not have a four-year degree. For them, the long American story of each generation living longer than the one before had already ended, before anyone had heard of COVID-nineteen.

And the gap is not only about deaths of despair. Case and Deaton found that even for heart disease and cancer, the great killers, where death rates have fallen enormously over thirty years, the gap between the educated and the less educated has been growing. The college-educated have been getting the benefits of medical progress faster.

[SLOW]

Now, I want to be careful here, because there is a dangerous way to hear this.

The dangerous way is to conclude that people without degrees are dying because they made bad choices, or because they are less intelligent, or because they did not try hard enough. That conclusion is wrong, and I want to tell you why.

A college degree is not magic. Sitting in a lecture hall does not protect your liver. What the degree has become, in the American economy of the last forty years, is a ticket. It is the ticket to the jobs that pay well, that come with health insurance, that do not wreck your body, that have a future. It is the ticket into the neighborhoods with good schools and the social circles where marriage is stable and the networks that help you find the next job when the last one ends.

[PAUSE]

When I was growing up, and when many of you were growing up, there was another ticket. You could finish high school and go to work in a plant, or a mine, or a trade, and if you worked hard and showed up, you could build a decent life. Not a rich life. A decent one. A life with a house and a truck and a fishing boat, maybe, and a union card and a pension, and the respect of your neighbors.

That second ticket has been quietly withdrawn. Not entirely; there are still good jobs in the trades and we should honor them. But for millions of Americans, the path that their fathers and mothers walked has been closed, and no one came to tell them it was closing, and no one built a new path in its place.

[SLOW]

And here is the cruelty of it. As the degree became more valuable, the country began, subtly and then not so subtly, to treat people without one as if they had failed a test. We told them, every day, in a hundred ways, that the future belonged to the educated, the credentialed, the people who moved to the cities and worked with their minds. We told them that if they had been smart, they would have gone to college. We told them that their kind of work was the past.

Is it any wonder that some of them came to believe it? Is it any wonder that some of them stopped expecting to matter?

[PAUSE]

I said I would push back on Case and Deaton where I think they are wrong, or at least incomplete, and here is the first place. The diploma line is real. But it can be misread as a story about education when it is really a story about what we have done to work, and to the people who do it with their hands. You cannot fix this simply by sending everyone to college. Not everyone wants to go, and not everyone should, and a society that can only offer a decent life to those with a bachelor's degree has made a decision about whose lives count. It is a decision we can unmake.

[BUILD]

Because the line through the diploma is not a law of nature. It does not exist, to anything like the same degree, in other rich countries. Our neighbors in Europe and Canada have educational divides too. But they did not see their less-educated citizens begin to die younger in the way ours did. Something about the American arrangement, our labor market, our health care system, our safety net, our communities, turned a difference in schooling into a difference of seven years of life.

We built that arrangement. Which means we can rebuild it.

[PAUSE]

Let me say a word here about the founding promise of this country, because Frederick Douglass taught us how to read it.

When Douglass rose in Corinthian Hall in Rochester in eighteen fifty-two, at the invitation of the Rochester Ladies' Anti-Slavery Society, he did not begin by attacking the Declaration of Independence. He began by honoring it. He praised the men who signed it, and he called its principles saving principles. He told his audience to stand by them. And only then, having granted the promise its full glory, did he turn and ask what that promise meant to the millions of people from whom it was withheld. His indictment was so powerful precisely because it was made in the name of the nation's own ideals.

I want to make a far smaller argument, in that same manner.

[SLOW]

The Declaration speaks of three rights: life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness. Notice the order. Life comes first. Before a person can be free, before a person can pursue anything, that person must be alive.

We are a nation that has spent a great deal of energy arguing about liberty, and a great deal arguing about happiness. We have spent much less arguing about the plain first right, the right to be alive, and about whether it is being honored equally among us. When a diploma predicts seven years of life, when the place you happen to live shapes how long you can expect to live, the first promise of the Declaration is being kept for some and quietly broken for others.

And the pursuit of happiness is not, in the founders' language, a promise of pleasure. It is a promise of the chance to build a life of meaning, a life with a future in it. For millions of our fellow citizens, that pursuit has come to feel like a race whose finish line was moved somewhere they cannot follow. Despair, in the end, is what happens to the pursuit of happiness when people stop believing that the race is open to them.

[PAUSE]

I do not say these things to tear down the founding promise. I say them, as Douglass did, to hold the nation to it. The promise is still good. It is still ours. And it is still unkept.

So let us look at the first pillar of it, the one most people point to first: work.

## X. The Work That Went Away

There is a kind of American town that many of you know, or come from, or have driven through without stopping.

Let me describe one. She is not any particular town; she is a composite, put together from many. There is a main street with a courthouse and a diner and, these days, a dollar store, a pawn shop, a vape shop, and a storefront church. At the edge of town there is a long brick building with broken windows and a chain-link fence, and the name of a company painted on the side, faded now, that once employed a third of the men in the county and a good share of the women.

[PAUSE]

When the plant was running, it did more than pay wages. It gave the day a shape. It gave a young man a place to go at eighteen, and a set of older men to teach him, and a reason to get out of bed. It sponsored the Little League team. Its workers filled the pews and the union hall and the volunteer fire department. It gave people something to be proud of, and a story to tell about who they were.

And then, one year, it closed. Maybe the work went to Mexico, or to China, or to a plant in another state with lower wages, or to a machine. The reasons do not matter much to the people who were left.

[SLOW]

Case and Deaton date the beginning of the long decline for working-class Americans to the early nineteen seventies, when the great postwar boom in blue-collar work came to an end. Each new generation of Americans without a college degree, they found, entered a worse labor market than the one before. Each cohort was less likely to be working, at the same age, than their parents had been.

And then, in the first decade of this century, came what economists now call the China shock. When the United States normalized trade relations with China around the turn of the millennium, and China joined the World Trade Organization, a flood of imports transformed American manufacturing. Economists still argue about how many jobs were lost because of it; serious estimates range from roughly half a million to more than two million. But the economists David Autor, David Dorn, and Gordon Hanson showed something that the national numbers had hidden. The losses were concentrated. In the places most exposed to Chinese competition, they found higher unemployment, lower participation in the workforce, and lower wages, and those effects lingered for years.

[PAUSE]

For a long time, the official economic story was that trade made the country richer as a whole, and that the losers could be compensated and retrained. The first half of that story was largely true. Consumers got cheaper goods; the economy as a whole grew. The second half of the story, the compensation, the retraining, the new jobs in new industries, never arrived at the scale that was promised. Autor and his colleagues have said as much: the offsetting gains in other industries, in the places that were hit, did not clearly appear.

[SLOW]

I want to be honest about the limits of this argument, because a speech that blames everything on trade is as lazy as one that blames everything on pills.

Other rich countries also lost manufacturing jobs. Britain lost its mines. Germany and France and Sweden faced the same globalization, the same automation, the same competition from Asia. Some of them had higher unemployment than we did. And yet their middle-aged working people did not start dying in the way ours did.

So job loss alone cannot be the whole answer. The question is what else America did, or failed to do, that turned economic hardship into death.

[PAUSE]

Here, I think, Case and Deaton make one of their most important arguments, and one that gets too little attention. They point to the American health care system.

In most rich countries, health care is paid for through taxes or through tightly regulated insurance, and losing your job does not mean losing your doctor. In the United States, for most working-age people, health insurance comes through an employer. That means it is, in effect, part of your wages. And as health care costs have risen, year after year, faster than the economy, those costs have eaten into what employers are willing to pay in cash. Case and Deaton describe our health care sector as one that, in their view, transfers money from working-class wages upward. It makes the less-educated worker more expensive to hire, and it leaves less in his paycheck.

And when the job is gone, so, often, is the coverage. The man with the bad back who loses his job at the plant loses the insurance that paid for his physical therapy, at the very moment the pill bottle in his medicine cabinet is running low and the street is offering a cheaper alternative.

[LET IT LAND]

So it was not one blow. It was a series of them, each landing on the same bruise. The good jobs left. The jobs that remained paid less and offered less security. Health care costs ate the raises. The union hall emptied. The pills arrived. And at every stage, the people who were losing were told that this was simply how the market worked, that it was nobody's fault, that they should move, or retrain, or try harder.

[PAUSE]

There is a piece of advice that the losers of economic change hear more than any other. Move. Go where the jobs are. Pack up and head for the growing cities on the coasts or in the Sun Belt.

It is advice that sounds sensible in a seminar room and cruel in a kitchen. Consider what it asks. It asks a family to sell a house in a town where nobody is buying houses, for a fraction of what they paid, and buy one in a city where prices have doubled. It asks them to leave the grandparents who watch the children, the church that knows their names, the brother who will help fix the roof, the cemetery where their parents are buried. It asks them to trade every form of wealth that does not show up on a bank statement for a chance at a job that may or may not last.

[SLOW]

Some people take that advice, and some of them thrive. The ones who stay are not foolish. Often they are the ones who are holding the town together: caring for aging parents, coaching the teams, keeping the volunteer fire company running. And by leaving them to fend for themselves, by treating their places as problems to be abandoned rather than communities to be renewed, we have told them that their attachment to home, which in any other context we would call loyalty, is a character flaw.

A country is not an airport, where people pass through on the way to somewhere better. It is a home. And a home owes something to every room in it.

[SLOW]

There is something else work gives a person that does not show up in the wage statistics. It gives dignity. It gives a man or woman the sense of being needed, of contributing, of having a place in the order of things. When we talk about unemployment as if it were only a loss of income, something a check could replace, we miss what is most important about it.

A country that loses that understanding can pay people not to starve and still leave them to despair. Our safety net, with all its gaps, has kept many people from destitution. But it was never designed to give anyone a reason to get up in the morning.

[PAUSE]

Case and Deaton found that alongside the decline in work came a decline in marriage. Each new cohort of working-class Americans was less likely to be married than the last. Families grew more fragile. Children grew up with less stability. Men in particular, cut loose from both work and family, drifted toward the margins, toward the bar stool and the couch and the pill bottle.

I do not say this to scold anyone about their private lives. People do not stop marrying because they have stopped believing in love. Very often they stop marrying because they do not think they, or their partner, can provide the stable life that marriage seems to require. Economic insecurity reaches into the most intimate corners of our lives.

[BUILD]

And so we arrive at a question that economics alone cannot answer. When the work goes, and the family frays, and the town empties, what is left to hold a person to life? What tells him that he matters? What tells her that tomorrow is worth waking up for?

In other ages and other places, the answer to that question would have been given by something larger than the economy. By faith. By community. By a shared story about what a life is for. And that brings me to the part of this subject that is hardest to measure and, I have come to believe, most important.

## XI. The Spiritual Ledger

In eighteen ninety-seven, Emile Durkheim published a book called Suicide. It is one of the founding works of sociology, and its argument is simple and profound.

Durkheim argued that whether people take their own lives is not only a matter of private psychology. It is shaped by two features of the society around them. The first is integration: how tightly people are bound to groups larger than themselves, to family, to congregation, to community. The second is regulation: how far shared norms and expectations give shape and limits to their desires and their lives.

[PAUSE]

When integration is weak, Durkheim said, people become detached, and life comes to seem meaningless. He called the resulting suicides egoistic. And when regulation breaks down, in times of upheaval, of sudden loss or even sudden gain, people lose their bearings. He called that condition anomie: a kind of moral confusion, a loss of social direction, when the old rules no longer apply and no new ones have taken their place.

I have read a great deal about deaths of despair, and I do not know a better description of what has happened to large parts of working-class America than those two words, detachment and anomie.

[SLOW]

Think about the institutions that once integrated ordinary Americans into something larger than themselves. The union local. The Rotary and the Elks and the American Legion. The bowling league. The volunteer fire company. The extended family living within a few miles. And, above all, the church.

In nineteen thirty-seven, when Gallup first asked, seventy-three percent of American adults said they were members of a church, synagogue, or mosque. In nineteen ninety-nine, it was still seventy percent. Then it fell, fast. In twenty twenty, for the first time in the eight decades Gallup has been asking, the number dropped below half, to forty-seven percent.

[PAUSE]

Let me be careful here. I am not standing here to tell anyone what to believe, or to say that people of faith are better than people without it. There are deeply moral people in every tradition and in none, and there are congregations that have wounded people terribly. That is not my point.

My point is about what a congregation does, sociologically, whatever you think of its theology. It gathers people every week. It knows when you are missing. It brings a casserole when your wife dies. It gives the unemployed man a role, as an usher or a deacon or a man who fixes the boiler, that has nothing to do with his paycheck. It tells everyone in the room, every week, that their life has a meaning and a worth that does not depend on their productivity. It offers a story in which suffering is not the end.

When those institutions fade, something has to take their place. In many places, nothing has.

[SLOW]

In May of twenty twenty-three, the Surgeon General of the United States, Dr. Vivek Murthy, issued a formal advisory, the kind usually reserved for threats like smoking, about what he called an epidemic of loneliness and isolation. He placed loneliness alongside smoking and obesity as a profound threat to public health, linked to heart disease, stroke, and dementia.

Think about that. The nation's doctor felt compelled to warn the country that we were, in a very literal sense, dying of loneliness.

[PAUSE]

Here is where I want to bring together the economic and the spiritual, because I think we make a mistake when we treat them as rival explanations.

Some people say: the problem is economic, so give people jobs and money and the despair will lift. Others say: the problem is cultural and moral, a decline in family and faith and responsibility, and no government program can fix it.

I think both are half right, and both are wrong in the same way. They imagine that the economy and the soul are separate rooms. They are not. Work is one of the ways people find meaning. Community is one of the ways people find work. The factory sponsored the Little League team; the church found the laid-off man a job. When the plant closes, the pews empty too, because the young people move away and the people who stay can no longer afford to give. When the church closes, the unemployed man loses the network that might have found him his next job. The material and the spiritual decay together, because they were always holding each other up.

[SLOW]

Case and Deaton themselves came close to this view. They have argued that these deaths track a sense that life has become unfulfilling, more than economic insecurity alone. Despair is not simply being poor. Our grandparents were poorer than we are, by any material measure, and they did not die like this. Despair is the loss of a story in which your life is going somewhere and your community is going somewhere with you.

[PAUSE]

There is a phrase that the old preachers used: a famine of the word. A famine not of bread but of meaning. I think that is what we have had in many American places: not only a famine of good jobs, though we have had that too, but a famine of belonging. And into that famine came the most efficient anesthetic ever devised, sold first by a corporation and then by a cartel.

[LET IT LAND]

I want to honor here the people who are fighting that famine every day, often without much help. The pastor in the mill town who opened the church basement to a recovery meeting. The retired nurse who carries naloxone in her purse. The grandmother raising her grandchildren because her daughter is gone. The man in recovery who drives forty miles each week to sit with someone newer to it than he is. The volunteer who answers the crisis line at three in the morning.

These people are doing, with their own hands, what our institutions have failed to do. They are rebuilding integration, one person at a time. They are the answer to Durkheim. And they deserve far more support than this country has given them.

[PAUSE]

And I want to honor one more group, the largest and the least seen. The families left behind.

Every number I have given you tonight has a circle of people around it. The parents who have buried a child, and who will set a place at the table in their minds for the rest of their lives. The brothers and sisters who wonder what they missed. The children who will grow up knowing a parent only through photographs, or who will be raised by grandparents who thought their parenting years were over. The friends who stopped answering the phone because the news was always bad.

[SLOW]

Grief like that does not appear in the mortality tables. But it is part of the cost, and it compounds. A child who loses a parent to an overdose carries that loss into school, into work, into marriage. A town that buries its young people loses not only those lives but the families they would have raised and the work they would have done. The deaths of despair, left unchecked, plant the seeds of the next generation's despair.

That is why this is not only a medical problem, or an economic problem, or a spiritual problem. It is a problem of inheritance. It is about what we pass down.

[SLOW]

Lincoln, in his second inaugural, did something that few leaders in wartime have ever done. He admitted that he did not fully understand the purposes of the catastrophe that had befallen his country. He spoke of the Almighty having his own purposes, and he suggested, with great humility, that the suffering of the war might be a judgment on the whole nation, North and South together, for a wrong in which all had shared.

I will not claim to know the purposes of providence. But I will say this. A nation that measures its success almost entirely in money, that tells its people their worth is their productivity, that lets its common institutions wither and calls it freedom, should not be astonished when some of its people conclude that their lives are worth nothing. We have been teaching that lesson for fifty years. Some of our neighbors learned it all too well.

## XII. The Strongest Objection

I promised you that I would take on the strongest argument against everything I have said, and now I will.

The argument goes like this. Deaths of despair, it says, is a beautiful phrase and a misleading one. The real story is not despair. It is drugs. Specifically, it is the supply of drugs: first the flood of prescription pills, then cheap heroin, then fentanyl. Look at the timing, the argument says. Overdose deaths did not rise smoothly with economic decline. They rose in waves, and the waves match the drug supply, not the job market.

[PAUSE]

And there is more. If despair were the cause, the argument continues, why did deaths soar in the pandemic and then fall so sharply after twenty twenty-three? The economy of Appalachia was not transformed between twenty twenty-three and twenty twenty-five. Nobody reopened the steel mills. Church attendance did not suddenly revive. And yet overdose deaths fell by roughly a third in two years, and kept falling. What changed was the drug environment: naloxone everywhere, more people in treatment, fentanyl test strips, and possibly changes in the supply itself. The despair stayed. The deaths fell. Doesn't that prove that despair was never the driver?

And finally: why did Black overdose deaths, which had nothing to do with the decline of white working-class identity, explode once fentanyl arrived? Doesn't that show that the drug, not the despair, is the killer?

[SLOW]

This is a serious argument, made by serious people, and I want to concede everything in it that is true.

It is true that the supply of drugs matters enormously. It is true that the waves of the epidemic track the waves of the supply. It is true that fentanyl turned what had been a slow-moving epidemic of addiction into a fast-moving epidemic of poisoning, and that many people who died of fentanyl had no idea they were taking it. It is true that the recent decline shows that you can save huge numbers of lives without first fixing the economy. And it is true that the phrase deaths of despair, as it entered our politics, was too often used to tell a story about one group of Americans and to ignore others.

All of that is true. I said so earlier, and I say it again. If I had to choose between a policy that rebuilds meaning in American towns over thirty years and a policy that puts naloxone in every pocket tomorrow, I would choose the naloxone tomorrow, because a dead person cannot be given meaning.

[PAUSE]

But here is my answer, and I ask you to weigh it.

First: the supply story explains how people died. It does not fully explain why the demand was there, and why it was there in this country, and among these people. The American reversal began around nineteen ninety-nine, years before fentanyl reached our streets. Opioid painkillers were sold in other countries, too. Alcohol is sold everywhere. Yet the United States, alone among rich nations, saw its midlife death rates turn upward. Something about American life created a market of millions of people in enough pain, physical or otherwise, to keep buying a product that was killing them.

[SLOW]

Second: think about the alcohol deaths, and the suicides. Neither of those depends on fentanyl. Neither of them follows the waves of the illicit drug supply. Yet they rose in the same years, among many of the same people, in many of the same places. A theory that explains overdose by supply alone has nothing to say about the man who drinks himself to death, or the woman who takes her own life without any drug at all. The despair explanation, whatever its weaknesses, ties the three together.

Third: the fall in overdose deaths is wonderful, and I celebrate it. But notice what it actually shows. It shows that when you make the means of death harder to find, or easier to survive, fewer people die. That is exactly what suicide prevention experts have said for years about lethal means. It does not show that the suffering has gone away. And the suffering has its own costs, in lost work and broken families and lives lived at half strength, that never appear in a mortality table at all.

[PAUSE]

Fourth: the experience of Black and Native Americans does not refute the despair argument. It deepens it. Communities that have been through generations of dispossession, of discrimination, of disinvestment, of being told in every possible way that their lives counted for less, are communities that know something about despair. The fact that the fentanyl wave struck them so hard is not evidence against the role of social suffering. It is evidence that social suffering is not confined to one race or one region.

[SLOW]

So here is where I come down. Supply and despair are not rival theories. They are the match and the tinder. You need both for a fire. Take away the match, and the tinder sits there, dry and dangerous, waiting for the next spark. Wet the tinder, and the match goes out. The recent decline shows how much we can do with the match. The long history of this epidemic shows how dry the tinder had become.

[PAUSE]

There is one more objection I want to answer, because I hear it often, and it deserves a response rather than a sneer.

It says: in the end, this is a matter of personal responsibility. Nobody forced anyone to take that pill or drink that bottle. Adults make choices, and they must live with the consequences.

I will grant what is true in it. Recovery does require personal responsibility. Every person I know in long-term recovery will tell you that no one could do it for them; they had to choose it, every day. A speech that treats people as nothing but victims of vast forces robs them of their agency and their dignity.

[BUILD]

But personal responsibility cannot explain a trend. Human nature did not change in nineteen ninety-nine. Americans without college degrees did not suddenly become less responsible than their parents, or than Germans, or than Canadians, or than Americans with degrees. When a whole class of people in a whole country starts dying in a new way at the same time, you are not looking at a sudden epidemic of individual weakness. You are looking at a change in the conditions under which individuals make their choices.

We hold individuals responsible for their choices. We hold ourselves, as a people, responsible for the conditions. Both of those things are true. A free people that forgets the second half of that sentence is not being tough-minded. It is simply looking away.

## XIII. What Works and What Is Owed

So what do we do?

I want to answer that question in two parts, because the problem has two parts. There is the emergency, and there is the condition underneath it. We must put out the fire, and we must stop the land from drying out. Let me begin with the fire, because people are dying in it tonight.

[PAUSE]

The first thing we know works is naloxone. It is a medicine that reverses an opioid overdose. Here is how it works, in plain words. Opioids attach to receptors in the brain and the body, and in an overdose they slow a person's breathing until it stops. Naloxone competes for those same receptors and pushes the opioids off them, and the person starts breathing again. If there are no opioids in the person's body, it does nothing at all; it cannot hurt them.

Naloxone nasal spray is now sold without a prescription in this country. That is one of the great public health advances of our time, and most Americans do not know it.

[SLOW]

So here is my first request of everyone in this room. Get naloxone. Keep it in your car, your purse, your kitchen drawer, your workplace first aid kit. Learn how to use it; it takes about a minute. And know this: because its effects can wear off before a strong opioid does, a person who has been revived still needs emergency care. Call for help, and stay with them.

You may never use it. But if you do, you will have done the most important thing one human being can do for another. You will have given them another day. And every person in long-term recovery is someone who was given another day.

[PAUSE]

The second thing we know works is medication for opioid addiction. Two medicines, methadone and buprenorphine, have been studied for decades. Both reduce the risk of death. They do not simply substitute one high for another, as critics sometimes claim; in proper doses they stabilize a person, quiet the cravings, and give them the room to rebuild a life.

France offers a striking example. Starting in the mid-nineteen nineties, it allowed ordinary doctors to prescribe buprenorphine. Between nineteen ninety-four and two thousand two, heroin overdose deaths in France fell by four-fifths. I will be honest: researchers cannot prove that the medicine alone caused that decline. But it is hard to ignore.

[SLOW]

For years, in this country, doctors who wanted to prescribe buprenorphine for addiction had to obtain a special federal waiver, and many never did. At the end of twenty twenty-two, Congress, in a bipartisan spending bill, abolished that requirement. Any doctor who can prescribe controlled medicines can now prescribe it for addiction, without a special license and without a cap on the number of patients.

That was a good law. It was passed quietly, by both parties, and it deserves more credit than it has received.

But a law that allows treatment is not the same as treatment. Over the decade from twenty ten to twenty nineteen, by one estimate, about six in seven Americans who could have benefited from treatment for opioid addiction were not receiving it. Six in seven. And we know that the most dangerous weeks are the first weeks after treatment begins and the first weeks after it ends. Which means that keeping people in treatment, making it easy to stay, not cutting them off over a missed appointment or a paperwork error, is not a kindness. It is life and death.

[PAUSE]

The third thing we know is that prescribing needed to change, and it has. The rate at which opioid prescriptions are dispensed in this country fell from about forty-seven per hundred people in twenty nineteen to about thirty-five per hundred in twenty twenty-four, and it had already fallen a long way before that.

But I want to sound a note of caution, in the spirit of Lincoln's restraint. Pendulums swing. A country that once handed out pills too freely can become a country that turns away people in real and terrible pain, and treats them all as suspects. Chronic pain is real. It is one of the conditions that drove this epidemic. The answer to bad prescribing is not to abandon the people in pain. It is to give them better care: physical therapy, careful medicine, time with a doctor who listens. Things our system has always found it easier to deny than to pay for.

[PAUSE]

There is a fourth thing that has helped, and it is the hardest for some people to accept. It goes by the name of harm reduction. It means meeting people where they are, rather than where we wish they were. It means handing out naloxone without asking questions. It means test strips that let a person check whether a drug contains fentanyl. It means outreach workers who keep in touch with people who are still using, so that when the day comes that they are ready for treatment, there is someone they trust to call.

Some people hear all this and think it sends the wrong message, that it condones drug use. I understand the worry. But I would ask them to consider the alternative message, the one we sent for decades: that if you are using drugs, you are on your own until you are ready to quit. Too many people died waiting to be ready. A living person can still recover. A dead person cannot. Harm reduction is not the opposite of recovery. For many people, it is the road that leads there.

[SLOW]

Now let me speak honestly about the most contested question in drug policy: how the law should treat people who use drugs.

In two thousand one, Portugal stopped treating the possession of small amounts of drugs as a crime. It remained illegal, but it became an administrative matter, handled by local panels that included a social worker, a psychiatrist, and a lawyer, and that could steer people toward treatment. In the years after, drug deaths in Portugal fell dramatically, from one hundred and thirty-one in two thousand one to twenty in two thousand eight. But I must also tell you that deaths later rose again, and researchers still argue about how much of the early improvement the policy itself caused.

In twenty twenty, the voters of Oregon, by about fifty-eight percent, passed a measure that decriminalized the possession of hard drugs and promised to fund treatment. Less than four years later, amid a public backlash, the legislature reversed course, and starting in September of twenty twenty-four, possession became a crime again, though the state kept the expanded treatment funding.

[PAUSE]

What do I take from these two stories? Not that one side of the debate is simply right. I take this: changing the law without building the treatment, the housing, and the follow-up to go with it does not work, and the public will not tolerate it for long. And arresting people without offering treatment does not work either; we have fifty years of evidence for that. The common thread, the thing that works wherever it is tried seriously, is the patient, expensive, unglamorous work of getting people into care and keeping them there.

[SLOW]

And that brings me to the question of who pays.

For most Americans with low incomes, the insurance that pays for addiction treatment is Medicaid. On July fourth, twenty twenty-five, the President signed a major budget law that, among many other things, added a work requirement to Medicaid for many adults between nineteen and sixty-four: eighty hours a month of work, school, or volunteering. The Congressional Budget Office estimated that the law would leave about ten point nine million more Americans without health insurance by twenty thirty-four.

I want to be fair to the people who wrote that law. They believe, sincerely, that work is good for people, that it gives them dignity and purpose. I have spent a good part of this speech saying the same thing. On that, we agree.

[PAUSE]

But here is where we part. A person in the first months of recovery from addiction, or in the grip of a depression, or caring for a dying parent, may not be able to document eighty hours of work a month. If losing coverage means losing the medicine that keeps them alive, then a policy meant to encourage work may instead end a life. The exemptions in that law, and how they are enforced, and whether they reach the people who need them, will be among the most important details in American public health over the next decade. I ask everyone who cares about this subject to watch them closely, and to insist that no one in treatment is cut off by a paperwork deadline.

[SLOW]

That is the emergency. Now for the condition beneath it, and here I must speak more as a citizen than as an expert, because there is no randomized trial for rebuilding a town.

We must make work pay for people without degrees again. That means valuing the trades, and apprenticeships, and the community colleges that train people for real jobs, as much as we value the four-year university. It means asking whether our health care system, which ties insurance to employment and lets its costs eat into working people's wages, is serving the people it is supposed to serve. It means that when we make economic decisions, about trade or technology or anything else, that will make the country richer as a whole, we take seriously our obligation to the particular places that will pay the price, not with a pamphlet about retraining, but with real investment that lasts.

[PAUSE]

We must rebuild the institutions of belonging. Government cannot create a church or a bowling league or a friendship. But it can stop making them harder to sustain. It can fund the recovery community centers and the peer support workers, many of them people in recovery themselves, who are often the first to reach someone in trouble. It can keep the libraries and the parks and the community centers open. And the billions of dollars now flowing to states and counties from the opioid settlements should be spent on exactly these things, on treatment and recovery and prevention, not plugged into budget holes and forgotten.

[SLOW]

And we must, every one of us, become part of the answer in our own small way. Learn the signs. Carry the naloxone. Ask the hard question. Call the lonely neighbor. Sit with the person who is struggling. Hire the person in recovery. Show up at the meeting in the church basement and make the coffee. Answer the phone.

None of this is beyond us. We are the country that built the interstate highways and the land-grant colleges and put human beings on the moon. We are the country that, after the Second World War, sent millions of veterans to college and built them homes. We know how to invest in our people when we decide that they matter.

[LET IT LAND]

The only question is whether we will decide that they matter.

## XIV. Peroration

Fellow citizens, I have kept you a long time, and I am nearly done.

I began with a number, a record life expectancy, and I asked you to hold it up to the light. Let me tell you now what I see when I hold it up.

[PAUSE]

I see a country that knows how to save lives. We cut overdose deaths by roughly a third in two years, by putting a medicine in people's pockets and opening the doors to treatment. We made a three-digit number that anyone in crisis can call. When we decided to act, we acted, and people are alive tonight because we did.

And I see a country that has not yet decided to act on the deeper thing. Under the record average there is a line that runs through every state and every town, and on one side of it people are living longer than any Americans have ever lived, and on the other side they are living shorter lives than their parents. Seven years of life now separate them. Seven years. And we have built that line ourselves, out of a thousand choices, and we can unbuild it.

[SLOW]

I will not tell you who is to blame. I have named the companies that lied, and the courts that judged them, and I will not take back a word. But I have also tried to show you that the wrong runs through all of us: through the boardroom and the pharmacy, through the Congress and the clinic, through the economists who promised that the losers would be compensated, through the rest of us who stopped driving through those towns, and stopped asking about the neighbor we had not seen in church for a while.

If there is judgment in all this, it falls on the whole nation, not on one part of it. And if there is to be healing, it must come from the whole nation, too.

[PAUSE]

So I will not end with blame. I will end with a charge.

To those who govern: do not mistake a falling number for a finished task. Seventy thousand dead in a year is not victory. Fund the treatment. Protect the coverage. Keep the crisis line staffed. Spend the settlement money on the living. And when you make the great decisions about trade and technology and health care, remember the town with the empty plant at the edge of it, and count its people among the people you serve.

To those who heal: treat the pain and treat the person. Do not abandon the patient who hurts. Do not turn away the patient who relapses. Offer the medicine that works, and keep offering it.

[BUILD]

To those who worship, and to those who do not: open your doors. Open your basements, your halls, your kitchens. Be the place where someone is missed when they do not come. Be the integration that Durkheim said a society cannot live without. You do not need permission from Washington to bring a casserole or to sit with someone at three in the morning.

To those who employ: hire the person with the gap in his work history. Hire the woman in recovery. Give the young man without a diploma the apprenticeship and the older man who taught him the respect. Work is not only a wage. It is a place in the world.

And to those of you who are hurting tonight, who have heard this whole speech as a description of your own life: you are not a statistic, and you are not alone, and you are not finished. Recovery is real. People come back from the very edge every single day, and many of them go on to pull others back. Call or text nine eight eight. Tell someone the truth. Let someone help you carry it.

[PAUSE]

I said at the start that a death certificate cannot record despair. It records the poison and the injury and the failing liver. It cannot record the closed plant, the emptied church, the lost marriage, the long years of feeling that you no longer counted.

[SLOW]

But we can record it. We can write it down in the record of how we choose to live together from here on. We can write that, in our time, the richest nation in history looked at the line on the chart that two economists drew, and did not look away. That we counted the dead, every one of them, in the hollows and the rowhouses and on the reservations and in the mill towns. That we refused to believe that any American life is surplus. That we rebuilt the work, and the coverage, and the congregations, and the friendships, that hold a person to the earth.

[BUILD]

Let us be the generation that turned the line back around. Let us measure our progress not by the average, but by the person at the bottom of it. Let us care for those who carry pain, and for the families who have buried their children, and for the children raised by grandparents, and for the towns that the country left behind. Let us do it without contempt for anyone, and without excuse for anyone, and with patience enough for the long work ahead.

[SLOW]

The despair was made. It was made by choices, and it can be unmade by choices.

[PAUSE]

Let us choose, together, to give every American a reason to see the morning.

[LET IT LAND]

Thank you.
