# A People That Wants a Future

## I. The Courtyard at Fontainebleau

I want to begin with a man saying goodbye.

It is the twentieth of April, eighteen fourteen. The place is a courtyard at the palace of Fontainebleau, south of Paris. Two weeks earlier the Emperor of the French signed his abdication. Paris has fallen. The armies of Europe are in the capital. And the man who for twenty years had remade the map of a continent is about to leave for an island in the Mediterranean.

Before he goes, he comes down the great horseshoe staircase to the men who are left. The Old Guard. The veterans. Soldiers who had followed him across the Alps and into the snows of Russia and back again.

[PAUSE]

He does not give them a lecture on strategy. He does not blame them. He does not blame himself either, which tells you something about the man. He tells them that he is going so that France will not be torn apart by a civil war. He tells them that he will write down what they did together. He tells them to serve the country that remains.

And then comes the moment that painters and storytellers have kept alive for two centuries. He says that he cannot embrace every one of them. So he embraces their general, General Petit, in the name of them all. And he asks for the eagle, the standard they had carried, and he kisses it.

The historians at the Fondation Napoleon will tell you, honestly, that this scene has been polished by legend and by propaganda. The words vary from account to account. The tears may have grown with each retelling. I tell it to you as what it has become, a story about farewell.

[SLOW]

But notice what the story is about. It is about a man who could not hold everyone he loved, and so he held one person, and one symbol, on behalf of all of them.

Keep that picture in your mind. I am going to return to it at the end.

[PAUSE]

Tonight I want to talk about a different kind of farewell. Not a farewell to an emperor, but a farewell to an assumption.

For almost all of human history, the people who lived in a place assumed that there would be more people after them. Not always richer. Not always safer. But more. More children in the schools. More hands in the fields and the factories. More voices at the table on the holidays. The future was a room that would be fuller than the present.

That assumption is leaving us. Quietly. Without a ceremony in a palace courtyard. In country after country, the number of children being born has fallen below the number needed to replace the parents. In some places it has fallen so far that each generation is on track to be little more than half the size of the one before it.

This is the fifth and last speech in a series about that fact. The earlier speeches counted the empty cradles, priced the cost of a child, weighed the career against the family, and looked at a nation that has stopped marrying.

This one is different. This one is a call.

[BUILD]

I am going to ask a question that every serious country on earth is now asking. What actually works? Not what feels good to say. Not what wins a news cycle. What has actually been tried, by real governments, with real money, over real decades, and what happened?

We are going to go to France, which has been trying for almost a hundred years. To Hungary, which bet more than almost anyone and has just watched the bet come due. To the Nordic countries, which built the most generous family policy the world has ever seen and still watched their birth rates fall by a quarter or a third. To South Korea, which spent a fortune and reached the lowest fertility rate ever recorded in a modern nation. And to Israel, the rich country that somehow never got the memo.

We are going to talk about money, about houses, about child allowances and tax credits. And then we are going to talk about something money cannot buy, which is the belief that the future is worth entering.

[PAUSE]

Here is my thesis, so you can test everything I say against it.

Pronatal policy is not a vending machine. You cannot put in dollars and get out babies. The record of the last thirty years proves that beyond any reasonable doubt.

But the record also proves something else. It proves that the gap between the families people say they want and the families they actually have is real, that parts of that gap can be closed, and that the countries which come closest to closing it are the ones that combine patient, generous, boring policy with a culture that treats children as a good thing and the future as a good place.

And it proves, I think, that wanting children is in the end an act of faith. A bet placed on a future you will not live to see. A people that wants a future has to build a country where that bet looks reasonable.

[LET IT LAND]

That is what I want to argue for the next two hours. Not with malice. Not with blame. Not by telling any person what to do with their own life. But as one citizen speaking to others about the one inheritance that every society must pass on or lose, which is the next generation itself.

## II. The Count

Let us start with the count. I will go slowly, because numbers spoken aloud are easy to lose.

Demographers use a figure called the total fertility rate. It is the number of children a woman would have over her lifetime if she lived through today's birth rates at every age. To replace a population without immigration, a rich country needs a figure of about two point one. Two to replace the parents, and a little more because not every child grows to adulthood.

Now listen to where the world stands.

In the United States, the Centers for Disease Control reported in April of this year that about three million, six hundred and six thousand babies were born in twenty twenty-five. That was down about one percent from the year before. The general fertility rate, which counts births per thousand women of childbearing age, fell to fifty-three point one, a record low. The total fertility rate, as reported by the Wall Street Journal from the same data, fell to about one point five seven.

One point five seven. Not two point one. About three quarters of replacement.

And one more detail from that report, which I think tells the story of a whole generation. In twenty twenty-five, for the first time, women in their late thirties had a higher birth rate than women in their early twenties.

[PAUSE]

Cross the Atlantic. France, for decades the great exception of Europe, the country that seemed to have figured it out. In January the national statistics office, INSEE, reported that French fertility had fallen to one point five six in twenty twenty-five. That is the lowest level since the end of the First World War.

And for the first time since the end of the Second World War, more people died in France than were born. About six hundred and fifty-one thousand deaths. About six hundred and forty-five thousand births. A gap of about six thousand. Small in number. Enormous in meaning.

Go north. Finland recorded a fertility rate of one point two five in twenty twenty-four. Statistics Finland keeps records going back to seventeen seventy-six. That was the lowest figure in all of them. Last year it ticked up, to one point three zero. Sweden fell to one point four two in twenty twenty-five, the lowest ever measured there. Norway rose, a little, to one point four eight.

Go east. Hungary, which we will discuss at length, fell to about one point three one last year, according to preliminary figures. Fewer babies were born there than in any year since nineteen forty-nine. Poland, which launched one of Europe's most famous child benefit programs, recorded a fertility rate of about one point one in twenty twenty-four. In that year about two hundred and fifty-two thousand Poles were born, and about four hundred and nine thousand died.

[SLOW]

Go further east still. Japan recorded about six hundred and seventy-one thousand births last year, the tenth record low in a row, with a fertility rate of one point one four.

And then there is South Korea. In twenty twenty-three, the Korean fertility rate fell to zero point seven two. Not one point seven. Zero point seven. At that rate, every hundred adults would have, in the next generation, about thirty-six children, and those thirty-six would have about thirteen.

Last year Korea climbed to about zero point eight. We will talk about why, because it matters.

[PAUSE]

And then, almost alone among rich countries, there is Israel. Close to three children per woman. Above replacement. A modern, educated, high-income, high-technology country with a fertility rate that the rest of the developed world has not seen in two generations.

Hold all these numbers loosely in your mind. You do not need to remember them. Remember the shape.

The shape is this. Nearly every rich country in the world is below replacement. Most are far below. The ones that were thought to be exceptions, France and the Nordic countries, have stopped being exceptions. The ones that threw the most money at the problem, Korea and Hungary, are not at the top of the list. And the one rich country that sits comfortably above replacement did not get there because of a program.

[BUILD]

That shape is the beginning of wisdom on this subject. If money alone could do it, the countries that spent the most would have the most children. They do not. If modernity alone made it impossible, no modern country would be above replacement. One is.

So the answer, whatever it is, has to be more interesting than money, and more hopeful than fate.

[PAUSE]

Why should any of this matter to someone who is not a demographer? Let me give you three reasons, briefly, because the earlier speeches in this series made the case at length.

The first is the ledger. Every pension system, every public health system, every promise a government makes to its older citizens is paid for, in the end, by the work of younger ones. When each generation is smaller than the last, fewer workers carry more retirees, and every one of those promises grows heavier. In the United States last year, births still outnumbered deaths by roughly half a million. The Wall Street Journal, reporting the new figures, noted that this surplus is projected to disappear within the next decade.

The second is speed. These trends have repeatedly moved faster than the experts expected. In Japan, the decline in births is running about fifteen years ahead of the official projections made by the government's own population institute. Countries that thought they had decades to prepare are discovering they have years.

The third reason is the one I care about most, and it is not economic at all. A society with fewer children is a society with fewer of the things that children bring. Fewer playgrounds full of noise. Fewer school plays and Little League games and first days of school. Fewer young people with new ideas and the energy to try them. A country is not only an economy. It is a family of families, and when the families shrink, something in the spirit of the place shrinks with them.

Before we go country by country, one more thing needs to be said about these numbers, because it is the thing most often left out. A low fertility rate is not a disease and it is not a crime. It is the sum of millions of private decisions, made by free people, for reasons that are their own. Nobody in this room owes the state a child. Nobody should be made to feel that their worth depends on how many children they have, or whether they have any at all.

What I want to ask is not whether people are doing their duty. It is whether people are getting what they want. And on that question the evidence is striking.

## III. The Families We Say We Want

In the summer of twenty twenty-three, Gallup asked Americans a question it has been asking since the nineteen thirties. What do you think is the ideal number of children for a family to have?

Forty-five percent said three or more.

That was the highest share since nineteen seventy-one. The average ideal came to about two point seven children. And here is the detail that should stop every pessimist in their tracks. The age group most likely to say three or more was the youngest, adults between eighteen and twenty-nine.

[PAUSE]

Think about that. The generation that is having the fewest children is the generation that says, more than any other, that a big family is the ideal.

The ideal is two point seven. The reality is one point five seven. That distance, more than one child per woman, is not a gap in values. It is a gap between what people hope for and what their lives allow.

Now, you can object that an ideal is not an intention. People say many things to pollsters. Ask me my ideal number of hours of sleep and I will tell you nine, and you can check my phone and see that I get six. That is a fair objection, and I take it seriously.

So let us look at a harder measure.

In twenty twenty-five, the United Nations Population Fund published its annual State of World Population report. For the first time it commissioned a survey, with the polling firm YouGov, of about fourteen thousand adults in fourteen countries, countries that together hold more than a third of humanity. It asked people not about ideals but about expectations.

Almost eighteen percent of people of reproductive age said they expected not to reach the family size they wanted. And most of those, about eleven percent of everyone surveyed, expected to have fewer children than they wanted, not more. Nearly a quarter said that at some point they had wanted a child but had not been able to have one when they wanted.

The UN did not call its report a crisis of too few babies. It called it "the real fertility crisis," and it defined that crisis as the inability of people to have the families they want. It listed the reasons people gave. Economic instability. Lack of support from partners and communities. Poor health care. Lack of access to childcare.

[SLOW]

I want to plant a flag here, because the whole rest of this speech stands on it.

There are two ways to talk about falling birth rates. The first way says: the nation needs more babies, so citizens must produce them. That way has a long and ugly history, and I reject it entirely.

The second way says: millions of people want children, or want more children, and something in the way we have arranged our economy and our culture is stopping them. That is not a demand on the citizen. It is a demand on the country. It says the nation owes its young people a life in which the family they hope for is within reach.

The first way treats people as means. The second way treats them as ends. The first way says you owe us a child. The second way says we owe you a chance.

[LET IT LAND]

Everything I will propose tonight comes from the second way.

And notice that the second way has a built-in limit, which protects us from the abuses of the first. If the goal is to close the gap between the families people want and the families they have, then the goal is never to push anyone past what they want. A woman who wants no children is not a policy failure. A couple who want one child and have one child are a policy success. The measure is not the total. The measure is the gap.

But here is the hard part, and the reason the rest of this speech is necessary. Closing the gap turns out to be very difficult. Countries have tried, and the trying has taught us a great deal, much of it humbling.

So let us go and look at the people who tried. And let us start with the country that has been trying the longest.

## IV. France: The Long Patience

To understand France, you have to understand fear.

In eighteen seventy, France was defeated by Prussia. In the decades that followed, French writers and politicians looked across the Rhine at a German population that was growing faster than their own, and they drew a conclusion that would shape French policy for a century. A nation with too few children, they believed, was a nation that would lose the next war.

That fear produced a movement. And the movement produced a man named Adolphe Landry, an economist and deputy who spent much of his life arguing that a republic had a duty to support the families that sustained it.

[PAUSE]

In March of nineteen thirty-two, a law bearing Landry's name extended family allowances to all employees in industry and commerce who had at least two children, paid for by compulsory employer funds. One French historian calls it the first action of the state in the protection of the family.

In February of nineteen thirty-nine, with war again on the horizon, France created a High Committee on Population, with Landry among its five members. Its report became, by decree in July of that year, the Code de la famille, the Family Code. It extended allowances to all working French people regardless of income, and it raised them steeply from the third child onward.

Then came the war, and the occupation, and the liberation. And at the end of December nineteen forty-five, France passed the law that created the quotient familial, the family quotient, a way of calculating income tax that divides a household's income by the number of people it must feed. More children, lower taxes. Not as a gift, but as recognition that a family of five living on one salary is not as rich as a single man living on the same salary.

The next year, nineteen forty-six, a law fixed the family benefit system in the shape it would keep for generations.

[SLOW]

Now pause and notice what kind of policy this was. It was not a bonus. It was not a campaign. It was not a slogan launched by one government and abandoned by the next. It was a set of institutions, built by the Third Republic, rebuilt by the Fourth, kept by the Fifth, defended by the left and the right alike. It survived wars, devaluations, and a dozen changes of constitution and party.

And it lasted long enough to become part of the furniture of French life. Generations of French parents grew up assuming that the state would help them raise children. That the school day would be long and the crèche available.

And the state kept building. By the middle of the twenty-tens, almost every three-year-old in France, about ninety-seven and a half percent, was already enrolled in the free public école maternelle, the nursery school, years before a law in twenty nineteen made instruction compulsory from the age of three. France has spent on family benefits, in cash, services, and tax breaks together, something on the order of three to three and a half percent of its economy, well above the average of the rich democracies. For a French parent, the first years of a child's life have long come with a public partner. That a third child would be met with a larger allowance and a lighter tax bill, not a raised eyebrow.

What happened? For most of the late twentieth century, France had one of the highest fertility rates in Europe. In two thousand ten, French fertility stood at about two children per woman, the highest it had been since the end of the baby boom, at a time when the European average was closer to one point six.

[PAUSE]

This is the first lesson of the evening, and it is the lesson of patience. Family policy works best not as a shock but as a climate. When people believe that support will be there not just this year but for the whole length of a childhood, they plan differently. The French did not have more children because of any single check. They had more children because their whole society had, for seventy years, told them that children were a public good and that the public would share the cost.

But I promised you honesty, so here is the rest of the French story.

In twenty twenty-five, French fertility fell to one point five six. Births have fallen by about a quarter since twenty ten. The great exception has stopped being exceptional.

Why? The French are still arguing about it. Some point to cuts and caps on family benefits in the twenty-tens, which reduced the generosity of the system for middle-class families. Some point to housing in the big cities. Some point to the same forces that are lowering birth rates everywhere else, later partnerships, longer education, economic anxiety, and a culture in which children have become one choice among many rather than the default shape of adult life.

In January of twenty twenty-four, President Macron called for what he named a "demographic rearmament." He promised a new, better-paid birth leave and a national plan against infertility. The phrase was widely mocked. Critics on the left called it reactionary. The reform itself was delayed and reshaped, and a version of the new birth leave was added to the twenty twenty-six social security budget.

[SLOW]

So France teaches two things at once.

It teaches that patient, generous, cross-party family policy can hold a nation's fertility near replacement for decades. That is not nothing. That is an achievement that very few countries can claim.

And it teaches that even the best policy is not a fortress. When the culture shifts, when housing tightens, when benefits are trimmed and the young begin to doubt the future, the numbers move. Policy can raise the floor. It cannot by itself hold back the tide.

[LET IT LAND]

Hold on to that. Policy can raise the floor. Remember it when we get to Hungary, because Hungary tried to do in ten years what France did in seventy.

## V. Hungary: The Bold Bet

If France is the story of patience, Hungary is the story of ambition.

When Viktor Orban's government came to power in twenty ten, Hungarian fertility stood at about one point two five, among the lowest in Europe. The government declared that it would answer the demographic problem not with immigration but with families. And over the following decade and a half it built what may be the most elaborate pronatal program in the democratic world.

Let me describe some of it.

[PAUSE]

There was a housing subsidy known by its Hungarian initials, CSOK, that gave families money and subsidized loans to buy or build a home, with the amounts rising with each child born or promised. Under the current version, according to the program terms published by Hungary's largest bank, a family committing to three or more children could borrow up to fifty million forints at a subsidized rate.

There was the baby expecting loan, introduced in twenty nineteen. A young married couple could borrow a large sum, at the time about thirty thousand dollars. Have a child, and repayments were suspended. Have a second, and part of the loan was forgiven. Have a third, and the whole loan was written off.

There were tax exemptions. Since twenty twenty, mothers of four or more children have paid no personal income tax for life. In twenty twenty-five, Parliament voted to extend that exemption, step by step, to mothers of three, and then to mothers of two under the age of forty, and then, from twenty twenty-nine, to mothers of two of any age.

There were grants for families of three or more children to buy a new car with at least seven seats. A childcare allowance for grandparents who stay home to look after grandchildren so the parents can work. A promise of twenty-one thousand new nursery places. And a government that talked about families constantly, on billboards and in speeches, as a matter of national survival.

The cost was enormous. Estimates vary, but a figure of around five percent of gross domestic product on family support is commonly cited, by supporters and critics alike. For comparison, that is more than many countries spend on their armies.

[SLOW]

So what happened?

For a while, it looked like it was working. Fertility rose from about one point two five in twenty ten to about one point six one in twenty twenty-one. Marriages rose sharply. The government reported that between twenty ten and twenty nineteen the number of marriages rose by more than forty percent. Hungary went from one of the least marrying countries in Europe to one of the most. Supporters around the world held it up as proof that pronatal policy could work.

Then the line turned. Fertility fell to about one point three nine in twenty twenty-four, and to about one point three one in twenty twenty-five, according to preliminary figures. About seventy-two thousand babies were born last year, the fewest since nineteen forty-nine. A critical analysis by the Cato Institute pointed out that Hungarian fertility was now essentially back where it had been before the program began.

[PAUSE]

And in April of this year, Hungarian voters ended Viktor Orban's sixteen years in power. The opposition Tisza party, led by Peter Magyar, won a landslide and a constitutional majority. The election was about many things, about corruption and the rule of law, about Europe and Russia and the economy. It was not a referendum on family policy. But a new government now inherits the most expensive pronatal program in Europe and a birth rate back near its starting point.

Now, how should we read this? There are two easy readings, and I think both are wrong.

The first easy reading says: Hungary proves that pronatal policy is a waste of money. Look, they spent five percent of their economy and ended up where they started.

The second easy reading says: Hungary proves that pronatal policy works, and the recent decline is due to inflation, the war next door, and the pandemic, not to any flaw in the program.

[BUILD]

Here is what I think the evidence actually supports.

First, money moves timing. When you tell couples that a loan will be forgiven if they have a child within a certain number of years, many of them have the child sooner. That raises the fertility rate for a few years. But if they were going to have that child anyway, later, the total number of children each woman has over her lifetime may barely change. Demographers call this a tempo effect. A good share of Hungary's rise looks like it.

Second, money moves marriage. Hungary's incentives were tied mostly to married couples, and marriage rose. That is a real result. Marriage is still, in most of the world, the setting where most children are born and raised, and a policy that encourages people to commit to each other earlier is doing something real.

Third, money cannot outrun a shock. When inflation in Hungary passed twenty-five percent in early twenty twenty-three, the highest in the European Union, the value of fixed benefits eroded and young families felt poorer. Some of the decline is about that.

Fourth, and this is the lesson I most want to draw, a family policy that becomes a partisan banner is fragile. When a program is identified with one leader and one party, half the country hears it as propaganda. And when that leader falls, the program is up for grabs. Compare that to France, where the family code survived every change of government for three generations because no party owned it.

[LET IT LAND]

So Hungary teaches that bold money can move the timing of births and the rate of marriage, but that money alone, in a hurry, under one party's flag, does not durably change how many children a people chooses to have. The French built a climate. The Hungarians built a campaign. Climates last longer than campaigns.

And yet I do not want to sneer at Hungary. Hungary looked at a falling population and decided to fight for its future instead of shrugging. Whatever you think of its government, that impulse is not shameful. The question is not whether to care. The question is how to care in a way that lasts.

## VI. The Nordic Paradox

Now let us go north, to the countries that have done almost everything that progressive family-policy experts recommend.

Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Finland, and Iceland have long, well-paid parental leave, often with months reserved for fathers. They have universal, heavily subsidized childcare. They have generous child allowances, strong labor protections, high rates of women in the workforce, and a culture that treats fathers pushing strollers as perfectly ordinary.

For years, demographers pointed to these countries as the proof that modern societies could have both gender equality and healthy birth rates. The argument went like this. Fertility falls when women are forced to choose between a career and a family. Remove the choice, make work and motherhood compatible, and fertility will recover.

[PAUSE]

And for a time, that argument seemed to be right. Around two thousand ten, the Nordic countries had fertility rates between about one point eight and two point five, depending on the country and the territory, among the highest in the rich world.

Then something happened that nobody predicted. According to Nordic Statistics, fertility in the region fell by between a fifth and a third from those levels. Finland fell by about a third, to the lowest level in its two and a half centuries of records. Sweden and Norway fell by more than a quarter. Denmark by more than a fifth.

And this happened without any cut to the welfare state. The parental leave was still there. The daycare was still there. The child allowances were still there. The fathers were still pushing strollers.

[SLOW]

This is what I call the Nordic paradox. The countries that made children most compatible with modern life saw births fall anyway.

Researchers have looked hard at Finland in particular. A study by Hellstrand, Nisen, and Myrskyla, published in twenty twenty-two, used Finnish registers to ask a simple question. Was the decline in first births happening because fewer people were forming couples, or because the couples were having fewer children?

The answer was: mostly the second. About three quarters of the decline since twenty ten came from lower fertility within couples, and only about a quarter from changes in partnership. Finnish researchers have also shown that the decline is not just a matter of having children later. Women are having fewer children, period.

[PAUSE]

So Finnish couples, living in one of the safest, most equal, most supportive countries on earth, with free schools and paid leave and subsidized daycare, are choosing to have fewer children than their parents did.

What does that tell us?

It tells us that the cost of a child, as economists measure it, is not the whole story. The Nordic countries did more than anyone to reduce that cost, and births fell anyway. Something else is going on, something that has to do with how people imagine their lives, what they think will make them happy, how certain they feel about the future, and what place children hold in the picture of a good life.

[BUILD]

I want to be careful here, because the Nordic paradox is often used to make a lazy argument. The lazy argument says: see, policy does not matter, so we should do nothing. That does not follow.

Here is what follows. Policy that makes children compatible with work is necessary but not sufficient. It removes obstacles. It does not create desire. A country that makes it possible to have children while working is a better country, a fairer country, a country where women are not punished for motherhood. But it has not, by that fact alone, given anyone a reason to want a child.

And I want to add a word for my own country, because Americans sometimes hear the Nordic story and draw exactly the wrong conclusion. The lesson of the Nordic paradox is not that paid leave and affordable childcare are pointless. The lesson is that they are the floor, not the ceiling. The Nordic countries built the floor and discovered they still needed a house on top of it. The United States remains the only rich democracy without a national program of paid parental leave, and in most states the average price of a year of infant care is higher than a year of in-state tuition at a public university. We have not yet built the floor. We should not use the Nordic experience as an excuse to skip it. We should use it as a warning that the floor alone will not be enough.

And there is a hopeful footnote. In twenty twenty-five, Finnish fertility ticked up, from one point two five to one point three. Norway rose for the third year in a row, to one point four eight. Sweden's decline, according to its statistics office, appears to have stalled. None of this is a rescue. All of it is a reminder that these numbers are not a law of physics. They move. They can move up as well as down.

[LET IT LAND]

So the Nordic countries teach the third lesson of the evening. You can remove every obstacle and still find that people do not walk through the door. The door has to lead somewhere they want to go.

France taught us patience. Hungary taught us the limits of money in a hurry. The Nordics teach us the limits of compatibility. And now we go to the country that teaches us what happens when everything goes wrong at once.

## VII. Korea: The Lesson of Failure

South Korea is one of the great success stories of the twentieth century. A country that was among the poorest in the world after the war of the early nineteen fifties became, within two generations, one of the richest, most educated, most technologically advanced societies on earth. It builds the phones in our pockets and the ships on our oceans. Its music and films are loved around the world.

And for most of the past decade, it has had the lowest fertility rate of any country on earth.

[PAUSE]

In twenty twenty-three, the Korean fertility rate fell to zero point seven two. Since twenty eighteen, Korea has been the only member of the OECD with a rate below one.

It is not as though the Korean government ignored the problem. Since two thousand six, successive governments have poured money into it. Reuters, reporting in twenty twenty-four, put the figure at more than three hundred and sixty trillion won, around two hundred and seventy billion dollars. A Korea Times editorial in twenty twenty-two put it at three hundred and eighty trillion won and compared the effort to pouring water into a bottomless pit.

Cash for births. Childcare subsidies. Housing preferences for newlyweds. Expanded parental leave. Campaign after campaign. Plan after plan.

And for fifteen years, the number went down, and down, and down.

[SLOW]

Why? If money could buy babies, Korea would be the most fertile country in Asia. It is not even close. So what was Korea up against that money could not overcome?

I want to name four things, and each of them carries a lesson for us.

The first is the tournament. In Korea, the life of a child is widely understood as a competition, beginning early and ending, or so it feels, at the university entrance exam. To compete, families pay for private cram schools, hagwons, on top of public school. In twenty twenty-four, total spending on private education in Korea reached about twenty-nine point two trillion won, a record. In twenty twenty-five, about three quarters of all school students took part, and the average family of a participating student spent about six hundred and four thousand won a month, per child. For high school students, it was closer to eight hundred thousand.

Think about what that does to the decision to have a child. If every child is a contestant in a twenty-year tournament, and the entry fee is a substantial share of your income, then a second child is not a joy to be added. It is a second contestant, a second entry fee, and a split of every resource the first child needed to win. Parents in that system do the rational thing. They have one, and pour everything into it. Or they have none.

[PAUSE]

The second is work. Koreans work some of the longest hours in the developed world. In twenty twenty-two, the average Korean worker put in about nineteen hundred hours a year, the fourth highest in the OECD. Long hours leave little time for dating, less for marriage, and almost none for the slow, unproductive, irreplaceable hours that raising a child requires.

The third is the burden carried by women. Korea has had the largest gender pay gap in the OECD. In twenty twenty-two, women in Korea earned on average about thirty-one percent less than men, more than double the OECD average. And many Korean women describe a culture in which a wife who becomes a mother is expected to step back from her career while her husband does not. When motherhood means accepting a large and permanent penalty, many women decline it. That is not selfishness. That is arithmetic.

The fourth is housing. Prices in the Seoul area, where half the country lives, have long been among the hardest in the world to reach on a young person's salary, and in Korea a home has often been expected before marriage.

[BUILD]

Now put those four together. A child is a tournament entry. Time is scarce. Motherhood carries a heavy penalty. And a home is out of reach. Into that system, the government poured cash. One-time bonuses. Monthly allowances. Subsidies here and there.

It was like trying to fill a bathtub with the drain wide open. The money was real. The drain was bigger.

Here is the lesson of Korea, and I think it is the most important lesson in this whole speech. You cannot pay people to have children inside a way of life that makes children feel impossible. If the structure of life itself is hostile, if the schooling, the working, the housing and the expectations placed on mothers all push the same way, then no check is large enough. You have to change the structure.

[PAUSE]

But there is a second chapter to the Korean story, and it is more hopeful.

In twenty twenty-four, Korean fertility rose for the first time in nine years, to zero point seven five. In twenty twenty-five, it rose again, to about zero point eight. Births rose by about six point seven percent, to about two hundred and fifty-four thousand. The rebound outpaced the government's own optimistic projections.

What drove it? Officials pointed first to marriage. Marriages in Korea jumped by a record fourteen point eight percent in twenty twenty-four and by another eight point one percent in twenty twenty-five. Part of that was a delayed wave of weddings postponed during the pandemic. Part of it was a larger cohort of women in their early thirties, born during a small echo boom in the early nineteen nineties.

Nobody serious believes Korea's problem is solved. Zero point eight is still barely more than a third of replacement. But the rebound teaches something worth remembering. In societies where nearly all children are born to married couples, the road to more children runs through more marriages. And marriages are about hope, about two people deciding that the future is worth sharing.

[LET IT LAND]

So Korea teaches us two things. That money poured into a hostile structure disappears. And that when young people begin to commit to each other again, children follow. Both lessons point the same way. The real work is not in the cash. It is in the shape of a life.

## VIII. Israel: The Exception

And now we come to the country that breaks the pattern.

Israel is rich. Its income per person is comparable to that of many Western European countries. It is highly educated, with one of the highest shares of university graduates in the world. It is a technological powerhouse, with more startups per person than almost anywhere. Its women work outside the home at high rates. By every measure that demographers use to predict low fertility, Israel should look like Germany or Italy.

Instead, in twenty twenty-four, Israel's total fertility rate was about two point nine. Nearly three children per woman. Not just above replacement but well above it. No other rich country comes close.

[PAUSE]

The first thing people say about Israel is that its high fertility is all about the ultra-Orthodox, the Haredim. And it is true that Haredi families are very large. Haredi women have, on recent figures, about six and a half children each. But the Haredim are only part of the story.

Religious Jewish women who are not Haredi have had, on recent figures, close to four children. And here is the number that should get our attention. Secular Jewish women in Israel, women who describe themselves as not religious at all, have had about two children each. Two. In Europe, secular women in rich, educated countries have one point three, one point four, one point five. In Israel, they have about two.

Something about Israeli life produces more children even among those who do not pray.

[SLOW]

What is it?

Part of it is policy. Israel offers some of the most generous state-funded fertility treatment in the world. The public health basket covers in vitro fertilization for women up to the age of forty-five until they have two children with their current partner, and Israel performs more IVF cycles relative to its population than any other country. That is a society saying, through its institutions, that if you want a child and nature is making it hard, the nation will help you.

But Israeli family benefits are not, by European standards, especially lavish. Housing in Tel Aviv is notoriously expensive. Childcare is not cheap. Israelis serve in the military. Life there is not easy. If fertility were purely a function of cost, Israel would not be the exception.

[PAUSE]

So I want to offer an interpretation, and I want to be honest that it is an interpretation, not a proven fact.

I think Israel has high fertility because Israelis, across religious and secular lines, believe that their people has a future, and believe that the future depends on them. A story is told in Israel, among the religious and the secular alike, of a people who were nearly destroyed in living memory and who answered by building a country and raising children in it. Having children is woven into the national story as an act of continuity, almost of defiance. Family is at the center of social life. Grandparents are close by and expected to help. A third child is ordinary. A fourth is not strange.

In other words, Israeli culture treats children not as a lifestyle option but as the natural shape of a hopeful adult life. And that culture is carried by institutions, by holidays and Sabbath dinners, by neighborhoods full of strollers, by a society in which the question at a dinner party is not whether you will have children but when.

[BUILD]

Now, I need to say three things clearly, so that no one mistakes what I am arguing.

First, I am not holding up war, or the threat of war, as a model. Nobody should want to live under that kind of pressure, and no one should want their country to have Israel's enemies in order to have Israel's birth rate.

Second, Israel is not immune to the trends we have been discussing. A December twenty twenty-five report from the Taub Center found that Israel's population growth had fallen below one percent for the first time since the founding of the state, driven partly by people leaving the country and partly by a rise in deaths. Secular fertility has been edging down for several years, and the Taub Center projects that secular Jewish women may reach about one point seven by twenty thirty. And yet the story keeps surprising. According to Israel's Central Bureau of Statistics, Jewish births in the first half of this year were about two point six percent higher than in the same months of twenty twenty-five, at a time when births across most of the rich world were still falling.

Third, Israel's high fertility raises real questions of its own, about how communities with very large families will support themselves and share the burdens of the state. Those are hard questions, and Israelis argue about them fiercely.

[LET IT LAND]

But with all that said, Israel proves something that the rest of the rich world badly needs to see. It proves that wealth, education, technology, and women's work are not, by themselves, a sentence to low fertility. A modern society can have children. What it needs, it seems, is a reason, a story in which children belong, and a community that carries that story from one generation to the next.

France showed us patience. Hungary showed us the limits of money in a hurry. The Nordic countries showed us the limits of compatibility. Korea showed us that a hostile structure eats every subsidy. And Israel shows us that the deepest force of all is meaning.

## IX. What Money Can Do

I have said several times now that money is not enough. I want to spend some time on the other half of that sentence, because "not enough" does not mean "nothing." And there is a real danger that people hear the stories of Korea and Hungary and conclude that we should simply do nothing. That would be a terrible mistake.

So let me tell you what the careful research says money can do.

[PAUSE]

Begin in Quebec. From nineteen eighty-eight to nineteen ninety-seven, the Canadian province of Quebec paid families a cash allowance for each new baby, with the amount rising steeply for later children, up to about eight thousand Canadian dollars for a third or later child. The economist Kevin Milligan studied it carefully, comparing Quebec families with similar families elsewhere in Canada, before and after the program, and before and after it was cancelled.

He found that the program worked. As reported at the time, fertility rose by roughly twelve percent on average, and by as much as twenty-five percent among the families eligible for the largest payments. The effect was strongest exactly where the money was largest, at the third child.

That is a real result from a careful study. It tells us that families do respond to money, especially when the money is large and aimed at the decision to have another child.

[SLOW]

Now Germany. In two thousand seven, Germany introduced Elterngeld, a parental allowance that replaced a large share of a parent's lost income for a year after a birth. The research suggests that it did not much change how many second children were born across the whole population. What it did, especially, was encourage highly educated women in their mid-thirties to have the children they had postponed. In other words, it helped with timing, and it helped a group that had been especially likely to delay.

Now Poland. In twenty sixteen, Poland introduced a monthly payment of five hundred zloty per child, later raised to eight hundred. It was one of the largest child benefit programs in Europe. Births rose briefly at first. Then fertility fell, to about one point one by twenty twenty-four. The Polish government itself admitted in twenty twenty that the program had not raised fertility over the long term. But, to be fair, it did substantially reduce child poverty. And as one Polish demographer has pointed out, we do not know how much lower fertility might have fallen without it.

[PAUSE]

And now the United States. Last year Congress passed the law known as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. Among many other things, it raised the maximum child tax credit from two thousand dollars to two thousand two hundred dollars per child, indexed to inflation, though the increase is only partly refundable, so many lower-income families do not get the full amount. It also created what are called Trump Accounts, savings accounts for children, with a one-thousand-dollar deposit from the Treasury for American citizens born from twenty twenty-five through twenty twenty-eight.

Critics, including analysts at Brookings, argue that the law's family provisions mostly help families that are already better off. Supporters argue that it is a first step toward treating children as an investment in the nation's future. Both can be partly right. But measured against the scale of what families face, these are modest sums.

Consider childcare. According to Child Care Aware of America, the average price of childcare in this country in twenty twenty-four was about thirteen thousand, one hundred and twenty-eight dollars a year. That is about ten percent of the median income of a married couple with children, and about thirty-five percent of a single parent's. A tax credit of two thousand two hundred dollars does not cover two months of that.

[BUILD]

So here is what the evidence says money can do. Let me put it plainly.

Money can change timing. It can help people have children sooner, which matters, because every year of delay is a year in which some people lose the chance altogether.

Money can help at the margin, and the margin that matters most is the third child. In Quebec, the effect was concentrated there. In France, the family code was built around it. When a family that already has two is deciding whether to have a third, money and space and help can tip the balance.

Money can reduce suffering. Child benefits reduce child poverty. That is worth doing for its own sake, whatever it does to the birth rate.

And money can send a signal, which may be the most important thing of all. When a society spends real resources on families, consistently, over decades, it tells its young people that children are valued, that they will not be alone, that the nation shares the burden.

[PAUSE]

Here is what money cannot do. It cannot, in a short period and by itself, persuade people who do not want children to want them. It cannot overcome a structure of life that makes each child a crushing burden. And it cannot substitute for a culture that has lost the sense that children are a good.

[LET IT LAND]

So the honest position is not that money does nothing, and not that money does everything. It is that money is a necessary tool that works best when it is generous, aimed at real decisions, kept for the long term, and joined to changes in how we live. A country that refuses to spend anything on families is not being prudent. It is telling its young people that their children are their own private problem. And they are hearing it.

## X. A Roof Before a Cradle

There is an old instinct, older than any government, that says you build the nest before you lay the eggs. Birds know it. Our grandparents knew it. Before you have children, you want a place to put them.

And that is exactly where the modern economy has placed one of its heaviest obstacles.

[PAUSE]

In November of twenty twenty-five, the National Association of Realtors published its annual profile of American home buyers. Two numbers in that report deserve to be read aloud in every legislature in the country.

The first is twenty-one percent. That was the share of homes bought by first-time buyers, the lowest ever recorded. The association noted that the share of first-time buyers has fallen by half since two thousand seven.

The second is forty. That was the median age of a first-time home buyer in America. Forty years old. In nineteen ninety-one, it was twenty-eight. Fifteen years ago, it was thirty. Five years ago, thirty-three. Last year, thirty-eight. Now forty.

[SLOW]

Think about what that means for a family. If the typical person does not buy a first home until forty, and if many people feel, as many do, that they want a stable home before they have children, then the window for having children and having them in a home of your own has narrowed to almost nothing.

When the association's deputy chief economist was asked what was holding first-time buyers back, she pointed not only to home prices but to rent, student loans, credit card debt, car loans, and childcare costs. Every one of those makes saving harder. And every year spent saving is a year in which a couple might have started a family.

[PAUSE]

Is there evidence that housing really affects births, or is this just a story that sounds plausible? There is evidence.

The economists Lisa Dettling and Melissa Kearney studied what happens to births when house prices change. Their study, published in the Journal of Public Economics in twenty fourteen, found something elegant and revealing. When house prices rise, people who already own homes have more children. People who do not own homes have fewer.

Their estimates implied that a ten-thousand-dollar increase in house prices led to about a five percent increase in births among owners and about a two point four percent decrease among non-owners.

Why would that be? For owners, a rising house price is extra wealth, which makes a child feel more affordable. For renters, a rising house price is a door closing, the price of the space a child needs moving further away.

[BUILD]

Now look at what has happened over the last fifteen years. Home prices have risen enormously. Who benefits? Older people who already own. Who loses? Younger people who do not. And which of those two groups is of childbearing age?

We have built an economy in which the reward for rising house prices goes to people whose families are complete, and the cost falls on the people who are trying to start them. Without anyone intending it, we have arranged our housing market as a machine for transferring wealth away from the young, at exactly the age when they would otherwise be having children.

This is not only an American problem. In Korea, as we saw, housing in the capital region has long been a barrier. In France, demographers point to the cost of housing in Paris and other big cities. Across the rich world, the same story repeats. The cities where the jobs are have become the places where families cannot afford to live.

[PAUSE]

So what would a country that wanted a future do about housing?

It would build. That is the first answer and the most important one. Prices are high, above all, because we have not built enough homes where people want to live. In much of the developed world, rules written decades ago make it illegal or prohibitively slow to build anything but a detached single-family house on most residential land. Those rules were written for a different time. A country that wants families would make it legal again to build the townhouse, the duplex, the small apartment building, the home with a garden apartment for a grandparent, near where the jobs are.

It would build for families specifically. Not only studio apartments and one-bedrooms, which are what the market often produces in expensive cities, but homes with two and three and four bedrooms, at prices young parents can reach.

It would help young families buy. There are many ways to do this, and countries have tried them. Hungary tied housing help to the number of children. France has long given families with more children more help with housing costs. One can argue about the design. But the principle is sound. If a home comes before a cradle, then helping young families into homes is helping them toward children.

[SLOW]

And it would remember that the point of housing policy is not to keep prices rising forever for those who already own. A home is a place to raise a family before it is an investment. Every political system must find a way to tell that truth to the voters who own homes, who are many, and who vote. It is a hard truth. But a nation that cannot tell it will find that it has made the homes of the old more valuable by making the families of the young impossible.

[LET IT LAND]

A roof before a cradle. Our grandparents had a chance at both before thirty. Our children deserve the same chance.

## XI. Culture and Meaning

Now we come to the part of the subject that policy people find most uncomfortable to talk about, and that I think matters most. Culture. Meaning. The stories a people tells itself about what a good life is.

Remember the Nordic paradox. The obstacles were removed, and people did not walk through the door. Remember Israel. The costs were high, and people walked through anyway. The difference between those two cases is not, primarily, a difference in policy. It is a difference in meaning.

[PAUSE]

Let me start with one of the most consistent findings in the study of fertility. In almost every country where it has been measured, religious people have more children than secular people.

In the United States, the Institute for Family Studies has analyzed decades of data from the federal government's National Survey of Family Growth. It found that women who attend religious services weekly have had fertility rates that rarely dropped much below two children per woman. Women who attend less often have had rates around one point seven and below. And women with no religion have had rates below one point five.

That is a large gap. It is the difference between a population that roughly holds its size and one that shrinks by a quarter or more each generation.

[SLOW]

Why would religion matter so much? I do not think it is mainly about any specific doctrine about contraception. Many religious people use contraception freely and still have larger families.

I think it is about three things.

The first is community. A congregation is a network of people who know each other, who bring meals when a baby is born, who watch each other's children, who make the burden of parenting a shared burden. A young mother in a strong congregation is not alone. A young mother in an anonymous apartment block often is.

The second is time. Most religions teach that the present is part of a longer story, one that stretches back through ancestors and forward through descendants. Inside a story like that, having children is not a disruption of your life plan. It is your part in the plan.

The third is hope. Religious traditions teach, in different ways, that the future is not to be feared, that life is a gift, and that bringing new life into the world is good. That belief makes the leap of having a child feel less like a gamble and more like an act of trust.

[PAUSE]

And here is a story that shows how powerful meaning can be. In the country of Georgia, at the edge of Europe and Asia, the birth rate had fallen after the collapse of the Soviet Union. In late two thousand seven, the head of the Georgian Orthodox Church, Patriarch Ilia the Second, made a pledge. He announced that he would personally baptize every third and later child born to married Orthodox parents.

It sounds like a small thing. It was not small to Georgians. The Patriarch was the most trusted figure in the country, and to be baptized by him was a great honor. In the years that followed he held great mass baptisms in the cathedral in Tbilisi, hundreds of infants at a time.

Births in Georgia rose from about forty-nine thousand in two thousand seven to about fifty-seven thousand in two thousand eight and about sixty-three thousand in two thousand nine. Other things were changing in Georgia at the same time, so we must be careful. But a study published in twenty twenty-five, using careful statistical methods, found suggestive evidence that the Patriarch's pledge did increase fertility, especially for third and later children. By one count, more than forty thousand such children were baptized.

[SLOW]

Ilia the Second died this past March, at the age of ninety-three, after nearly half a century leading his church. Whatever one thinks of his views on other matters, he understood something that governments with far more money did not. He understood that people will do for honor and meaning what they will not do for cash. He did not pay anyone. He simply told a nation, through a gesture of personal blessing, that its children were precious, and that a larger family was something to be celebrated.

[PAUSE]

Now, I am not asking anyone to become religious for the sake of the birth rate. Faith that is adopted as a policy tool is not faith at all. And secular people can and do build lives full of meaning, community, and children.

But I am asking us to notice what religion provides and to ask whether a secular society can provide some of it too. Community that shares the weight of raising children. A sense of time that runs longer than a single lifetime. And hope, the belief that the future is good enough to bring a child into.

[BUILD]

And here I want to say something about the culture we actually have, because it is not neutral. It pushes.

Our culture tells young people, in a thousand messages a day, that the purpose of life is the self. Self-expression. Self-optimization. Self-care. Experiences to be collected, places to be seen, a career to be climbed, a body to be perfected, and all of it displayed. Children appear in that culture mostly as a cost, a constraint, an item on a list of things that might, someday, be fitted in once everything else is arranged.

And so much of that culture now lives on a screen. The thinker whose metaphysics has run through this series of speeches has a sharp way of describing the feed. He calls it a counterfeit. It offers the feeling of reaching toward knowledge and connection, while quietly consuming the things that actually sustain a life, your hours, your attention, your sleep, your health. He calls it knowledge without sustainment. He also describes it as a local minimum, a place that feels like progress from the inside but is not where we are meant to end up.

You do not have to accept his whole philosophy to see the point. Hours spent watching other people's lives through glass are hours not spent building your own. A generation that dates less, marries later, and spends more time alone with a phone is not a generation that is likely to have the families it says it wants.

[PAUSE]

There is one more part of culture I want to name, because three of the countries we visited tonight pointed to it from different directions. Marriage.

In Hungary, the most durable result of the whole program was not the birth rate. It was the marriage rate. In Korea, the first real rebound in a decade came on the back of a surge in weddings. In Finland, a quarter of the fall in first births came from fewer couples forming at all. And in the United States, the long decline in marriage, which an earlier speech in this series examined, is one of the clearest signals we have of what will happen to births a few years later.

I am not saying that only married people should have children, or that every child born outside marriage is a problem. Many wonderful families take other shapes. I am saying something simpler. Children are a long project. People undertake long projects when they have a partner they trust to stay. Anything that helps young people find each other, commit to each other, and stay together, whether it is a church social, a neighborhood that is not empty at night, a tax code that does not penalize marriage, or a culture that celebrates commitment rather than mocking it, is quietly doing more for the future than many a government program.

A people that wants a future has to want, first, for its young to find each other. That is where every family begins.

[PAUSE]

So a culture that wants a future must offer a better story. Not a story that shames people who do not have children. A story that honors people who do. A story in which a father leaving work early for a school play is admired, not quietly marked down. A story in which a mother of three is seen as having done something important, not as having given up on her ambitions. A story in which a young couple with a baby is welcomed in the restaurant, the office, the airplane, the church, and the public square.

[LET IT LAND]

Policy can make children possible. Only culture can make them wanted. And culture is not something the government makes. It is something we make, every day, in the way we speak about children, the way we treat parents, and the stories we choose to tell.

## XII. The Strongest Objection

Every argument worth making has to face the strongest case against it. Not a straw man. The real thing, stated as well as its best defenders would state it. So let me do that now.

[PAUSE]

The strongest objection comes in several parts, and I will take them one at a time.

The first part comes from two of the most respected economists who study this subject, Melissa Kearney and Phillip Levine. In twenty twenty-two they published a careful study of why American births have fallen since the Great Recession. They looked at a long list of suspects: economic conditions, housing costs, student debt, childcare prices, state policies, access to contraception. And they concluded that they were unable to identify any of those factors as explaining much of the decline.

What they suspected instead was something harder to measure. They suggested that the decline more likely reflects shifting priorities among recent generations of young adults, different hopes for how to spend their lives, different views of what parenthood would mean for their careers, their freedom, and their happiness. And they concluded that incremental policy responses are unlikely to reverse the trend.

That is a serious finding from serious people, and I want to state it plainly. If they are right, then a good deal of what I have said about housing and childcare and tax credits will not move the birth rate very much. People are not having fewer children mainly because they cannot afford them. They are having fewer children because they want different things.

[SLOW]

The second part of the objection is about freedom. It says: this is none of the government's business. Whether and when to have children is the most personal decision a human being makes. A state that tries to influence it, even with carrots rather than sticks, is overstepping. And history, the objection continues, shows what happens when states take an interest in the wombs of their citizens.

The objector has history on their side. In October of nineteen sixty-six, the Romanian dictator Nicolae Ceausescu issued Decree seven hundred and seventy, which banned abortion for most women. In the year that followed, the Romanian birth rate roughly doubled. Then it fell back, as women found other ways, many of them dangerous. Maternal deaths rose sharply. Orphanages filled. The decree was revoked only when the regime fell in nineteen eighty-nine. The very phrase pronatalism still carries, for many people, the memory of governments that treated women as instruments of national power.

The third part of the objection is about the planet. It says that there are already more than eight billion people on earth, that our consumption is straining the climate and the natural world, and that a gradual decline in human numbers is not a catastrophe but a relief.

[PAUSE]

These are real objections. I do not think any of them can be dismissed. But I do think each of them can be answered.

Let me start with Kearney and Levine, because their finding is the most important. They found that measurable economic factors do not explain most of the decline, and they pointed to changing priorities. I accept that. But I want to ask a question that their study, by its nature, could not answer. Where do priorities come from?

Priorities are not handed down from heaven, fully formed, to each young person on their eighteenth birthday. They are formed by the culture young people grow up in, the examples they see, the costs they observe their parents bearing, the stories they are told about what a good life is. A generation that watched its parents struggle with childcare costs and housing costs and the demands of work without support is a generation that will form priorities accordingly. A generation raised in a culture that treats children as a burden will come to see them as one.

[SLOW]

In other words, shifting priorities are not an alternative to the argument I have been making. They are part of it. If priorities have shifted, then the question is not whether we can change behavior without changing priorities. It is whether a society can, over time, change the conditions in which priorities form. And the answer to that, I think, is yes. France did it, over seventy years. Israel does it every day. Georgia did it, at least for a while, with a single gesture of honor.

And remember the Gallup finding. The youngest adults, the very generation whose priorities are supposed to have shifted, are the most likely to say that three or more children is ideal. Their priorities have not shifted away from children as much as their lives have shifted away from the conditions in which children seem possible. When the ideal is two point seven and the reality is one point five seven, that is not a story of people who do not want children. It is a story of people who want them and do not get there.

[PAUSE]

Now freedom. The objection from freedom is, I think, the most important of all, and I accept its core completely. No person owes a child to the state. No policy should ever coerce, pressure, shame, or punish anyone for their choices about having children. Romania is not a cautionary tale about pronatalism done badly. It is a cautionary tale about tyranny, and any pronatalism that could even begin to resemble it should be rejected without hesitation.

But notice that the freedom objection cuts both ways. If the eighteen percent of people in the UN survey who expect to have fewer children than they want are falling short because of housing they cannot afford, work that leaves no time, and costs they cannot carry, then they too are suffering a loss of freedom. Their freedom to have the family they want is being limited, not by a decree, but by the arrangement of the economy.

A free society should care about both kinds of freedom. The freedom not to have children, which must be absolute. And the freedom to have them, which right now, for millions of people, is not.

[BUILD]

That is why I have insisted throughout that the measure of success is the gap, not the total. A pronatalism of freedom does not ask anyone to do anything. It asks the country to remove the obstacles that stand between people and the families they want, and to build a culture in which those who choose children are honored, without shaming those who do not.

And now the planet. I take the environmental concern seriously. But I would point out three things. First, the population of the world is still growing, and will be for some decades, and the low-fertility countries we have discussed account for a modest share of that growth. Second, the challenge of climate change is primarily about how we produce energy and use resources, not about whether a young couple in Ohio or Lyon has a second child. And third, the nations that will invent and build the technologies to protect the planet need young scientists and engineers and workers. An aging, shrinking society is not a greener society. It is a poorer and more cautious one.

[PAUSE]

There is one more objection I owe you, because it is the one I hear most often from thoughtful people. Why worry, they ask, when there is immigration? If a rich country has too few young people, it can welcome young people from elsewhere. Many countries already do. And immigration has enriched this country in ways that are impossible to count.

I agree with much of that. Immigration is a real part of the answer for many countries, including our own, and nothing in this speech is an argument against it.

But it cannot be the whole answer, for a simple reason. The fall in fertility is not confined to rich countries. According to the United Nations' most recent world population projections, the global fertility rate has fallen from about three point one in nineteen ninety to about two point two five today, and more than half of the world's countries are now below replacement. The UN expects the world's population to peak at around ten point three billion in the mid twenty-eighties and then begin to decline. The countries that send young migrants today are, many of them, on the same path as the countries that receive them, only a few decades behind.

A strategy that depends forever on other nations' children is a strategy that runs out. And there is something else. A people that has stopped wanting children of its own, and relies on others to provide its future, has lost something that no immigration policy can restore, which is its own confidence that its way of life is worth passing on. The healthiest countries, I think, will be those that welcome newcomers and also raise children of their own, and invite the newcomers to join them in doing both.

[PAUSE]

And now I want to say something in the spirit of the man whose speech gives part of the shape to this one.

When Abraham Lincoln rose to give his second inaugural address, on the fourth of March, eighteen sixty-five, the Civil War was nearly won. He could have gloated. He could have claimed that God had been on the side of the Union all along. Instead, in a speech of about seven hundred words, he did something remarkable. He observed that both sides read the same Bible and prayed to the same God, and that each invoked God's aid against the other. He refused to pretend that his side owned Providence. He named the sin that had caused the war, and he did not spare the North its share of the blame. And then he turned, not to triumph, but to the work of healing.

I want to borrow that humility, because this subject badly needs it.

[SLOW]

Both sides of our politics have something to answer for here. The right has too often talked about birth rates in ways that sound like nostalgia for a world in which women had fewer choices, and has too often been unwilling to spend the money that families actually need. The left has too often treated any concern about falling birth rates as suspect, as if wanting children for one's country were itself a form of reaction, and has too often neglected the culture of meaning without which no amount of spending will be enough.

Neither side owns this cause. Neither side is innocent. Each has a piece of the truth. The right is correct that culture and meaning matter, that marriage and faith and community are powerful. The left is correct that money matters, that childcare and housing and the treatment of mothers at work are real obstacles, that women must never be treated as means.

[LET IT LAND]

The children who are not being born do not belong to a party. The future does not vote in our primaries. If we are serious, we will stop using this subject to win arguments with each other and start using it to build something together.

## XIII. An Act of Faith

I have spent most of this speech on evidence. Now I want to speak about something the evidence points toward but cannot capture. I want to speak about faith. Not necessarily religious faith, though for many people it is that. Faith in the future.

[PAUSE]

Think about what it actually means to decide to have a child.

It means committing the next eighteen years of your life, at least, to a person you have not met. It means accepting a cost you cannot fully calculate and a love you cannot yet imagine. It means believing that the world your child will grow up in will be good enough to be worth growing up in. It means trusting that you will be able to provide, that your partner will stay, that your community will help, that your country will not fall apart.

None of that can be proven in advance. Every parent who has ever lived took a leap. And the leap is always, at bottom, a statement about the future. It says: I believe that tomorrow is worth entering, and I want someone I love to enter it after me.

[SLOW]

That is why I think the falling birth rate is, among other things, a measure of our collective faith in the future. When young people tell pollsters that they are afraid of climate change, of economic collapse, of political chaos, of a world remade by machines they do not understand, they are telling us something about why the leap feels so hard. A child is a bet on the future. A people that has lost faith in its future will place fewer bets.

Some of those fears are reasonable. The climate is changing. Economies do falter. New technologies are reshaping work faster than anyone predicted, and nobody knows exactly what jobs our children will do. I do not want to pretend that the future is guaranteed to be bright.

But I want to point out something about every generation that has ever had children. None of them knew what was coming. The parents of the nineteen thirties raised children through a depression and into a world war. The parents of the nineteen fifties raised children under the shadow of nuclear weapons, with schoolchildren practicing to hide under their desks. Parents in every century have brought children into a world of plague and war and uncertainty far beyond anything most of us face.

They did it not because they were sure the future would be good. They did it because they were determined to help make it good, and because they believed their children would help make it better still. The future is not a forecast that we receive. It is a house that we build, and children are not only its residents. They are its builders.

And here, lightly, I want to bring in the thinker whose metaphysics has run beneath this series of speeches like an underground river.

In his notes on what he calls the Esketian metaphysics, the author describes the universe as a kind of becoming, a process reaching toward a final state in which everything is known and existence is sustained without end. He calls that final state God. And he says that we tend to experience as good those activities that sustain our existence through time, from breathing and eating, up through more and more lasting goods.

Among those goods he lists the raising of children. And he adds a parenthesis that I have not been able to stop thinking about. We see, he writes, "the immortality of God through the eyes of our children."

[PAUSE]

I want to be careful here, as he is careful. This is one thinker's metaphysics, and he offers parts of it as an image rather than a proof, and holds some of it loosely. You do not have to believe that the universe is becoming God to feel the force of what he is pointing at.

Because every parent knows the experience he is describing. You look into the face of your child and you see something that will outlast you. Not you, exactly. Something carried forward. Your mother's eyes, your father's laugh, the language you speak, the songs you sing, the stories you tell, the things you believe are worth believing. In that face, the future stops being an abstraction. It becomes a person you love.

That is why I say that wanting children is an act of faith. It is the most ordinary way that human beings have of reaching toward something that lasts. Philosophers write books. Builders raise cathedrals. Scientists chase truths that will be understood long after they are gone. And mothers and fathers, in their billions, across all of human history, have done the same thing in the most intimate way possible, by bringing into the world someone who will carry it on.

[BUILD]

But faith is not only a feeling. Faith without works, an old book says, is dead. If we believe the future is worth entering, then we must build a country in which that belief is reasonable. So let me say, as plainly as I can, what a people that wants a future would do.

It would build homes. It would make it legal again to build the townhouse and the duplex and the family apartment near where the jobs are. It would build homes with room for children, at prices young couples can reach before they are forty.

It would pay for the work of raising children, not as charity but as recognition. A generous child allowance, paid every month, with more for the third child than the first, as France and Quebec have shown makes sense. A tax code that treats a parent raising three children on one salary as poorer than a single person on the same salary, because they are.

It would make childcare affordable and varied, so that parents who want to work can do so without spending a third of their income, and parents who want to stay home are not penalized for that choice either. Support should follow the child, not the parent's particular arrangement.

It would make it easier to start young. It would ask why we require so many years of schooling and so many credentials before a young person can earn a family wage. It would make it possible to finish an education with a child, to start a career with a child, to take a few years off and come back without being punished.

[PAUSE]

It would help with infertility, as Israel does, because some of the gap between the children people want and the children they have is a matter of biology, and the longer people wait, the larger that part becomes. No one who wants a child should be kept from one merely because treatment is too expensive.

It would change the shape of work. It would reward employers who let parents leave on time, who offer real flexibility, who do not quietly mark down the mother who leaves for the doctor's appointment or the father who takes his leave. Korea shows what happens when work consumes everything. We should learn before we get there.

It would keep its promises. It would build its family policy to last, across parties and across decades, so that a couple deciding today whether to have a child can trust that the support will still be there when that child starts school, and when that child turns twelve. France shows that patience pays. Hungary shows that a program tied to one party is fragile.

And it would tell a better story. Through its leaders and its schools and its media and its churches and its neighborhoods, it would say to young people: we are glad you are here, and we will be glad when your children are here. Your family is not your private problem. It is our common future.

[PAUSE]

But not all of this belongs to governments. Much of it belongs to us, and I want to be concrete about that too, because a call to action that only asks politicians to act is not much of a call.

If you run a business, you can decide that the parents who work for you will not be quietly punished for being parents. You can schedule the important meeting at ten in the morning instead of six in the evening. You can make it normal, not heroic, for a father to take the leave he is entitled to.

If you lead a congregation or a club or a civic group, you can make it a place where young families are welcomed, where the crying baby in the back is a sign of life and not an annoyance, where older members adopt younger families and bring the casserole and take the toddler for an afternoon.

If you are a friend of a new parent, you can show up. Not with advice. With dinner. With an hour of holding the baby so a mother can sleep. The single most common thing new parents describe is loneliness. A society that wants a future cures that loneliness one visit at a time.

And if you are a neighbor, you can vote for the apartment building down the street, the one that will bring young families into your neighborhood, even if it changes the view. Especially if it changes the view. A neighborhood that will not let families in is a neighborhood that has chosen to grow old alone.

[SLOW]

None of this is cheap. Some of it will be resisted by people who benefit from the way things are. Some of it may not work as well as we hope. All of it will take longer than a single election cycle.

But think about what we spend our money on as a society. Think about what we have been willing to borrow for, and build for, and fight for. Is there any investment more fundamental than the next generation itself? Every road we build, every institution we defend, every idea we cherish, will be handed to someone. If there is no one to hand it to, then everything we have built is a monument to people who are no longer here.

[LET IT LAND]

A people that wants a future builds homes for families. A people that wants a future pays for the raising of children. A people that wants a future makes room for parents at work and honor for parents in public. A people that wants a future keeps its promises across generations. And a people that wants a future believes, and teaches its children to believe, that tomorrow is worth entering.

## XIV. Adieu and Charge

I began with a farewell in a courtyard, and I want to return there now.

The man on the staircase at Fontainebleau was saying goodbye to an empire. He had lost. He knew it. And yet the words that have survived from that morning are not words of despair. They are words of charge. In substance he told them: keep serving France, do not grieve for me, and I will set down in writing what we did together. He turned his farewell into a commission for the people who would remain.

[PAUSE]

I said at the beginning that we too are saying goodbye, to an old assumption, the assumption that the future would be fuller than the present. And I want to end by doing what the farewell at Fontainebleau did. I want to turn the goodbye into a charge.

So let me speak directly, for a few minutes, to the people in this room, and to the people beyond it.

To the young, who are deciding now, or will decide soon, what shape their lives will take. I will not tell you what to do with your life. It is yours. But I will tell you this. If you want children, and so many of you say that you do, then do not let anyone persuade you that wanting them is foolish, or old-fashioned, or selfish, or naive. The wish to bring new life into the world is one of the oldest and best things in the human heart. Do not let it be crowded out by fear, or by a feed, or by a voice that tells you to wait until everything is perfect. Nothing is ever perfect. Every family in history began in an imperfect world.

[SLOW]

To the parents, who are in the thick of it, tired, stretched, wondering how you will pay for it all. You are doing the most important work there is. The country does not say that to you often enough. Let me say it. Every bedtime story, every packed lunch, every sleepless night, every argument about homework, is a brick in the future. You are not falling behind. You are building the thing that everything else depends on.

To the grandparents, who have seen the long arc of a life and know what lasts. Your children and grandchildren need you more than they may say. Live near them if you can. Help with the babies. Tell the stories. And use your votes, which are many, to make it possible for the young to have what you had, a home of their own and a family before they are forty.

[PAUSE]

To those who have wanted children and could not have them, or who have lost children, or who are still waiting. Nothing in this speech is a judgment on you. Your grief is real, and a society that wants a future should do far more to help you, through medicine, through adoption, through the simple kindness of noticing.

To those who have chosen not to have children. You are not the problem, and you never will be. You are aunts and uncles and teachers and mentors and neighbors, and a society needs you in its children's lives. The future belongs to you too, and you can help to raise it.

And to those who hold power, in legislatures and boardrooms and city councils and newsrooms and pulpits. You have the tools to change the conditions in which millions of young people decide whether to have a family. Build the homes. Fund the families. Change the workplace. Keep the promises. Tell the better story. Do it together, across the aisle, because no party owns the future and every party will be judged by it.

[BUILD]

Now I want to return, one last time, to the courtyard.

The emperor could not embrace every soldier. There were too many of them, and he was only one man. So he embraced one, the general, in the name of all. And he took the eagle, the symbol of everything they had carried together, and he held it.

I cannot embrace the children who are not yet born. None of us can. They do not exist yet. They are only a possibility, a future that may or may not come. They have no names, no faces, no voices. They cannot lobby. They cannot vote. They cannot speak for themselves.

But each of us can do what the emperor did. Each of us can hold one, in the name of all.

[SLOW]

If you are a parent, go home tonight and hold your child, and know that in holding that one child you are holding the future of your people.

If you are not yet a parent, hold the hope of one, and refuse to let it go merely because the world has made it hard.

If you are past that time of life, hold a grandchild, or a neighbor's child, or a student, and give that one child what every child needs, the sense that the world is glad they are here.

And if you hold power, hold the families of this country in your decisions, as though each law you write were written for one particular child whose face you could see.

[PAUSE]

Abraham Lincoln ended his second inaugural not with a boast but with a long sentence of duties, a sentence in which the work of the living was gathered up and handed to the people. He spoke of binding wounds and caring for widows and orphans and doing all that might bring a just and lasting peace. He spoke without malice and with charity. And forty-one days later, he was dead, and the work he had named was left for others to finish.

I am not Lincoln, and this is not a war. But I want to end, in his spirit, with my own sentence of duties.

[BUILD]

So let us, with blame for no one and room for everyone, with firmness in our purpose and humility about our means, with patience for the long work and urgency for the short time, strive on to build the homes our children will grow up in, to lift the burden from the parents who carry it, to honor the mothers and fathers who give the nation its tomorrow, to help those who long for a child and cannot have one, to keep our promises to families across every change of party and government, to make our work fit our lives instead of our lives fit our work, to tell our young people that they are wanted and that their children will be wanted too, and to do all that may make this a country where the families people hope for are the families they have, and where every child born is born into a people that wants a future.

[PAUSE]

We will not live to see most of what we plant. That has always been the bargain. The people who built the cathedrals did not see them finished. The people who planted the oak trees did not sit in their shade. The parents who raised us did not know what we would become.

But they planted anyway. They built anyway. They had children anyway. They placed their bet on a future they would never see, and that bet is the reason we are here.

[LET IT LAND]

Now it is our turn to place the bet.

Let us be a people that wants a future. Let us build a country that deserves one. And let us look into the eyes of our children, and see in them what every generation before us has seen, that the story is not over, that the best of us goes on, and that tomorrow is worth entering.

[PAUSE]

Thank you.
