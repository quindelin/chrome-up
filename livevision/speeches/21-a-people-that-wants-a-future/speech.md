# A People That Wants a Future

## I. The Courtyard at Fontainebleau

I want to begin with a man saying goodbye.

It is the twentieth of April, eighteen fourteen. The place is a courtyard at the palace of Fontainebleau, south of Paris. Two weeks earlier the Emperor of the French signed his abdication. Paris has fallen. The armies of Europe are in the capital. And the man who for twenty years had remade the map of a continent is about to leave for an island in the Mediterranean.

Before he goes, he comes down the great horseshoe staircase to the men who are left. The Old Guard. The veterans. Soldiers who had followed him across the Alps and into the snows of Russia and back again.

[PAUSE]

He does not give them a lecture on strategy. He does not blame them. He does not blame himself either, which tells you something about the man. He tells them that he is going so that France will not be torn apart by a civil war. He tells them that he will write down what they did together. He tells them to serve the country that remains.

And then comes the moment that painters and storytellers have kept alive for two centuries. He says that he cannot embrace every one of them. So he embraces their general, General Petit, in the name of them all. And he asks for the eagle, the standard they had carried, and he kisses it.

The historians at the Fondation Napoleon will tell you, honestly, that this scene has been polished by legend and by propaganda. The words vary from account to account. The tears may have grown with each retelling. I tell it to you as what it has become, a story about farewell.

[SLOW]

But notice what the story is about. It is about a man who could not hold everyone he loved, and so he held one person, and one symbol, on behalf of all of them.

Keep that picture in your mind. I am going to return to it at the end.

[PAUSE]

Tonight I want to talk about a different kind of farewell. Not a farewell to an emperor, but a farewell to an assumption.

For almost all of human history, the people who lived in a place assumed that there would be more people after them. Not always richer. Not always safer. But more. More children in the schools. More hands in the fields and the factories. More voices at the table on the holidays. The future was a room that would be fuller than the present.

That assumption is leaving us. Quietly. Without a ceremony in a palace courtyard. In country after country, the number of children being born has fallen below the number needed to replace the parents. In some places it has fallen so far that each generation is on track to be little more than half the size of the one before it.

This is the fifth and last speech in a series about that fact. The earlier speeches counted the empty cradles, priced the cost of a child, weighed the career against the family, and looked at a nation that has stopped marrying.

This one is different. This one is a call.

[BUILD]

I am going to ask a question that every serious country on earth is now asking. What actually works? Not what feels good to say. Not what wins a news cycle. What has actually been tried, by real governments, with real money, over real decades, and what happened?

We are going to go to France, which has been trying for almost a hundred years. To Hungary, which bet more than almost anyone and has just watched the bet come due. To the Nordic countries, which built the most generous family policy the world has ever seen and still watched their birth rates fall by a quarter or a third. To South Korea, which spent a fortune and reached the lowest fertility rate ever recorded in a modern nation. And to Israel, the rich country that somehow never got the memo.

We are going to talk about money, about houses, about child allowances and tax credits. And then we are going to talk about something money cannot buy, which is the belief that the future is worth entering.

[PAUSE]

Here is my thesis, so you can test everything I say against it.

Pronatal policy is not a vending machine. You cannot put in dollars and get out babies. The record of the last thirty years proves that beyond any reasonable doubt.

But the record also proves something else. It proves that the gap between the families people say they want and the families they actually have is real, that parts of that gap can be closed, and that the countries which come closest to closing it are the ones that combine patient, generous, boring policy with a culture that treats children as a good thing and the future as a good place.

And it proves, I think, that wanting children is in the end an act of faith. A bet placed on a future you will not live to see. A people that wants a future has to build a country where that bet looks reasonable.

[LET IT LAND]

That is what I want to argue for the next two hours. Not with malice. Not with blame. Not by telling any person what to do with their own life. But as one citizen speaking to others about the one inheritance that every society must pass on or lose, which is the next generation itself.

## II. The Count

Let us start with the count. I will go slowly, because numbers spoken aloud are easy to lose.

Demographers use a figure called the total fertility rate. It is the number of children a woman would have over her lifetime if she lived through today's birth rates at every age. To replace a population without immigration, a rich country needs a figure of about two point one. Two to replace the parents, and a little more because not every child grows to adulthood.

Now listen to where the world stands.

In the United States, the Centers for Disease Control reported in April of this year that about three million, six hundred and six thousand babies were born in twenty twenty-five. That was down about one percent from the year before. The general fertility rate, which counts births per thousand women of childbearing age, fell to fifty-three point one, a record low. The total fertility rate, as reported by the Wall Street Journal from the same data, fell to about one point five seven.

One point five seven. Not two point one. About three quarters of replacement.

And one more detail from that report, which I think tells the story of a whole generation. In twenty twenty-five, for the first time, women in their late thirties had a higher birth rate than women in their early twenties.

[PAUSE]

Cross the Atlantic. France, for decades the great exception of Europe, the country that seemed to have figured it out. In January the national statistics office, INSEE, reported that French fertility had fallen to one point five six in twenty twenty-five. That is the lowest level since the end of the First World War.

And for the first time since the end of the Second World War, more people died in France than were born. About six hundred and fifty-one thousand deaths. About six hundred and forty-five thousand births. A gap of about six thousand. Small in number. Enormous in meaning.

Go north. Finland recorded a fertility rate of one point two five in twenty twenty-four. Statistics Finland keeps records going back to seventeen seventy-six. That was the lowest figure in all of them. Last year it ticked up, to one point three zero. Sweden fell to one point four two in twenty twenty-five, the lowest ever measured there. Norway rose, a little, to one point four eight.

Go east. Hungary, which we will discuss at length, fell to about one point three one last year, according to preliminary figures. Fewer babies were born there than in any year since nineteen forty-nine. Poland, which launched one of Europe's most famous child benefit programs, recorded a fertility rate of about one point one in twenty twenty-four. In that year about two hundred and fifty-two thousand Poles were born, and about four hundred and nine thousand died.

[SLOW]

Go further east still. Japan recorded about six hundred and seventy-one thousand births last year, the tenth record low in a row, with a fertility rate of one point one four.

And then there is South Korea. In twenty twenty-three, the Korean fertility rate fell to zero point seven two. Not one point seven. Zero point seven. At that rate, every hundred adults would have, in the next generation, about thirty-six children, and those thirty-six would have about thirteen.

Last year Korea climbed to about zero point eight. We will talk about why, because it matters.

[PAUSE]

And then, almost alone among rich countries, there is Israel. Close to three children per woman. Above replacement. A modern, educated, high-income, high-technology country with a fertility rate that the rest of the developed world has not seen in two generations.

Hold all these numbers loosely in your mind. You do not need to remember them. Remember the shape.

The shape is this. Nearly every rich country in the world is below replacement. Most are far below. The ones that were thought to be exceptions, France and the Nordic countries, have stopped being exceptions. The ones that threw the most money at the problem, Korea and Hungary, are not at the top of the list. And the one rich country that sits comfortably above replacement did not get there because of a program.

[BUILD]

That shape is the beginning of wisdom on this subject. If money alone could do it, the countries that spent the most would have the most children. They do not. If modernity alone made it impossible, no modern country would be above replacement. One is.

So the answer, whatever it is, has to be more interesting than money, and more hopeful than fate.

Before we go country by country, one more thing needs to be said about these numbers, because it is the thing most often left out. A low fertility rate is not a disease and it is not a crime. It is the sum of millions of private decisions, made by free people, for reasons that are their own. Nobody in this room owes the state a child. Nobody should be made to feel that their worth depends on how many children they have, or whether they have any at all.

What I want to ask is not whether people are doing their duty. It is whether people are getting what they want. And on that question the evidence is striking.

## III. The Families We Say We Want

In the summer of twenty twenty-three, Gallup asked Americans a question it has been asking since the nineteen thirties. What do you think is the ideal number of children for a family to have?

Forty-five percent said three or more.

That was the highest share since nineteen seventy-one. The average ideal came to about two point seven children. And here is the detail that should stop every pessimist in their tracks. The age group most likely to say three or more was the youngest, adults between eighteen and twenty-nine.

[PAUSE]

Think about that. The generation that is having the fewest children is the generation that says, more than any other, that a big family is the ideal.

The ideal is two point seven. The reality is one point five seven. That distance, more than one child per woman, is not a gap in values. It is a gap between what people hope for and what their lives allow.

Now, you can object that an ideal is not an intention. People say many things to pollsters. Ask me my ideal number of hours of sleep and I will tell you nine, and you can check my phone and see that I get six. That is a fair objection, and I take it seriously.

So let us look at a harder measure.

In twenty twenty-five, the United Nations Population Fund published its annual State of World Population report. For the first time it commissioned a survey, with the polling firm YouGov, of about fourteen thousand adults in fourteen countries, countries that together hold more than a third of humanity. It asked people not about ideals but about expectations.

Almost eighteen percent of people of reproductive age said they expected not to reach the family size they wanted. And most of those, about eleven percent of everyone surveyed, expected to have fewer children than they wanted, not more. Nearly a quarter said that at some point they had wanted a child but had not been able to have one when they wanted.

The UN did not call its report a crisis of too few babies. It called it "the real fertility crisis," and it defined that crisis as the inability of people to have the families they want. It listed the reasons people gave. Economic instability. Lack of support from partners and communities. Poor health care. Lack of access to childcare.

[SLOW]

I want to plant a flag here, because the whole rest of this speech stands on it.

There are two ways to talk about falling birth rates. The first way says: the nation needs more babies, so citizens must produce them. That way has a long and ugly history, and I reject it entirely.

The second way says: millions of people want children, or want more children, and something in the way we have arranged our economy and our culture is stopping them. That is not a demand on the citizen. It is a demand on the country. It says the nation owes its young people a life in which the family they hope for is within reach.

The first way treats people as means. The second way treats them as ends. The first way says you owe us a child. The second way says we owe you a chance.

[LET IT LAND]

Everything I will propose tonight comes from the second way.

And notice that the second way has a built-in limit, which protects us from the abuses of the first. If the goal is to close the gap between the families people want and the families they have, then the goal is never to push anyone past what they want. A woman who wants no children is not a policy failure. A couple who want one child and have one child are a policy success. The measure is not the total. The measure is the gap.

But here is the hard part, and the reason the rest of this speech is necessary. Closing the gap turns out to be very difficult. Countries have tried, and the trying has taught us a great deal, much of it humbling.

So let us go and look at the people who tried. And let us start with the country that has been trying the longest.
