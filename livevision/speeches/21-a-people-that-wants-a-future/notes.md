## What the models teach, and how they are used

Napoleon at Fontainebleau: direct address to comrades ("soldiers", "my children"),
a farewell that is also a charge, the refusal of self-pity, the promise to
record what was done together, and above all the proxy embrace: he cannot hold
every soldier, so he embraces the general and kisses the eagle on behalf of all.
Used here as the frame of the opening and the closing image: the speaker cannot
embrace every child who is not yet born, so the speech ends by asking each
listener to hold one, on behalf of all of them. The Fontainebleau scene is
partly legend (the Fondation Napoleon says so), and the speech says so.

Lincoln's Second Inaugural close: humility about Providence (neither side can
claim God), refusal to gloat, the balanced antithesis, short Anglo-Saxon words,
and one long periodic sentence of resolution that gathers the duties of the
living (bind up, care for, do all). Used in sections XII and XIV: no party owns
this cause, both sides have been wrong, and the peroration is a single long
sentence of charges, written in the speech's own words. No sentence of either
original is copied; Napoleon and Lincoln are described, not quoted at length.

Other techniques: anaphora ("A people that wants a future..."), tricolon,
antithesis (timing vs. number, money vs. meaning), the concrete case carrying
the abstract point (each country is a story with a lesson), a strongest-
objection section answered in full.

## Thesis

Pronatal policy is not a lever that can buy babies, and the record proves it:
Korea spent hundreds of trillions of won and hit 0.72; Hungary spent around
five percent of GDP and rose to 1.6 before sliding back to 1.31; the Nordics
built the best family policy on earth and still fell by a quarter or a third.
But the record also shows what does help: policy kept for generations (France),
help with the third child and with housing (Quebec, Dettling and Kearney),
cheaper and simpler lives for young families, and above all a culture that
treats children as a good and the future as worth entering (Israel, the
religious gap, Georgia). The goal is not a quota but closing the gap between
the families people say they want and the families they have. Wanting children
is, at bottom, an act of faith in the future, and a people that wants a future
has to build a country that makes that faith reasonable.

Strongest counterargument, answered in XII: Kearney and Levine find no economic
or policy factor that explains much of the decline and point to shifting
priorities; add the freedom objection, the environmental objection and the
history of coercion (Romania's Decree 770). Answer: priorities are not formed
in a vacuum; the gap between wanted and actual families is real; coercion is
ruled out absolutely; and a culture can be invited, not commanded.

## Esketian tie (light, as the brief asks)

Source: `Esketian Metaphysics and The Will To God.md`, Metaphysics section,
read in full. The author lists child-rearing among the activities that sustain
existence in time, with the parenthesis that "we see the immortality of God
through the eyes of our children." The speech quotes that parenthesis once,
attributes it to the author's framework, calls it one thinker's metaphysics
rather than proven fact, and uses it as an image in section XIII. It also
borrows, lightly and attributed, the author's idea that the feed is a
counterfeit (knowledge without sustainment) and his picture of a local minimum.
No claim is made that the framework is proven; the author himself holds parts
of it loosely.
