# The Unmarried Nation

## I. The Empty Chair

Good evening, my friends.

I want to talk with you tonight the way you would talk across a kitchen table. No podium voice. No slogans. Just a few honest hours about something that is happening in nearly every family I know, and probably in yours.

[PAUSE]

Picture a dinner table at Thanksgiving. There is the turkey, there is the uncle who argues about football, there is the grandmother who still makes the pie from her mother's recipe. And there, at the end of the table, there is a chair.

Twenty years ago that chair would have been filled by a new boyfriend, a nervous fiancée, a young wife holding a baby who would not stop squirming. Tonight it holds nothing but a coat someone threw over it on the way in.

Nobody mentions the chair. It would be rude. Your son is thirty-one, he has a good job, he lives two states away, and when your aunt asks whether he is seeing anyone, he smiles and changes the subject. Your daughter is twenty-nine, she has two degrees and a demanding career, and she tells you, with a tired laugh, that there is nobody out there worth the trouble.

[PAUSE]

You love them. You do not want to pry. So you pass the potatoes and say nothing.

My friends, I am here to say something.

Because that empty chair is not one family's private sorrow. It is in millions of homes. It is one of the largest changes in the way Americans live that has ever happened in peacetime, and it has happened so quietly, so politely, so one-family-at-a-time, that we have barely noticed it as a nation.

We talk endlessly about the falling birth rate. We argue about whether it matters, whose fault it is, whether the government should pay people to have babies. But we skip over the step that comes before the baby.

Before there is a child, there is a couple.

[SLOW]

Before there is a couple, there is a meeting. A glance. A first awkward dinner. A second one that goes better. A drive home with the radio on and neither person wanting the night to end.

What I want to tell you tonight is that the step before the baby is the step that is breaking. America is not only having fewer children. America is forming fewer couples. Fewer marriages. Fewer partnerships of any kind. Fewer dates. Even, as the surveys tell us, less sex. And the decline is not spread evenly. It has opened a gap between our young men and our young women, in schooling, in politics, in hope, so wide that in some places they seem to be living in two different countries.

[LET IT LAND]

I call it the unmarried nation. Not as an insult. Many of the finest people I know are single, some by choice, some by circumstance, and I will say plainly that there is nothing shameful about either. A good single life is a good life.

But a country is not one life. A country is the sum of millions of choices and chances, and when those choices and chances begin to fall, all of them, in the same direction, at the same time, a country has to ask why.

So tonight I want to do four things. I want to show you what is actually happening, with numbers I have checked and sources you can check too. I want to explain, as best I can, why it is happening, and I will tell you where the evidence is strong and where it is only suggestive. I want to take on the strongest argument against everything I am about to say, and answer it fairly. And then I want to ask something of you.

Because I believe this is a problem a free people can solve. Not by force. Not by shame. By rebuilding the ground on which love grows.

It will take a little while. Pour yourself something warm. Let us begin.

## II. The River at the Edge of the World

Let me start not with a statistic but with a story, one that is more than two thousand years old.

In the year three hundred and twenty-six before Christ, Alexander of Macedon stood on the bank of a river in the Punjab, in what is now northern India. The Greeks called it the Hyphasis. Today we call it the Beas.

He had been marching for eight years. His army had crossed the Hellespont, conquered Asia Minor, taken Egypt, broken the Persian Empire, crossed deserts and mountains and the Hindu Kush. They had fought in heat that killed horses and through rains that rotted their clothes and their boots. They had won every battle they had fought.

And on the far side of that river, the scouts told him, lay rich land, good farmers, brave fighters, and more elephants than anyone had ever seen. Alexander wanted to cross.

[PAUSE]

His men did not.

The ancient historian Arrian tells us that the soldiers gathered in little knots throughout the camp. The moderate ones complained about their lot. The bolder ones said they would not follow him any further, even if he went first.

So Alexander called his officers together and gave them one of the great rallying speeches of the ancient world.

He reminded them of everything they had already crossed. He named the lands one after another: Ionia, the Hellespont, Phrygia, Lydia, Egypt, Babylon, Persia, Bactria, the great rivers of India. He asked them why, after all of that, they would shrink from one more river.

He told them that for a brave man there is no end to labour except the labour itself, if it leads to something glorious. He told them, and this is the part I love, that the labours were common to all of them. That he was not sitting safe in a tent while they bled. That they shared the dangers and they would share the rewards.

It is a magnificent speech. If you read it, you will want to pick up a spear.

[PAUSE]

And then there was silence.

A long silence, Arrian says. The officers did not dare to argue with the king, and they did not want to agree with him either. Alexander asked again and again for anyone with a different view to speak. Still nothing.

Finally an old general named Coenus stood up. He had fought in every battle. Nobody could call him a coward. And he said, in effect: Your Majesty, look how few of us are left. Many died in battle, more died of disease, the rest are worn out in body and more worn out in spirit.

And then he said the thing that turned the whole army.

[SLOW]

He said the men who still had living parents yearned to see them once more. They yearned for their wives and their children. They yearned for their native land.

When Coenus finished, the officers applauded, and many of them wept.

Alexander was furious. He went into his tent and would not come out for three days. He waited for the army to change its mind. It did not. And so, in the end, the man who had never been defeated by any enemy was turned back by his own men's longing for home.

When he announced that they would return, Arrian says, the soldiers shouted and cried for joy, and some came to his tent to bless him, because he had allowed himself to be conquered by them alone.

He built twelve great altars on that riverbank, and then he turned around.

[LET IT LAND]

My friends, I have thought about that story a great deal while preparing to speak to you tonight. Because I am, in a way, trying to do what Alexander did. I want to rally a tired people. I want to ask a generation that is exhausted, anxious, broke and lonely to cross one more river.

But I have come to believe that Coenus had the better argument.

What moved those soldiers was not glory. It was not land or gold or elephants. It was the picture in their minds of a door opening, and a wife, and children running down a path, and a mother's face. They had marched to the edge of the known world, and what they wanted at the end of it was someone to come home to.

That is what human beings want. It has not changed in two thousand three hundred years.

And here is the cruel twist of our moment. Many of our young people have marched a long way too. They have crossed the rivers we told them to cross. They studied hard. They took the tests. They took on the loans. They moved to the cities where the jobs were. They worked the long hours and kept their heads down. They did everything we asked.

And when they stand at the end of that march and look around for the home they were supposed to be building, too many of them find that there is nobody there.

[PAUSE]

So the river I am asking us to cross tonight is not a river of conquest. It is the river between two people. Between a young man and a young woman who are both lonely, both wary, both staring at screens in apartments a few blocks apart. That is the Hyphasis of our time. It is narrower than any river Alexander ever crossed, and somehow it has become much harder to cross.

Let me show you how hard.

## III. What the Numbers Say

I promised you numbers I had checked, so let me lay them out the way Franklin Roosevelt laid out the banking system in his first fireside chat: plainly, one step at a time, because you are perfectly capable of understanding them.

Start with the long view.

In nineteen sixty, according to the Pew Research Center, seventy-two percent of American adults were married. By twenty-ten, it was fifty-one percent. Barely half.

Among young adults, the change was far sharper. In nineteen sixty, fifty-nine percent of Americans between eighteen and twenty-nine were married. By twenty-ten, it was twenty percent.

[PAUSE]

Think about what that means. In your grandparents' generation, most people in their twenties were married. In your children's generation, four out of five are not.

Some of that is simply delay. In nineteen sixty, the typical woman married for the first time at about twenty, and the typical man at about twenty-three. By the time of that twenty-eleven Pew report, it was twenty-six and a half for women and nearly twenty-nine for men, and it has kept rising since.

Now, delay by itself is not a catastrophe. A couple who marry at twenty-eight instead of twenty-two may be more mature, more financially stable, more likely to stay together. I am not here to romanticize teenage weddings.

But delay has a way of turning into absence.

[SLOW]

In twenty twenty-three, Pew published a short report with a headline that should have stopped the country in its tracks. It found that in twenty twenty-one, a record twenty-five percent of forty-year-old Americans had never been married.

One in four.

In twenty-ten, that figure was twenty percent. In nineteen eighty, it was six percent. The researchers went back through census records all the way to the nineteenth century, and they found no year in which a higher share of forty-year-olds had never married.

And the people who have not married by forty mostly do not marry later. Pew found that of those who were still unmarried at forty in two thousand one, only about one in four had married by the time they reached sixty.

[PAUSE]

Now, someone will say: but people are living together without marrying. Cohabitation has replaced marriage. The ring is just a piece of paper.

That is partly true, and it is an argument worth taking seriously. So let us look at it.

In twenty twenty-one, Pew examined Americans in their prime years, ages twenty-five to fifty-four, and asked a broader question. Not whether they were married, but whether they were partnered at all: married, or living with a partner.

In nineteen ninety, sixty-seven percent of that age group were married, and four percent were living with a partner. By twenty nineteen, fifty-three percent were married and nine percent were cohabiting.

Do the arithmetic and you see the problem. Cohabitation went up by five points. Marriage went down by fourteen. Living together did not fill the hole. It filled about a third of it.

So the share of prime-age adults who were unpartnered, neither married nor living with anyone, rose from twenty-nine percent in nineteen ninety to thirty-eight percent in twenty nineteen.

[LET IT LAND]

Nearly four in ten Americans in the years when people traditionally build families are building them with nobody.

And here is a detail that matters for everything else I will say tonight. In nineteen ninety, men and women in that age group were equally likely to be unpartnered. Twenty-nine percent each. By twenty nineteen, it was thirty-nine percent of men and thirty-six percent of women. The men have fallen further.

Now let me be fair about the most recent numbers, because the story is not a straight line down.

The CDC counts marriages every year. In twenty twenty-two the marriage rate was six point two per thousand Americans. In twenty twenty-three, six point one. In twenty twenty-four, the latest provisional figure, it was six point three, a little over two point one million weddings.

So the marriage rate has stabilized since the pandemic. Weddings that were postponed in twenty twenty have happened. That is good news, and I will not pretend otherwise.

But stabilizing at a low level is not the same as recovering. The wedding halls are busy again. The share of adults who will never enter one is still at a record high.

[PAUSE]

Let me add one more thing about who is marrying, because it tells us something important.

In that same Pew study of forty-year-olds, the share who had never married was about one in three among those with a high school diploma or less. Among those with a bachelor's degree or more, it was eighteen percent.

Marriage in America has become, in part, a class marker. It is thriving among the college-educated and it is collapsing among working people. The very families who could most use the stability of two incomes and two parents are the least likely to have it.

And in Pew's study of prime-age adults, the contrast between partnered and unpartnered men was stark. Ninety-one percent of partnered men were employed, compared with seventy-three percent of unpartnered men. The partnered men had median earnings of fifty-seven thousand dollars. The unpartnered men, thirty-five thousand six hundred. Thirty-one percent of unpartnered men were living with their parents. Among partnered men, two percent.

[SLOW]

I want to be careful here. Those numbers do not tell us which way the arrow points. Does a good job make a man more likely to marry, or does marriage make a man work harder and earn more? The honest answer is probably both. A steady job makes a man a better prospect. A wife and child give a man a reason to get up in the morning.

But whichever way the arrow points, the picture is the same. There is a large and growing group of American men who are not working full time, not earning much, living in their childhood bedrooms, and not partnered with anyone. And there is a large and growing group of American women who look at those men and decide, quite reasonably, that they would rather be alone.

That is not a moral failing on either side. It is a broken market, and broken markets can be fixed.

But before I talk about fixing it, I need to tell you why it matters so much. Because there are people who will hear everything I have said so far and shrug. People marry less. So what?

Here is so what.

## IV. Fewer Couples, Fewer Children

There is a simple fact at the center of this whole subject, and it is so obvious that experts sometimes forget to say it out loud.

[SLOW]

Babies come from couples.

Not from governments. Not from tax credits. Not from think-tank reports or presidential speeches. A child begins when two people trust each other enough to make a life together, even for a night, and in the overwhelming majority of cases, for a great deal longer than a night.

So when the number of couples falls, the number of children falls with it. You can have all the baby bonuses in the world, and if there is nobody to have the baby with, the bonus sits unclaimed.

Let me show you how this works in the American numbers.

In April of this year, the National Center for Health Statistics published its provisional count of births for twenty twenty-five. There were about three million six hundred and six thousand babies born in the United States last year. That was about one percent fewer than in twenty twenty-four.

The general fertility rate, which counts births per thousand women of childbearing age, fell from fifty-three point eight to fifty-three point one. The birth rate for teenagers fell seven percent, to the lowest level ever recorded.

[PAUSE]

Now, I want to say clearly: the drop in teen births is good news. Nobody in this room wants fifteen-year-olds raising babies. Some of the decline in American fertility is exactly the decline we wanted, and we should be honest about that.

But the decline does not stop with teenagers. It runs through women in their early twenties, and through their late twenties, the years when, for most of human history, most families began. And by the best international estimates, the American total fertility rate is now about one point six children per woman. A population replaces itself at about two point one. We are well below that line, and we have been for well over a decade.

So where are the missing children?

[PAUSE]

Here is where the couples come in.

In twenty twenty-four, the CDC tells us, thirty-nine point five percent of American births were to unmarried mothers. That means roughly sixty percent, three in every five American babies, were born to married parents.

Now think about what happens to a country where three in five babies are born inside marriage, and the share of young adults who are married falls from well over half to one in five. You do not need a doctorate in demography to see the result. You are removing people from the room where most of the babies are made.

And among the unmarried births, a large share are to couples who are living together. Children born entirely outside of any partnership are a smaller slice still.

Let me put it even more concretely, with a number from that Pew study of prime-age adults that I find almost unbearably sad.

Among men aged twenty-five to fifty-four who were married or living with a partner, sixty-one percent were living with a child of their own. Among men the same age who were unpartnered, eight percent.

[LET IT LAND]

Sixty-one percent versus eight percent.

That is the whole argument in two numbers. When a man has a partner, he usually has children in his home. When he does not, he almost never does.

For women, the gap is smaller, because single mothers are far more common than single fathers. Thirty-two percent of unpartnered women were living with their own child, compared with sixty percent of partnered women. But the direction is the same.

[PAUSE]

Some demographers have gone further and argued that most of the fall in births across the developed world is not about married couples having fewer children. Married couples, they argue, still want and still have something close to two. It is about fewer people becoming married couples at all.

I have not been able to pin down a single precise figure for how much of the American decline that explains, and I will not invent one for you. Different researchers slice it different ways. But I will tell you my own judgment, and you can weigh it: when the share of people in partnerships collapses, and partnership is where nearly all children are raised, you do not need any more evidence to know that the collapse in coupling is a large part of the collapse in births. Maybe the largest part.

And that changes how we should think about solutions.

[SLOW]

For years, the debate about birth rates has been a debate about money. Make childcare cheaper. Expand the child tax credit. Lengthen parental leave. I support many of those ideas, and I will come back to them. They matter.

But notice what they assume. They assume there is already a couple, sitting at a kitchen table, doing the math, deciding whether they can afford a second or third child. They are policies for people who have already crossed the river.

What about the people who are still standing on the bank? The thirty-year-old man who has not been on a date in two years? The thirty-four-year-old woman who has given up on the apps and decided to freeze her eggs and hope? No child tax credit reaches them, because they have no child and no one to have one with.

If we want more children in America, and I believe a nation that is confident in its future does want that, we have to start one step earlier. We have to ask why so many of our people are not finding each other.

[PAUSE]

Let me be clear about one more thing before I go further. I am not saying that every person must marry, or that every couple must have children. Some people do not want children. Some people cannot have them. Some of the most generous and fruitful lives I know belong to people who never had children of their own and poured themselves into nieces and nephews, students and neighbors.

What I am saying is this. In my experience, most young Americans, when you sit with them long enough for the jokes to run out, still hope to find a partner, and most still hope, someday, for children. I believe the gap is not mainly between what people want and what the government wants. It is between what people want for themselves and what they are actually able to build.

And when a whole generation is falling short of its own hopes, that is not a private matter. That is a public emergency wearing the disguise of a thousand private disappointments.

[PAUSE]

Let me add a word of history here, because honesty requires it.

People sometimes talk as if the American family of the nineteen fifties, married at twenty, three or four children, a house in the suburbs, was the natural state of humankind, and everything since has been a fall from grace. That is not right. The years after the Second World War were unusual. Young people who had lived through a depression and a war married early and had children in a rush. The baby boom was, in many ways, a historical exception, not the rule.

So I am not asking us to go back to nineteen fifty-five. We could not if we tried, and much of what that era demanded of women, and of anyone who did not fit its mold, we should not want back.

But notice something about that exception. It happened right after a generation had endured enormous hardship. The Depression and the war had been far harder than anything our young people face today. And yet, when the hardship lifted, those young people did not retreat into themselves. They reached for each other. They built families on very little, in small houses, with one car if they were lucky.

[SLOW]

What made the difference was not wealth. They were poorer than we are. It was something closer to confidence. A belief that the future was worth building, and that you did not have to build it alone.

That confidence is what I think we have lost, and what I think we can recover.

[PAUSE]

And there is one more reason this matters, beyond the number of children. It matters for the children who are born.

The economist Melissa Kearney, whose work I will return to later, has made the case that children raised by two parents have real advantages on average, in resources, in time, in stability, and that the decline of marriage has fallen hardest on the families who were already struggling. That is not a judgment of single parents, many of whom are performing heroic work every day against very long odds. It is simply a recognition that raising a child is the hardest job there is, and it is easier with two people sharing the load.

So the unmarried nation is not only a nation with fewer children. It is, increasingly, a nation in which the children we do have are raised with fewer hands to hold them.

So let us look more closely at what is going wrong, starting with the most intimate place of all.

## V. The Quiet Recession

There is an awkward subject we have to discuss, and I promise to do it as gracefully as an old fireside allows.

A few years ago, writers began using a phrase that made people laugh and then stop laughing: the sex recession.

The idea was that the generation everyone assumed must be the most sexually active in history, the generation raised with dating apps and the internet and every kind of freedom, was in fact having less sex than its parents and grandparents.

At first, many people dismissed this. Surely, they said, it is just young people being more honest on surveys. Or more selective. Or waiting longer.

[PAUSE]

Then the careful researchers looked.

In twenty twenty, a team of scholars led by Peter Ueda published a study in the medical journal JAMA Network Open. They used the General Social Survey, one of the longest-running and most respected surveys of American life, and they asked a simple question: how many young adults reported having had no sex at all in the past year?

Among men aged eighteen to twenty-four, in the years two thousand to two thousand two, it was about nineteen percent. By twenty sixteen to twenty eighteen, it was about thirty-one percent.

[SLOW]

Nearly one in three young men had gone an entire year without a single sexual partner.

Among young women, the share also rose, from about fifteen percent to about nineteen percent, but the researchers found that change was not statistically significant. The big shift was among the men.

And the researchers found something else, which brings us back to that Pew data on work and income. Among men, being unemployed or working only part-time was linked with higher odds of having no sex. So was low income. A man earning less than ten thousand dollars a year was much more likely to be sexually inactive than a man earning fifty thousand or more. Among women, income and employment made little difference.

[PAUSE]

Now, I can already hear the objection, and it is a fair one. Is this really a problem? Plenty of people, including plenty of religious people, would say that young people having less casual sex is a good thing. Fewer unplanned pregnancies. Fewer broken hearts. Fewer diseases. Teen births at record lows.

I take that seriously. If the sex recession meant that young people were waiting for committed, loving relationships before becoming intimate, I would cheer it from this stage.

But that is not what the data show. The data show that the decline in sex is happening alongside a decline in relationships, not instead of casual encounters but instead of everything. Young people are not trading hookups for marriages. They are losing both.

Remember the Pew numbers on singles. In early twenty twenty-three, Pew found that sixty-three percent of American men under thirty described themselves as single. Among women of the same age, thirty-four percent.

[LET IT LAND]

Sixty-three percent and thirty-four percent.

How can that be? If there are roughly as many young men as young women, how can nearly two-thirds of the men be single and only one-third of the women?

Part of the answer is that young women often date men a few years older, so a twenty-four-year-old woman's boyfriend might be twenty-nine, or thirty-two, and outside that under-thirty group. Part of the answer is that a smaller number of men may be dating more than one woman, and a larger number of men are dating no one. And part of it may be that men and women describe the same situation differently. A woman who has been seeing someone casually for a month may say she is in a relationship. The man may say he is single.

But however you explain it, the human reality is the same. There is a very large number of young men who are alone.

[PAUSE]

And here is what worried me most in that same Pew report. In twenty nineteen, sixty-one percent of single men said they were looking for a relationship or dates. By twenty twenty-two, it was fifty percent. Among single women, the share looking fell from thirty-eight percent to thirty-five percent. Overall, the share of single Americans who were looking at all fell from forty-nine percent to forty-two percent in just three years.

People are not just failing to find love. A growing number have stopped looking.

[SLOW]

My friends, that is what giving up looks like in a survey.

Think about Alexander's soldiers on the bank of the Hyphasis. Arrian tells us that Alexander called his council because he wanted to act before the mood in his camp grew any worse. The danger was not the enemy across the river. It was the mood in his own camp. The flagging spirit. The little knots of men complaining. The silence when he asked for someone to speak.

That is where we are. Not a crisis of enemies, but a crisis of spirit. A generation that has been disappointed often enough that it has decided disappointment is safer than hope.

And I understand that. I truly do. Rejection hurts. Being ignored hurts more. Going on a date with someone who looks nothing like their photo, or who spends the evening on their phone, or who never texts back, is exhausting. After enough of that, staying home with a screen and a streaming service feels like self-respect.

[PAUSE]

But I want to say something gently to anyone listening who has stopped looking.

The loneliness you feel is not proof that something is wrong with you. Millions of people your age feel it too. You are not failing at a game everyone else is winning. Most people are losing at it, and almost everyone is pretending otherwise.

The system you are trying to find love inside is badly designed. It was not designed to help you find a partner. As we are about to see, it was designed for something else entirely.

## VI. The Marketplace of Faces

For most of human history, here is how people met the person they married.

They met through family. Through church or synagogue or mosque. Through school. Through work. Through friends who said, you know, my cousin is moving to town, you should meet her. They met at dances and at county fairs and at the neighbor's barbecue.

All of those ways of meeting had something in common. They happened inside a web of people who knew you. Your reputation came with you. If a young man treated a young woman badly, her brother knew, her friends knew, the pastor knew, and the young man's mother heard about it by Sunday.

That web was sometimes stifling. It enforced rules that were often unfair, especially to women and to people who did not fit in. I do not want it back in every respect.

But it did something important. It made strangers into acquaintances before they became partners. It vouched for people.

[PAUSE]

Then, in the space of about a decade, much of that changed.

Pew Research reported in twenty twenty-three that three in ten American adults have used a dating site or app. Among adults under thirty, more than half, fifty-three percent. About one in ten partnered Americans met their current partner online. Among partnered adults under thirty, about one in five.

Now, I want to be fair to the apps. For a lot of people they have been a gift. They have helped people in small towns, gay and lesbian Americans who could not easily find each other, divorced parents with no time for bars, people who are shy, people who are new to a city. Real marriages, many of them happy, began with a swipe. I know some of those couples, and so do you.

But listen to what the people who use the apps say about them.

[SLOW]

In that same Pew study, among recent users, fifty-four percent of women said they felt overwhelmed by the number of messages they received. Only twenty-five percent of men said the same. And sixty-four percent of men said they felt insecure about the lack of messages they received, compared with forty percent of women.

Think about what those two numbers describe. Women drowning. Men starving. In the same pool, at the same time.

[LET IT LAND]

That is not a marketplace that works. It is a marketplace that produces exhaustion on one side and humiliation on the other.

Here is the way I have come to understand it. When you meet someone through a friend or at church or at work, you meet one person at a time, in three dimensions, with a voice and a laugh and a way of holding a coffee cup. You see them be kind to a waiter. You see them in a bad mood and a good one.

On an app, you meet a hundred people at once, in two dimensions, as a photograph and a few lines of text. You judge them in a fraction of a second, and they judge you. And because the choice seems endless, every individual choice seems cheap.

[PAUSE]

A woman with a hundred messages has no reason to give any single man the benefit of the doubt. Why would she? There are ninety-nine more. So she filters ruthlessly, often on height, on looks, on income, on a single photo, because she has to filter somehow.

A man with no messages has every reason to despair. So he swipes on everyone, which makes his attention worthless to the women receiving it, which makes them filter harder, which leaves him with even fewer replies.

Nobody in that loop is a villain. The woman is not shallow. The man is not entitled. They are both responding sensibly to a machine that rewards the wrong behavior.

And the machine has a business model. I want to say this carefully, because I am not accusing anyone of a conspiracy. But a dating company earns money when people keep using its product. A couple who meet, fall in love, and delete the app forever are a success story and a lost customer at the same time. That does not mean the companies are trying to keep people single. It means their incentives are not perfectly lined up with yours.

[PAUSE]

[PAUSE]

Let me paint you a picture, and I will tell you up front that it is a composite, built from the kinds of stories so many families tell.

A young woman in nineteen fifty-something goes to a church dance with her sister. She does not want to go. Her shoes pinch. A young man who has just come back from the service is standing by the punch bowl, and his friend dares him to ask the shy girl in the blue dress to dance. He does, badly. He steps on her foot. She laughs. He asks if he can walk her home. Her sister walks three steps behind them the entire way, which is the first of many tests he will have to pass.

By the next summer they are married. Her father has asked around about him. His mother has met her at church. Half the town knows them both. When they fight, as every couple does, there are a dozen people who know them well enough to say: you two belong together, work it out.

[SLOW]

Now picture the same two people today. She is on her phone, scrolling past his photo in under a second because he is not smiling in it. He sends a message to her and to forty other women on the same night, because that is what the advice videos tell him to do. Neither of them knows a single person who knows the other. If they do meet, and it goes badly once, there is no sister, no pastor, no mother, no town to nudge them toward a second chance. There is only the next face on the screen.

Same two people. Same hearts. A completely different set of odds.

There are signs that young people have noticed. In twenty twenty-five, the Wall Street Journal reported that Match Group, the company behind Tinder and Hinge and many other apps, was cutting about thirteen percent of its workforce amid weak demand from younger users. You hear more and more about running clubs and supper clubs and in-person singles events filling up, about young people who say they would rather meet someone in real life.

That is a hopeful sign. It suggests the generation that grew up inside the marketplace of faces is beginning to look for the door.

But here is my worry. If you take away the app and send a young person out into the world to meet someone the old-fashioned way, where exactly do they go?

The church they never attended? The bowling league that closed? The friends who are all at home on their own phones? The office that went remote?

[SLOW]

We did not only change how people meet. We tore down most of the places where they used to meet, and the apps rushed in to fill the empty lot.

And the reason so many of those places emptied is a story about a small glass rectangle that every one of us is carrying right now.

## VII. Alone Together

Take your phone out of your pocket for a moment. Or, if you are sensible and left it in the car, just picture it.

[PAUSE]

It is an astonishing object. It holds every book ever written, every song ever recorded, a map of the entire world, a camera better than the ones professional photographers used when I was young, and a direct line to nearly every human being on earth.

And yet, somehow, in the years since it arrived in every pocket, we have become lonelier.

The social psychologist Jonathan Haidt published a book in twenty twenty-four called The Anxious Generation. His argument, in brief, is that between about twenty-ten and twenty-fifteen, childhood in the developed world was rewired. Teenagers moved from a childhood built around play, in person, with friends and siblings and neighbors, to a childhood built around a phone. And he argues that this rewiring helps explain the sharp rise in anxiety, depression and self-harm among young people that began right around then, in many countries at once.

[PAUSE]

I want to be fair here, as I have promised to be all evening. Haidt's argument is contested. Some serious researchers, the psychologist Candice Odgers among them, writing in the journal Nature, have argued that the evidence does not show that social media causes large harms on its own, and that the arrows of cause and effect may run partly the other way. Anxious kids may reach for phones, rather than phones making kids anxious. This debate is not settled, and anyone who tells you it is settled is selling something.

But you do not need to settle the mental health debate to see what phones have done to time. A day has twenty-four hours. If several of them go to a screen, they come from somewhere. They come from sleep, from homework, from family dinners, and above all from time spent face to face with other people.

[SLOW]

And face-to-face time with other people is where love begins.

Consider friendship, which is the soil in which romance usually grows. In twenty twenty-one, the Survey Center on American Life asked Americans how many close friends they had. In nineteen ninety, when the question was asked in a similar survey, three percent of Americans said they had no close friends at all. By twenty twenty-one, it was twelve percent. In nineteen ninety, thirty-three percent said they had ten or more close friends. By twenty twenty-one, thirteen percent.

[LET IT LAND]

The number of Americans with no close friends quadrupled in thirty years.

Now think about how couples have always formed. Very often, through friends. Your friend's roommate. Your friend's sister. The guy your friend brought to the party. A wide circle of friends is a wide circle of possible introductions. A narrow circle is a narrow chance.

When friendship thins, romance thins with it.

[PAUSE]

There is something else the phone does, and it is subtler. It makes being alone comfortable.

In the past, loneliness was painful in a way that pushed you out the door. A young man alone in a rented room on a Saturday night, with nothing but a radio and four walls, felt the walls closing in. So he went out. He went to the dance hall or the diner or the church social, not because he was brave, but because staying home was unbearable.

Today the same young man has a phone that will entertain him until four in the morning. Games that never end. Videos that never stop. Pornography, endless and free, which I will mention only once and plainly, because many parents and many young men themselves worry it substitutes a screen for a person. The phone does not cure his loneliness. But it numbs it just enough that he never has to do the frightening thing that would cure it.

[SLOW]

The phone is a painkiller. And like many painkillers, it treats the symptom and leaves the disease alone.

The same is true for young women, though the pattern looks different. For many young women, the phone is not a source of games but a source of comparison, a constant parade of other people's beautiful lives and perfect relationships and flawless faces, and a constant stream of messages from strangers, some flattering, some threatening, many just tiring. It is not surprising that a young woman who spends her evenings fending off strangers online would rather not spend her weekend meeting more of them in person.

[PAUSE]

Let me tell you about something I have seen in more than one restaurant. Maybe you have seen it too.

A young couple sit across from each other at dinner. They are clearly on a date. They are dressed up a little. And for long minutes at a time, both of them are looking down at their phones.

They are not being rude, exactly. They have simply never known a world in which you did not do that. The phone sits on the table like a third guest, and it is the most interesting one.

Now picture that same pair seventy years ago. Nothing on the table but a candle and a salt shaker. They have no choice but to look at each other. They are forced, for two hours, into the terrifying and wonderful work of paying attention to another human being.

[LET IT LAND]

Love, at bottom, is a kind of attention. And we have built a machine that is very, very good at taking attention away.

I am not proposing that we smash our phones. I could not live without mine either, and I suspect neither could you. But I am proposing that we stop pretending the phone is neutral. It is not. It is the most powerful device ever invented for keeping people apart while making them feel connected.

And there are things we can do about it, which I will come to. Haidt himself proposes some of them: keeping phones out of schools for the whole school day, giving children basic phones before smartphones, raising the age at which kids can open social media accounts, and letting children play freely in the real world again. Many schools and states have moved in exactly that direction in the last two years.

Those are good ideas for children. But we also need a version for adults. We need to rebuild a social life that does not run through a screen.

Because there is one more thing the phone has done, and it may be the most dangerous of all. It has sorted our young men and our young women into separate worlds, with separate feeds, separate heroes, separate villains and separate truths. And that brings us to the gap.

## VIII. Two Countries in One Generation

I want to talk now about something that is uncomfortable to discuss in public, because whatever you say about it, somebody will hear an insult. So let me start with a promise.

I am not going to blame young women for doing well. And I am not going to blame young men for doing badly. Both of those are cheap, and both are wrong. What I want to do is describe a gap that has opened up between them, and ask what it means for a country that depends, as every country always has, on young men and young women finding each other.

[PAUSE]

Start with school.

In the fall of twenty twenty-one, according to the National Center for Education Statistics, fifty-eight percent of American undergraduates were women. Forty-two percent were men. About eight point nine million women and six point five million men.

In my parents' day, that gap ran the other way. Men far outnumbered women on campus. The great movement to open higher education to women was one of the finest achievements of the last century, and every father of a daughter in this room should be grateful for it. I certainly am.

But the pendulum did not stop in the middle. It swung past it. And now, across the country, there are nearly four women in college for every three men.

[SLOW]

Now ask yourself what that means for marriage.

For most of history, women have tended to marry men with at least as much education and income as they had. You may think that preference is old-fashioned. You may think it will fade. But so far, the evidence is that it has faded only slowly. Many educated women still want a partner who matches them.

Think about what happens when you put those two facts together. A growing pool of educated women, a shrinking pool of educated men, and a preference that has not yet adjusted. The arithmetic is unforgiving. A great many women who did everything right, who studied and worked and built careers, will look around in their thirties and find that the men they would have chosen are already taken, and the men who remain are men they have trouble imagining a future with.

[PAUSE]

And look at it from the other side. Remember the men Pew described: the unpartnered men, with lower employment, lower earnings, and three in ten living with their parents. The young man without a degree, in a town where the factory closed, working part-time at a warehouse, with no clear path upward. He is not a villain. He is not lazy. He is a young man for whom the economy and the school system have offered very little, and who knows, every time he opens a dating app, exactly how little he looks like on paper.

What does that man have to offer a partner? He has everything that has always mattered most: loyalty, humor, strength, a willingness to show up. But those things do not fit in a profile. And he may have absorbed the message, from a hundred directions, that he is not needed.

[LET IT LAND]

A nation that tells its young men they are not needed should not be surprised when they stop showing up.

The writer and scholar Richard Reeves has spent years making this argument in his book Of Boys and Men. He points out that boys fall behind girls in school from an early age, that their brains in some respects tend to mature later, that they are more likely to be held back, suspended, diagnosed with learning difficulties, and less likely to graduate. He has proposed ideas like letting boys start school a year later, recruiting more male teachers, and investing far more in vocational and technical education. You do not have to agree with every one of his proposals to see that he is describing something real.

[PAUSE]

Let me make this personal for a moment.

Think of two cousins. I will call them Jake and Emma. They grew up three houses apart. They went to the same elementary school. Emma was always the better student, not because Jake was dim, but because she could sit still, do the homework, and please the teacher, and he could not, not yet.

Emma went to the state university, then to a master's program in the city. She works in health care administration now, earns a good salary, and lives in an apartment with two roommates and a cat. She has been on perhaps sixty first dates in five years. Most went nowhere. Many of the men, she says, seemed younger than her, not in age but in seriousness.

Jake did a semester at community college, did not like it, and left. He works at a distribution center, on and off. He lives in his old bedroom. He spends his evenings gaming with friends he has never met in person. He tried the apps for a while and got almost no matches, and he quit. He tells his friends he is fine. He is not fine.

[SLOW]

[PAUSE]

Now let me be fair to Emma, because her story is easy to tell carelessly.

It would be wrong to say that the answer is for women like Emma to lower their standards. Many of her standards are not shallow at all. She wants a man who will carry his share of the housework and the child care, and she has watched friends marry men who did not. She wants a man who is emotionally steady, who can talk about a hard day without shutting down or blowing up. She wants a man who respects her ambitions rather than resenting them. Those are not unreasonable things to want. They are exactly the things a good marriage needs.

[SLOW]

The problem is not that Emma wants too much. The problem is that we have done far too little to raise young men who can offer it. We told our daughters they could be anything, and we were right. We forgot to tell our sons what they were for. We gave girls a new story and left boys with an old one that no longer fit the world, and then we were surprised when the two stories would not fit together.

That is why I believe the gender gap is not a battle of the sexes. It is a failure of the adults. It is our job, as parents and teachers and mentors, to give young men a picture of manhood that is strong and gentle at the same time, ambitious and faithful, confident and kind. A picture that young women would actually want to build a life with.

Emma and Jake are not real people. But you know them. You have them in your own family. And here is the thing that breaks my heart: if Emma and Jake were not cousins but strangers, they would not even appear on each other's screens. Their worlds no longer overlap. Different schools, different jobs, different neighborhoods, different feeds.

And increasingly, different politics.

## IX. The Ballot and the Bedroom

Here is a number that would have surprised almost any political scientist twenty-five years ago.

In twenty twenty-four, Gallup published an analysis of how young Americans, ages eighteen to twenty-nine, describe their own politics. Gallup averaged its surveys over three periods.

From two thousand one to two thousand seven, twenty-eight percent of young women called themselves liberal, and twenty-five percent of young men did. A gap of three points. Barely noticeable.

From two thousand eight to twenty sixteen, it was thirty-two percent of young women and twenty-seven percent of young men. A gap of five.

From twenty seventeen to twenty twenty-four, forty percent of young women called themselves liberal. Among young men, it was still twenty-five percent.

[LET IT LAND]

A gap of fifteen points, and growing. Young women moved sharply left. Young men did not move with them.

Gallup also found that on specific issues, from the environment to abortion to guns, young women had moved substantially, and young men had moved less, so that the gaps between them widened. On most issues, the views of young women now sit much closer to those of older liberals, while young men are split.

[PAUSE]

Now, I am not going to tell you tonight which side is right. Some of you are liberal and some of you are conservative, and I respect you both, and that is not the argument I am making. The argument I am making is about what this does to coupling.

Politics used to be something couples disagreed about at the dinner table and then went to bed. Plenty of long marriages have had one Republican and one Democrat in them. Some of the happiest couples I know cancel each other's votes every November and laugh about it.

But something changed when politics stopped being about policy and started being about identity. When your political party became a kind of tribe, with its own music, its own news, its own enemies and its own sense of who is a good person and who is a bad one. When a vote stopped being a choice and started being a statement about your character.

[SLOW]

Today, many young people say they would not date someone who voted the other way. Whatever the exact share, you have heard it, and you may have said it. Political views are on dating profiles. Disagreement on a single hot-button issue can end a conversation before it starts.

Now combine that with the Gallup numbers. If young women are moving left and young men are not, and if both increasingly refuse to date across the line, then you have taken a dating market that was already strained and cut a canyon down the middle of it.

[PAUSE]

[PAUSE]

Why has this gap opened? Honest scholars disagree, and I will not pretend to settle it. Some point to the different paths men and women have taken through school and work. Some point to the great movements of the last decade around sexual harassment and women's rights, which many young women experienced as a long-overdue reckoning and some young men experienced as an accusation aimed at them personally. Some point to the phone again, to algorithms that feed young men one diet of videos and young women another, each designed to provoke, each rewarding anger with attention.

I suspect all of those play a part. But whatever the causes, the effect is that a young man and a young woman who grew up on the same street can reach adulthood with almost no shared picture of the world. Different heroes, different fears, different villains. And it is hard to fall in love with someone whose whole picture of the world strikes you as alien.

If you want to see where that road can lead, look at South Korea.

South Korea is one of the great success stories of the last century. A country that was among the poorest in the world after its war became, within two generations, one of the richest, most educated, most technologically advanced nations on earth.

And South Korea now has one of the lowest birth rates ever recorded by any nation. In twenty fifteen, its total fertility rate was about one point two four children per woman. By twenty eighteen, it had fallen below one. In twenty twenty-three, it hit about zero point seven two. Less than one child per woman. Each generation roughly a third the size of the one before.

And alongside that collapse, South Korea has seen one of the sharpest political divides between young men and young women anywhere in the democratic world. In the presidential election of twenty twenty-two, which was decided by less than one percentage point, the major broadcasters' exit poll found that among voters aged eighteen to twenty-nine, about fifty-nine percent of young men voted for the conservative candidate, Yoon Suk Yeol. About fifty-eight percent of young women voted for his liberal opponent, Lee Jae-myung.

[LET IT LAND]

Young men and young women in the same country, the same age, the same schools, voting almost as mirror images of each other.

I want to be careful. I am not saying that the gender divide in politics caused South Korea's birth rate to collapse. The causes there are many: crushing work hours, brutal competition for schools and jobs, the very high cost of housing in Seoul, and expectations about who does the housework and the child care that many young women find unacceptable. The gender war is as much a symptom of those pressures as a cause of anything.

But that is exactly my point. When young men and women feel that the system is pitting them against each other, when each sees the other as a rival rather than a partner, they stop coming together. And when they stop coming together, the children stop coming.

[PAUSE]

There is one small piece of hope in the Korean story. In twenty twenty-four, after years of decline, the fertility rate rose slightly, to about zero point seven five, the first increase in nearly a decade. It is far too early to call it a turnaround. But it reminds us that these trends are not laws of physics. They are the result of choices, by people and by governments, and choices can change.

[SLOW]

So here is what I want to say to the young men and young women listening tonight, across whatever political line divides you.

The person on the other side of that line is not your enemy. They are worried about the same rent, the same student loans, the same uncertain future that you are. They want, underneath all the noise, very much what you want. Someone to come home to. Someone who will be glad to see them.

Your political opinions matter. Hold them. Argue for them. Vote for them. But do not let a feed that profits from your anger convince you that half of your generation is unworthy of love.

Alexander's army was made up of Macedonians and Greeks from many cities, peoples who had fought one another for generations and had every reason for grudges. On the march, they became something else. They became people who shared the labour.

That is what we need now. Not agreement. Shared labour.

## X. The Strongest Objection

Now I owe you something. I have spent a long time making my case, and a speech that only makes its own case is not an argument. It is a sermon. So let me give the other side the best hearing I can, in the strongest form I can find.

[PAUSE]

Here is the objection.

Everything you have described, someone might say, is the result of freedom. And freedom is good.

For most of human history, marriage was not a free choice, especially for women. A woman married because she had no other way to eat, no right to own property, no realistic way to earn a living, no access to most professions, and in many places no legal standing apart from her father or her husband. She married because the alternative was poverty and disgrace. She stayed married because divorce was nearly impossible and leaving meant ruin.

The old marriage rates, the objection goes, were high because the cage was locked.

Then the cage was opened. In nineteen sixty, the federal government approved the first birth control pill. Women entered colleges and professions in enormous numbers. Laws changed so that a woman could get a credit card, own a business, leave a violent husband. And as soon as women had real choices, many of them chose to marry later, or to marry less, or not at all.

[SLOW]

From this point of view, the unmarried nation is not a tragedy. It is a liberation. A woman who stays single at thirty-five because she has not met a man worth marrying is not a symptom of social collapse. She is a free person exercising a free choice that her great-grandmother never had. And if men are struggling to adapt to a world in which women no longer need them for survival, the answer is for men to become better partners, not for society to engineer more marriages.

And as for the birth rate, the objection continues, the world has eight billion people. The planet is warming. Fewer children may be a blessing. And in any case, how many children a woman has is her business, not the nation's. Every time governments have tried to tell women how many babies to have, from fascist Italy to communist Romania to China's one-child policy, the results have been cruel.

[PAUSE]

That is a serious argument. Let me tell you what I think it gets right, and then what I think it gets wrong.

It gets right that the freedom of women to choose whether and whom to marry is a precious achievement. I would not trade it for any birth rate. Any solution that involves pressuring women back into dependence, or shaming them for their choices, or restricting their rights, is not a solution I will ever support. Let me say that as plainly as I can. The answer to the unmarried nation is not less freedom.

It gets right that history is full of cruel population policies, and that a free country must never treat its citizens as breeding stock. Every idea I will offer tonight is about widening people's choices, not narrowing them.

And it gets right that men have a great deal of growing to do. Many of the young women who have given up on dating did not give up on a whim. They gave up after being ghosted, insulted, pressured or simply bored by men who had never learned how to court a woman with respect. Men who want to be chosen have to become worth choosing. I will say more about that.

[SLOW]

But here is what the objection gets wrong.

It assumes that what we are seeing is people freely choosing the lives they want. And for some people, that is exactly true. But for many others, the data tell a different story.

Remember the forty-year-olds. One in four never married, a record. Is that because a quarter of Americans set out at twenty-two to remain single for life? I do not believe so, and I do not think most of them believe so either. When I talk with people in their thirties and forties who are single, very few tell me this is what they planned. Many tell me, quietly, that it just did not happen. That they were waiting for the right time, or the right person, or the right job, and the years slipped by.

Remember the single young men in that Pew survey, the share who were even looking falling from sixty-one percent to fifty percent in three years. That is not what freedom looks like. That is what resignation looks like.

And remember the class divide. If declining marriage were simply a story of liberated women choosing independence, you would expect it to be strongest among the most educated and most affluent women, the ones with the most options. But it is the opposite. Marriage is holding up best among the college-educated, and collapsing among working people. The economist Melissa Kearney, in her twenty twenty-three book The Two-Parent Privilege, argues that the decline of marriage has contributed to economic disadvantage in America and has hit the most vulnerable families hardest. Children of the affluent are still mostly raised by two parents. Children of the poor increasingly are not.

[LET IT LAND]

The people losing marriage are not mostly the people who have the most freedom. They are mostly the people who have the least.

[PAUSE]

And here is the deepest thing the objection misses. Freedom is not only the absence of a cage. It is also the presence of a path.

A young man is free to start a business, in the sense that no law forbids him. But if he has no capital, no training, no network and no one who believes in him, that freedom is mostly theoretical. A young woman is free to marry a good man, in the sense that no law forbids her. But if she lives in a town where most of the young men have left or given up, and she meets the rest through an app that shows them to her as a stream of disappointing photographs, that freedom is mostly theoretical too.

Real freedom requires real options. And the options for building a family, for millions of young Americans, have been shrinking.

[SLOW]

As for the birth rate and the planet, I will say this. I care about the environment. But in my judgment, the number of children a family has is the wrong lever for climate policy; cleaner energy and better technology are the right ones. And a country that is aging rapidly, with fewer workers supporting more retirees every year, will find it harder, not easier, to afford the investments that a cleaner future requires. A society that has stopped believing in its own future is rarely a society that plans well for it.

And as for whose business it is, I agree completely. It is no one's business how many children a particular woman has. But it is very much the nation's business whether its people are able to have the families they want. When a whole generation is falling short of its own hopes, a decent country asks why, and it clears the obstacles out of the way. That is not coercion. That is what we do for every other kind of hope, from home ownership to higher education to retirement.

So I accept the objection's love of freedom. I just think it has not followed that love far enough. A country that truly loves freedom wants its people free to build the lives they dream of, and many of them dream of a family.

Now let us talk about the most practical obstacle of all.

## XI. The Money and the House

Let me speak now about money, because whenever I talk with young people about marriage and children, the conversation arrives at money within about five minutes.

[PAUSE]

I want to be careful not to overstate this. If money were the whole story, rich countries would have the highest birth rates and the highest marriage rates, and they do not. Within America, marriage is stronger among the well-off, but birth rates have fallen even among people who are doing well. And many couples in hard times, in the Depression and after the war, married young and raised families in small houses with very little.

So money is not everything. But it is not nothing either. And I think it works on coupling in two ways that are worth understanding.

The first is what you might call the marriageability problem. Remember the Pew numbers. Unpartnered men are much less likely to be employed, earn much less, and are far more likely to live with their parents. Remember the study of the sex recession: among men, low income and lack of full-time work were linked with being sexually inactive.

[SLOW]

Whether we like it or not, many women, and many men too, still think of marriage as something you do once you have a stable footing. A steady job. A place of your own. Some savings. Marriage, for a lot of Americans, has become a capstone, something you do after you have built your life, rather than a cornerstone, something you build your life upon.

And when a large share of young men cannot reach that footing, when wages for men without degrees have been weak for a generation, they are not seen, and often do not see themselves, as ready to marry. So they wait. And waiting, as we have seen, has a way of turning into never.

[PAUSE]

The second way money works on coupling is through the house.

Ask any young couple what stops them from marrying, or from having a child, or from having a second one, and very often the answer is not the wedding or the hospital bill. It is the rent. It is the down payment. It is the realization that a two-bedroom apartment in the city where the jobs are costs more than one of their paychecks.

And one of the saddest effects of this is that many young people cannot even begin the process. A young man living with his parents at twenty-eight is not in a strong position to start a romance. A young woman sharing an apartment with two roommates to make the rent is not in a place where a relationship has room to grow. Housing is not only about where you raise children. It is about whether you have a door you can close, a place you can invite someone back to for dinner, a life of your own to share.

[SLOW]

When we made housing scarce and expensive, through decades of zoning rules, permit delays and resistance to building, we did not only make homes cost more. We made adulthood cost more. And we pushed back the age at which young people feel ready to start one.

[PAUSE]

There is a third financial problem, and it is one that government created all by itself.

In many of our safety-net programs, a couple can lose benefits by marrying. Two low-income adults living separately may each qualify for help. Married, their incomes are added together, and they may lose health coverage, food assistance, a tax credit or child care help. Economists call these marriage penalties, and they run through parts of our tax code and our benefit programs.

I want to be careful: the size of the penalty varies enormously from family to family, and scholars argue about how much it actually changes behavior. But consider the message it sends. We have built a system in which, for some of the poorest Americans, the government charges a fee for getting married.

[LET IT LAND]

If you set out to design a policy to discourage marriage among the people who most need its stability, you could hardly do better.

[PAUSE]

And then there is the debt. Millions of young Americans have student loans that they began paying in their early twenties and will still be paying in their forties. A young woman with a large balance may hesitate to marry a man with a large balance of his own. A young man with debt may feel he has nothing to offer. Debt is not romantic. It does not show up on a dating profile, but it shows up in every conversation about the future.

[SLOW]

[PAUSE]

Let me make this concrete with one more composite couple, because numbers alone can feel abstract.

Call them Daniel and Maria. They are twenty-nine. They have been together four years. They love each other, and they both say, when friends ask, that they will get married someday.

Daniel is a paramedic. Maria teaches second grade. Together they earn a respectable income. But they live in a one-bedroom apartment where the rent has risen sharply in the time they have lived there. Each of them still carries student loans. They have looked at houses in their area and given up. They have priced child care and laughed, the way you laugh at a joke that is not funny.

[SLOW]

So they wait. They tell themselves they will marry when they can afford a real wedding, and have a child when they can afford a second bedroom. Each year, the second bedroom recedes a little further. Maria starts to hear her friends talk about fertility clinics. Daniel works extra shifts and wonders, at three in the morning in the back of an ambulance, whether he will ever be a father.

Nobody is stopping Daniel and Maria from marrying. No law forbids it. But everything about the way we have arranged the economy of young adulthood tells them: not yet. And not yet, repeated often enough, becomes not ever.

So let me sum up what money does. It does not stop people from falling in love. But it stops them from feeling ready to act on it. It delays the wedding, delays the move-in, delays the first child, and then the second child never comes, because by the time the couple feels ready, they are thirty-eight and tired.

Alexander told his men that the labours were common, that he shared the danger and they would share the reward. Our young people have been asked to bear a great deal of labour: the loans, the rent, the long hours, the uncertain jobs. And too often, the reward they were promised, a home and a family of their own, has receded every time they reached for it.

That is a promise we can keep. Let me tell you how.

## XII. Rebuilding the Ground

My friends, I have spent a long time describing the problem. I do not want to leave you with only a diagnosis. Franklin Roosevelt, in that first fireside chat, did not just explain why the banks had failed. He told people what was being done, and what they could do. So let me do the same.

I will start with what government can do, and then, in a moment, I will turn to what only the rest of us can do, which in the end matters more.

[PAUSE]

First, put phones in their place, beginning with children.

The case for keeping smartphones out of classrooms for the whole school day is now strong enough, and the cost low enough, that I believe every school in America should do it. Many have already moved that way. Let children spend the school day looking at teachers and at each other. Let lunch be loud again. Let recess be a place where children learn to negotiate, to lose, to make up, to be brave.

Beyond school, I believe the age at which children can open social media accounts should be raised and actually enforced, and that phone makers should give parents simple tools to make that possible. Whatever one thinks of Haidt's larger claims, the simple principle of giving childhood back to children is one that parents of every political stripe can share.

[SLOW]

Why does this belong in a speech about marriage? Because the children of today are the couples of twenty years from now. A child who learns to make friends face to face, to read a room, to tolerate awkwardness, to handle rejection and try again, is a child who will one day be able to walk across a room and say hello to a stranger. We are not only protecting their mental health. We are protecting their capacity to love.

[PAUSE]

Second, take the boys' education gap seriously.

We spent decades, rightly, working to open every door to girls. We should keep every one of those doors open. And we should now give the same energy to boys who are falling behind. That means more male teachers, especially in the early grades, where boys may go years without seeing a man at the front of a classroom. It means looking hard at proposals like Richard Reeves's idea of giving boys the option of starting school a year later. And above all, it means treating vocational and technical education not as a consolation prize but as a first-class path, with real investment, real apprenticeships and real respect.

A young man who learns a skilled trade, an electrician, a welder, a machinist, a nurse, can earn a good living, stand on his own feet and offer a partner a stable future. We should make that path as visible and as honored as the path to a four-year degree.

[PAUSE]

Third, end the marriage penalties.

No American should lose health coverage, food assistance or a tax credit simply because they married the person they love. Congress should go through the tax code and the major benefit programs line by line and smooth out the cliffs that punish marriage at the bottom of the income ladder. This will cost some money. I believe it is worth every dollar. A government that wants stable families should at least stop charging people to form them.

[SLOW]

Fourth, build homes.

This is not a partisan idea. Cities run by both parties have made it too hard to build. Let people build more homes, more kinds of homes, closer to where the jobs are. Reform the zoning rules that forbid anything but large single-family houses on large lots. Speed up permits. Allow the duplex, the townhouse, the apartment over the garage. Every young couple that can afford a place of their own a few years earlier is a couple with a few more years to build a family.

[PAUSE]

Fifth, support families once they form.

I mentioned earlier that the usual pro-family policies are aimed at people who have already crossed the river. That does not make them useless. It makes them the second half of the bridge. A young couple deciding whether to marry and start a family looks ahead. If they see child care that costs as much as a mortgage, parental leave that does not exist, and a tax code that does little for parents, they hesitate. If they see a country that will meet them halfway, they are more likely to step forward. A generous child tax credit, help with child care, and paid leave for new parents tell young people that the nation is on their side.

[SLOW]

Sixth, rebuild the places where people meet.

This one sounds soft, but I believe it may be the most important of all. For generations, Americans met each other in what sociologists call third places: not home, not work, but somewhere in between. The church hall. The union hall. The bowling alley. The diner. The library. The park. The community dance. The volunteer fire company. The softball league.

Many of those places are gone or struggling. Governments can help, a little. Keep libraries open in the evenings. Fund parks and recreation leagues for adults, not just for children. Make it easy, not hard, to open a café or a music venue or a community space. Support national and community service programs that put young people of different backgrounds side by side, working on something real, as the Civilian Conservation Corps once did in Roosevelt's day.

[PAUSE]

And seventh, measure what matters.

Here is a small fact that tells you how little attention we pay to this. The federal government tracks unemployment every month, inflation every month, retail sales every month. But when you go to the CDC's own page on marriage and divorce, you find that its latest divorce figures cover only forty-five states and the District of Columbia, because not every state reports. We count every barrel of oil and every bushel of corn more carefully than we count the families our country is, or is not, forming.

[SLOW]

What a nation measures, it manages. I would like to see a regular, serious national report on how Americans are coupling: how many are dating, partnering, marrying, separating, and why. Not to pry into anyone's private life, but so that the people we elect cannot keep pretending this is not happening.

But the truth is that most of this cannot be done by government at all. It has to be done by us.

## XIII. What Only We Can Do

So now I want to speak to you directly. Not as citizens of a nation, but as parents, grandparents, aunts and uncles, friends, pastors, coaches, bosses and neighbors. And to the young people listening, as the ones who will cross this river or not.

[PAUSE]

To the older generation first.

Stop telling young people to wait.

For thirty years, the advice we have given our children has been some version of: finish your education, establish your career, see the world, find yourself, and then, someday, think about settling down. We meant well. We wanted them to have opportunities we did not have. But we sent a message, without quite meaning to, that marriage and children are things you do after life has given you everything else, rather than part of what makes life worth living.

[SLOW]

Tell them the truth instead. Tell them that the best things in your life were not your promotions. Tell them that marrying young and poor was hard and wonderful and that you would do it again. Or, if your marriage failed, tell them what you learned, and that you still believe in the idea. Tell them that children are exhausting and expensive and the greatest joy you have ever known. They will not hear it from their feeds. They have to hear it from you.

And do something even more old-fashioned. Introduce people.

There was a time when every community had its matchmakers. The aunt who knew everybody. The pastor who noticed that the new schoolteacher and the young mechanic both sat alone at the potluck. The friend who threw a dinner party and quietly seated two single people next to each other. That work was never glamorous, but it built more families than any app ever will.

[PAUSE]

Be that person. If you know a good young man and a good young woman who might like each other, say so. Throw the dinner. Make the introduction. Take the risk of being a little embarrassing. The worst that can happen is an awkward evening. The best that can happen is a grandchild you will never know you helped bring into the world.

[PAUSE]

Now, to the young men.

I know it is hard out there. I know the apps feel rigged, the schools did not fit you, the jobs are uncertain, and the culture sends you mixed messages about whether you are needed at all. I know you hear voices online telling you that women are the enemy, that the game is lost, that you should give up or get angry.

Do not listen to them.

[SLOW]

You are needed. Your strength is needed, your loyalty, your steadiness, your humor, your willingness to show up and keep showing up. But you have to bring it into the world, not into a screen. Get off the couch. Get the training. Take the job, even if it is not the job you dreamed of, and work your way up. Join something. A gym, a team, a church, a volunteer crew, a club. Be around people. And when you meet a woman you like, treat her with the kind of respect you would want a man to show your sister. Be kind. Be direct. Be brave enough to ask, and gracious enough to accept a no.

Women are not a prize you win by gaming a system. They are partners you earn by becoming a man worth partnering with. That is not a burden. It is the oldest adventure there is.

[PAUSE]

And to the young women.

I know you have been disappointed. I know you have dealt with men who ghosted you, pressured you, belittled you, or simply never grew up. I know you have worked hard to build a life you are proud of, and you are right not to settle for someone who would tear it down.

But I want to ask you, gently, to keep a door open.

[SLOW]

Some of the best men you will ever meet do not have impressive résumés. They may not have a degree. They may be an inch shorter than you would prefer. They may be awkward in a text message and wonderful at a kitchen table. They may be the quiet guy at work, the friend of a friend, the man in your running club who never quite worked up the nerve. Give him a second date. Look for character over credentials. Look for the man who will show up when your car breaks down at midnight and when your mother is in the hospital.

And if you find yourself thinking that politics makes a man impossible to love, ask yourself whether it is his values you object to, or the version of him your feed has taught you to expect.

[PAUSE]

And to all the young people, men and women both: put the phone down sometimes. Go where people are. Say yes to the invitation. Host the dinner yourself. Be the one who introduces your friends to each other. Ask someone out in person, the frightening, old-fashioned way. You will be rejected sometimes. Everyone always has been. Your grandparents were rejected. They tried again.

[LET IT LAND]

Courage, in the end, is what love has always required. The apps promised to make it safe. That promise was the problem. Love was never meant to be safe. It was meant to be worth it.

## XIV. Coenus Was Right

Let me go back, one last time, to Alexander.

I told you that at the Hyphasis he turned around. What I did not tell you is what he did on the way home, because it surprised me when I read it, and I think it belongs at the end of this long evening.

[PAUSE]

The march back was terrible. Part of the army went through the Gedrosian desert, and many died. But eventually they reached the Persian city of Susa. And there, Arrian tells us, Alexander did something that the historian called the most popular thing he ever did.

He held a wedding.

Not one wedding. Dozens. Alexander married, and he gave brides to about eighty of his Companions in a single ceremony. Seats were set in a row for the bridegrooms. After the banquet, the brides came in and each sat beside her husband. Each man took his bride by the right hand and kissed her, the king first. And Alexander gave every one of them a dowry.

Then he did something more. He ordered that the names of all the ordinary Macedonian soldiers who had married women in Asia be written down. There were more than ten thousand of them. And he gave every one of them a wedding gift.

[SLOW]

Now, I want to be honest about this story, as I have tried to be honest all night. Those marriages were political. They were meant to bind a conquering army to a conquered empire. And I doubt very much that the brides at Susa were asked what they wanted. That is not a model for anything we should do today, and I hold it up for a different reason.

I hold it up because even the greatest conqueror of the ancient world, at the end of the longest march in history, understood something we seem to have forgotten. When the fighting is over, people need homes. They need someone beside them. And a leader who wants a people to have a future does not only lead them into battle. He sits them down at a banquet, and he pays for the wedding.

[PAUSE]

Not long after, the army mutinied again, at a town called Opis, angry that Alexander was sending veterans home and bringing Persians into the ranks. And Alexander gave another speech. In it, he reminded the men of what he had done for them. He said he had celebrated their weddings with his own. He said the children of many of them would be kin to his own children. And he reminded them that he had paid off their debts, without asking too closely how they had run them up.

[LET IT LAND]

He paid for the weddings. He forgave the debts. He made the soldiers' children part of his own family.

My friends, I am not asking any president to forgive every loan or pay for every wedding. But I am asking us to remember that a nation's future is not built on monuments or markets alone. It is built in the small rooms where two people decide to share a life. And a wise people, and a wise government, clears the path to those rooms rather than blocking it.

[PAUSE]

So let me draw it all together.

We are the unmarried nation. One in four of our forty-year-olds has never married. Nearly four in ten of our prime-age adults have no spouse or partner at all. Nearly two-thirds of our young men call themselves single. Nearly a third of the youngest men went a whole year without intimacy. Our young women are moving one way in politics and our young men another. Our young people meet through a marketplace that drowns half of them and starves the other half. Our friendships have thinned. Our gathering places have emptied. Our homes cost too much. And, at the end of the line, the births keep falling, year after year.

[BUILD]

But hear me now.

None of this was decreed by fate. None of it is written in our genes. All of it was built, decision by decision, over the last few decades, by a society that did not notice what it was doing. And what was built can be rebuilt.

We can give childhood back to our children, and with it their ability to look a stranger in the eye.

We can lift up our boys without pulling down our girls.

We can stop charging the poor to marry.

We can build homes, and let the young have doors of their own.

We can stand behind young families when they form, so the second child is not a luxury.

We can reopen the halls, the parks, the leagues and the dinner tables where people have always found each other.

We can introduce people. We can tell the truth about what makes a life good. We can stop telling our children to wait.

[PAUSE]

And to our young people, I say what Alexander said to his weary army, and what I believe in my bones.

You have already crossed so much.

[BUILD]

You crossed a recession that greeted many of you as you came of age. You crossed a pandemic that stole your proms and your graduations and two years of your youth. You crossed tuition bills and rent bills and a job market that changes faster than anyone can keep up with. You crossed a flood of information and misinformation that no generation before you ever had to wade through. You have been told, from every direction, that the world is ending, that the future is closed, that your generation is the first to do worse than its parents.

And you are still here. Still working. Still hoping, even if you would not admit it out loud.

[SLOW]

So I am not asking you to cross a great river tonight. I am asking you to cross a small one. The one between you and the person across the room. The one between your apartment and the dinner party you were invited to. The one between the screen in your hand and the face in front of you.

[PAUSE]

And here is where I break with Alexander.

He said that for a brave man there is no end to labour except the labour itself. He believed glory was the goal. He wanted to march to the edge of the world.

But it was Coenus who was right. When the old general stood up in that silent tent and spoke of the soldiers' longing for their parents, their wives, their children and their homes, he was speaking for every human being who has ever lived. That longing is not weakness. It is not a lack of ambition. It is the deepest and most honorable desire we have. It is the reason all the other labour is worth doing.

The men at the Hyphasis wept for joy when they were told they could go home.

[LET IT LAND]

Our tragedy is that so many of our young people have marched to the edge of the world and found that there is no home to go back to. No one waiting at the door. No children running down the path.

Let us build that home for them. Let us build it together.

[PAUSE]

My friends, it is late, and I have kept you a long time. Franklin Roosevelt, ending that first talk with the American people in nineteen thirty-three, told them that the problem he had described was theirs as much as his, and that together they could not fail. I will end with something like that, in my own words.

This is not the government's problem alone, and it is not yours alone. It is ours. It lives at every Thanksgiving table with an empty chair. It lives in every apartment where a young man is gaming at three in the morning because he cannot face another empty evening. It lives in every phone where a young woman is scrolling through a hundred faces and seeing nobody. It lives in every grandparent who wonders, quietly, whether there will ever be a grandchild.

[SLOW]

And it can be answered. Not by force. Not by shame. Not by nostalgia for a past that was never as simple as we remember. It can be answered by courage, by kindness, by good laws and by better habits, by a people deciding, together, that the most important thing a free country can protect is the freedom to love and be loved, and to raise the next generation in a home of their own.

[BUILD]

So go home tonight. Call your son. Call your daughter. Call the friend you have been meaning to introduce to someone. Put the phone face down on the table at dinner. Say yes to the next invitation. Ask the question you have been afraid to ask.

Cross the river.

[PAUSE]

And next Thanksgiving, when you look down the table, may that empty chair be filled.

[LET IT LAND]

Good night, my friends. And God bless you all.
