# The Counterfeit God

## I. The Congregation of Bowed Heads

I want to begin in an airport.

Not a particular airport. You know the one I mean, because you have sat in it. The carpet that is meant to hide stains. The rows of chairs bolted together so that no one can move them closer or farther apart. The departure board that is always a little bit late. The smell of coffee and floor cleaner and a hundred strangers who have all been awake too long.

I was sitting in that room some years ago, waiting for a flight, and I looked up. That was the whole of my accomplishment that afternoon. I looked up.

[PAUSE]

And what I saw was a congregation.

Two hundred people, give or take, in a room built for waiting. And nearly every one of them was in the same posture. Head bowed. Shoulders curved forward. Hands cupped together in the lap, holding something small and glowing. The posture of prayer. The posture that human beings have adopted in front of the sacred for as long as there have been human beings.

A grandmother in a cardigan, head bowed. A businessman with his tie loosened, head bowed. Two teenagers sitting side by side, shoulder to shoulder, close enough to touch, each of them bowed over a separate light, each of them somewhere else. A young father with a toddler pulling at his trouser leg, and the father, head bowed, gently moving the small hand away without looking down.

I do not tell you this to sneer at them. I was one of them. I had my own glowing rectangle in my own two hands, and I had been bowed over it for the better part of an hour before I happened to raise my eyes. If I am about to preach to you tonight, understand that I am preaching first to the man in that chair.

But I want you to sit with what I saw, because I think it is the central image of our age.

Here were two hundred people, each of them carrying in their pocket the most powerful communication device ever built by our species. A machine that can carry your voice to the far side of the planet in less time than it takes to blink. A machine that can summon, in an instant, nearly every book ever written, every song ever recorded, every map ever drawn.

And not one of us could say a single word to the person in the next chair.

[LET IT LAND]

I could reach a stranger in Jakarta. I could argue with a stranger in Ohio. I could watch a stranger in Seoul cook dinner. But the woman two seats down, who might have been a doctor, or a widow, or someone flying home to a funeral, or someone who would have become my closest friend, was as unreachable to me as the far side of the moon. There was no channel for her. There was no door.

I remember thinking, how can this be? How can it be that we all hold these devices, and I can speak to no one around me?

That question has stayed with me. It has become, in a sense, the question of my working life. And tonight I want to give you my answer to it, at length, because I think the answer is not a technical one. I think it is a religious one.

I am going to make a claim that will sound extravagant at first, and I am going to spend the next two hours earning it.

The claim is this. What I saw in that airport was not two hundred people killing time. It was two hundred people at worship. They were devout. They were faithful. They were observing the central rite of the dominant religion of our civilization.

And that religion is a counterfeit.

[SLOW]

It is a counterfeit of something real. Something I believe is the deepest drive in a human being, perhaps the deepest drive in the universe itself. The feed did not invent our hunger. It found our hunger, and it learned to feed it with something that looks like bread and is not bread.

So tonight I want to do three things.

First, I want to name the religion. I want to show you that the word is not a metaphor. That by any honest measure of what a religion is, the feed is one, and that it may be the most widely practiced faith on Earth.

Second, I want to indict it. I want to lay out, as plainly as I can, what it takes from us and what it fails to give. I will bring evidence. I will bring the companies' own documents. I will bring a jury verdict handed down this very year. And I will bring the strongest arguments against my own case, because a sermon that cannot survive its critics is not worth your evening.

And third, I want to show you the way out. Because I do not believe this is our fate. I believe it is a detour. A place where a whole civilization has stopped on the road and mistaken the rest stop for the destination. And the road out, I will argue, runs through the oldest and simplest instruction in the moral history of our species.

Love thy neighbor.

Not the neighbor in your feed. The neighbor in the next chair.

[PAUSE]

Let us begin.

## II. What You Do Religiously

There is a sentence I have come to believe is one of the truest things anyone can say about human beings. It is very short.

Your religion is what you do religiously.

Not what you profess. Not the name on the building you visit at Easter, or the creed you were raised with, or the box you tick on a census form. Your religion is the thing you return to, faithfully, without being told. The thing you do first in the morning and last at night. The thing you do when you are frightened and the thing you do when you are bored. The thing that receives your attention, which is to say, the thing that receives your life.

By that measure, I want to ask you an uncomfortable question. What is your religion?

[PAUSE]

Let me describe a liturgy, and you tell me whether you recognize it.

The day begins before the eyes are fully open. A hand reaches out in the dark to the nightstand, and the first light the eyes receive is not the sun. It is the screen. Before you have spoken to the person lying next to you, before you have spoken to your children, before you have said a word of thanks or a word of anything to anyone in the room, you have checked in. You have consulted the oracle. You have asked: what happened while I slept? Who wants me? What do I need to know?

That is the morning office.

Then the day proceeds, and it is punctuated, the way a monk's day is punctuated by the bells. Except the bells do not ring at fixed hours. They ring whenever the machine decides to ring them. A buzz in the pocket. A banner on the screen. A small red circle with a number inside it, asking to be cleared. And each time the bell rings, the faithful stop what they are doing and turn toward it. In the middle of a conversation. In the middle of a meal. At a red light, which is the most dangerous chapel ever built.

There are the hours of waiting, which are the hours of devotion. The line at the pharmacy. The elevator. The bathroom. The ninety seconds while the kettle boils. Every gap in the day, every place where once a human being might have been alone with their own thoughts, or might have looked up and caught a stranger's eye, has been filled.

And then there is the evening office, which is the longest. The couch, the bed, the thumb moving upward in that small repeated gesture. Up. Up. Up. A gesture that has no natural ending, because the feed has no bottom. You do not finish it. You only stop, usually because you are too tired to continue, and often you do not stop even then.

[LET IT LAND]

If an anthropologist from another planet came to study us, what would she write in her notebook? She would not write that these people worship in their temples on the seventh day. She would write that these people carry a small shrine on their bodies at all times, that they touch it hundreds of times a day, that they consult it before any decision, that they become anxious and bereft when separated from it, and that they bow their heads before it in every public place.

She would write that this is their religion. And she would be right.

Let me give you some numbers, because I do not want you to take this on the strength of a metaphor.

In the summer of twenty twenty-three, Gallup surveyed more than fifteen hundred American teenagers about how they spend their time on social media. The average teenager reported four point eight hours a day. Nearly five hours. Every day. Half of all American teenagers, fifty-one percent, spent at least four hours a day.

And the devotion deepens with age. Thirteen-year-olds averaged about four hours. By seventeen, it was nearly six. Girls averaged five point three hours, boys four point four.

Sit with that for a moment. Nearly six hours a day, for a seventeen-year-old. That is longer than a full school day of instruction. That is more time than most of the great monastic orders in history ever asked their monks to spend in formal prayer.

[PAUSE]

The Pew Research Center asked a simpler question in the fall of twenty twenty-four. They asked teenagers how often they are online. Nearly half said: almost constantly.

Almost constantly. Think about the theological weight of that phrase. The mystics of every tradition have written about the dream of unceasing prayer. The Desert Fathers longed for it. Monks in the Eastern church repeated a single short prayer with every breath, hoping to make their whole life one continuous act of devotion. It was considered the summit of the spiritual life, reached by a very few after decades of discipline.

We have achieved it. Our children have achieved it. Almost constantly. Except the prayer is not to God. It is to the feed.

It is worth remembering that the great religions understood this danger long before there was any such thing as a screen. They understood that the things which fill our days will, if we let them, fill our souls. And so they built walls around time.

The Sabbath is the oldest of these walls. One day in seven when the work stops, the buying and selling stop, and the people turn instead toward God, toward family, toward rest. It was a radical idea in the ancient world, a day on which even servants and animals were to rest. Other traditions built their own walls: the fixed hours of prayer, the seasons of fasting, the monastic rule that divided the day between labor, study and silence.

What all of these share is a single insight. Attention is the substance of a life, and if you do not decide where it goes, something else will decide for you. The traditions did not leave that decision to appetite. They gave it a shape.

The feed has no Sabbath. It has no hours of rest, no seasons of fasting, no rule. It is open every hour of every day, and it is engineered to fill whatever space you leave it. In that sense it is not merely a religion. It is a religion without a single wall around its time. A faith that demands everything and never tells you that you may stop.

Now, I want to be careful. I am not saying that every minute on a phone is an act of idolatry. Some of those hours are a teenager talking to a grandmother on a video call. Some of them are a young man learning to fix his car from a stranger who was kind enough to film it. Some of them are real connection, real learning, real good. I will come back to that, because it matters more than anything else I will say tonight.

But I am saying that the shape of the practice is religious. The regularity. The ritual. The posture. The anxiety when it is interrupted. The way it orders the day. The way it receives the first and last hours of our waking life, the hours that every faith on Earth has reserved for the sacred.

And I am saying something more than that. I am saying that we should stop pretending it is not real.

[BUILD]

There is a way of talking about all this that I find very unhelpful. People say: get off your phone and go live in the real world. As if the phone were a dream and the sidewalk were reality. As if the feed were a kind of fiction we could simply set down.

But the feed is not unreal. It is entirely real. It is where the attention of a civilization actually goes. It is where opinions are formed and elections are fought and marriages are strained and teenagers learn what their bodies are supposed to look like. It is where a very large share of the waking hours of the human race are spent. You cannot call a thing unreal when it receives five hours of every day of a child's life.

No. The feed is real the way a religion is real. Real the way a temple is real, the way a ritual is real, the way devotion is real. And that is precisely why it is dangerous. Because the question to ask of any religion is never whether it is real. The question is: what god does it serve? And what does that god do with your devotion?

[SLOW]

That is the question I want to put to the religion of the feed. Not, is it real. But: what does it give back?

## III. What to the Lonely Is Your Feed?

In the summer of eighteen fifty-two, in Rochester, New York, a man stood up in Corinthian Hall to give a speech for the Fourth of July.

He had been invited by the Rochester Ladies' Anti-Slavery Society. He chose to speak not on the Fourth but on the fifth. And the audience who had paid their twelve and a half cents expected, perhaps, the usual thing. Praise for the founders. Praise for liberty. A warm hour of national self-congratulation.

The man was Frederick Douglass. He had been born into slavery. He had taught himself to read in defiance of the law. He had escaped. And he did not give them the usual thing.

He began graciously enough. He praised the founders, and meant it. He praised the principles of the Declaration, and meant that too. And then, having built them up, he turned. He asked them what this day of theirs could possibly mean to a man still held in chains. He told them that their celebration, which was joy to them, was to the enslaved a reminder of the injustice done to them every day. He told them, in effect: this day is yours, not mine. You may rejoice. I must mourn.

And then he said something I have never forgotten.

"At a time like this, scorching irony, not convincing argument, is needed."

[PAUSE]

Now. I am not comparing the condition of a person enslaved in eighteen fifty-two to the condition of a teenager on a smartphone in twenty twenty-six. They are not the same. Nothing is the same as that. I want to be absolutely clear about that, because comparisons of that kind are cheap and they insult the dead.

What I am borrowing from Douglass is not his subject. It is his method. The method of the prophet. You stand in the middle of a celebration, and you ask the celebrants to look at what their celebration costs. You hold the professed ideals of a society up against its practice, and you let the distance between them speak.

Douglass understood that sometimes a people have already heard every argument. They know the facts. The facts have been in the newspapers for years. What they lack is not information. What they lack is the feeling of the thing. And so the prophet's job is not to inform. It is to make the comfortable feel the weight of what they already know.

So let me try, in my own small way, to do the same.

[BUILD]

What, to the lonely, is your feed?

You tell me it is connection. You tell me that it has brought the world together, that no one need ever be alone again, that every human being is now only a click away from every other. You throw a party every quarter when the user numbers come in. You announce the billions of daily active people with the pride of a nation announcing its victories.

And the lonely look at your celebration, and they ask: connected to whom?

Because in the same years that you have counted your billions, the Surgeon General of the United States has declared an epidemic of loneliness. In May of twenty twenty-three, he issued an advisory warning that about half of American adults report experiencing loneliness. He said that the health risk of being socially disconnected is as deadly as smoking up to fifteen cigarettes a day.

Fifteen cigarettes a day. In the most connected society in the history of the world.

What, to the lonely, is your feed?

It is a window into a party they were not invited to. It is the photographs of other people's dinners, other people's weddings, other people's beaches, curated and filtered and arranged so that their own life looks small and grey beside it. It is a thousand acquaintances and no one to call at two in the morning. It is a place to perform friendship rather than a place to have it.

[PAUSE]

What, to the teenage girl, is your feed?

You tell her it is self-expression. You tell her it is a stage where she can be anyone, find her people, build her brand. And in a company slide written by your own researchers, which a newspaper published to the world in twenty twenty-one, you told yourselves something else. You told yourselves, in plain words: we make body image issues worse for one in three teen girls.

You knew. You wrote it down. And the feed kept running.

What, to the child, is your feed?

You tell the child it is play. Endless play. Video after video after video, each one chosen more precisely than the last, a toy box with no bottom. And the child does not know, cannot know, that every one of those videos was chosen not because it would make her happy or wise or kind, but because it would keep her watching. That on the other side of the screen sits the most sophisticated persuasion system ever constructed, and it is pointed at her, and its only instruction is: do not let her leave.

[LET IT LAND]

What, to the family at dinner, is your feed?

It is the fourth guest at a table of three, the one who interrupts every conversation and never apologizes. It is the reason the father did not hear what his son said about school. It is the reason the son stopped trying to say it.

What, to the old man in the nursing home, is your feed?

It is the reason his grandchildren visit with their heads bowed. They came. They sat with him. They were physically in the room. And they were not there.

[BUILD]

I say to you, as Douglass said to his audience, that I see this celebration. I see the conferences and the keynotes, the product launches with their dramatic music, the language of community and connection and bringing the world closer together. I see the trillion-dollar valuations raised up like temples. And I say that underneath all that celebration there is a sound you are not meant to hear. It is the sound of a civilization that has never been more connected, and has never been more alone.

Your connection is a performance. Your community is a metric. Your engagement is a harvest. And the crop is us.

[PAUSE]

[SLOW]

Now. Having said all of that, having let the irony burn as hot as I can make it, I must do something that the prophets did not always do. I must be fair.

Because I have not yet told you what I believe. And what I believe is going to surprise some of you, because it is not what most critics of technology believe.

I do not believe that connection is bad. I do not believe that information is bad. I do not believe that the drive to plug into the world, to know what is happening, to reach for more and more knowledge, is a sickness.

I believe it is the most sacred drive we have.

And that is exactly why its counterfeit is so dangerous.

## IV. The Will To God

I need to tell you how I came to see the world, because the indictment I am bringing tonight only makes sense from inside it.

When I was in college, I read a great deal of philosophy. More than was healthy, probably. And one night, as I was falling asleep, four words arrived in my mind, in a particular order, as if they were a summary of everything I had read.

The Will To God.

I remember being startled by them. The first thing I did, of course, was search to see who had said it before me. I could not find anyone who had used it in the sense I meant. And so began years of turning those words over, asking what it would mean if they were true. Or if not true in the strict sense, at least a fair way of describing what it feels like to be alive.

[PAUSE]

Here is the idea, as simply as I can put it.

Nietzsche famously spoke of a will to power. I came to believe that underneath the will to power is something deeper. A will toward a particular state of being. A state in which we would know everything, and through that knowing, would endure. Would not die. Would be, in the oldest sense of the word, immortal.

I call that state God. Not a bearded figure on a throne. Not a being outside the universe. I mean something more like a destination. The absolute objective of existence. The moment at which knowledge and endurance become one. And I believe that every living thing, and perhaps the universe itself, is reaching toward it.

Let me make that concrete. Think about what you find good, at the most basic level.

Breathing is good. You do not need to be told that. When the air is cut off, every cell of your body screams. Breathing sustains you in time.

Eating is good. Drinking water is good. They sustain you.

Raising children is good, in a way that goes beyond what any argument can capture. When you look into the eyes of your child, you are looking at your own continuation. Something of you will go on after you are gone. We glimpse a kind of immortality in the faces of our children.

And then there are the higher goods. Science, which extends our lives and our understanding. Medicine, which pushes back death. The labor we do to feed and house the people we love. The dream, which some of us have, of carrying human life out among the stars, so that our species might endure for hundreds of thousands of years.

What do all of these have in common? They are all, in one way or another, ways of knowing more and enduring longer. Knowledge joined to sustainment. That is the pattern I see in everything we call good.

[SLOW]

I should be honest with you about the status of this idea, because I would rather lose your agreement than your trust.

I have sometimes described it with a picture borrowed from the way artificial intelligence is trained. An optimizer, descending step by step toward a target, adjusting itself a little at a time. I have imagined that God, as the absolute future, sends something like a signal backward through time, the way a training algorithm sends a correction backward through a network, shaping us moment by moment toward the good.

That is an image. It is not a mechanism I can demonstrate. I offer it as a picture of how it feels, not as a claim about the physics. And when I have said that physics seems to point the same way, that the universe began with everything entangled and that faint correlations among all things never entirely disappear, I mean that as analogy more than argument. Suggestive, not proof. I hold the large cosmic parts of this picture loosely. There are parts of it I find beautiful and cannot yet defend.

But the part I want to stand on tonight is not the cosmic part. It is the human part. And the human part, I think, is very hard to deny.

[BUILD]

Human beings are creatures who reach for knowledge. We cannot help it. Put a child in a new room, and she will touch every object in it. Tell an adult there is a secret, and he will not rest until he learns it. We crane our necks at car accidents. We read the last page of the novel first. We stay up too late on the internet because there is always one more thing to know.

And this is not a defect. It is the engine of everything we have built. The same hunger that makes us scroll made us map the coastlines and split the atom and sequence the genome. It made the printing press. It made the telescope. It made the very network that carries the feed.

I have a phrase for the feeling of our age. I call it the Esketian World-Feeling, from an old phrase, let's get it, and it can be summed up in a single rule. Everything will come to know everything.

You can see it everywhere. Search engines. Prediction markets. Recommendation systems. Surveillance, which is the dark side of the same thing. And now the great machines of artificial intelligence, which take in the written record of our species and give it back on demand. This year, for the first time, Americans are spending more to build data centers than to build general office space. According to Census figures published this summer, data center construction ran at an annual rate of fifty billion dollars, ahead of general offices. We are building temples to knowledge faster than we are building places to work together.

I do not say that with alarm. I say it with something closer to awe. I believe the drive to connect, the drive to know, the drive to bring every mind into contact with every other, is the Will To God operating as it should.

[LET IT LAND]

And so you can see why I cannot join the people who say the problem is that we know too much, or connect too much, or that we should all throw our phones in a lake and go back to some imagined age of innocence.

No. The exchange of information between human beings is good. That is my whole thesis. I do not retreat from it.

The problem is not that we reach for God.

The problem is that someone has learned to sell us an idol instead.

## V. The Counterfeit

What is a counterfeit?

A counterfeit is not the opposite of the real thing. That is what makes it dangerous. A counterfeit twenty-dollar bill is not a leaf or a stone. It is printed on paper that feels almost right, in ink that looks almost right, with a face that looks almost right. It is designed to pass. It is designed to be accepted in exactly the places where the real thing would be accepted.

And the harm it does is not only that it is worthless. The harm is that it occupies the place of the real thing. The shopkeeper who takes a counterfeit bill has given away real goods in exchange for nothing. And if enough counterfeits circulate, people stop trusting money at all.

[PAUSE]

The feed is a counterfeit of the Will To God.

It is designed to be accepted in exactly the place where the real thing belongs. It speaks directly to the hunger I just described, the hunger to know, to be in contact, to be up to date with everything the world can hand us. When you pick up your phone and open the feed, it feels like reaching for the absolute. It feels like plugging into the whole of human knowledge, the great current of everything that is happening.

And what it delivers is something else. It delivers fragments. A joke. An outrage. A stranger's lunch. A clip of a fight. An advertisement disguised as a friend. Another outrage. A celebrity. A war, briefly, between two dances. Another advertisement. It delivers what I can only call base phenomena. Sensation without substance. Stimulation without nourishment.

You come away, after an hour, with a strange feeling. You have taken in a staggering amount of information. And you know nothing. You could not tell anyone what you learned. You feel full and empty at the same time, the way you feel after eating a whole bag of something that was engineered to be impossible to stop eating.

[SLOW]

Let me be precise about why I call this a counterfeit and not simply a bad habit.

Remember my definition. God, as I understand it, is the state in which we know everything and, through that knowing, endure. Knowledge joined to sustainment. Both halves together. Not one without the other.

Now look at what the feed does. It offers you a simulation of the first half, the knowing. And it pays for that simulation by quietly consuming the second half, the sustainment.

It consumes your hours. Hours you will never get back, from a life that has a fixed number of them.

It consumes your attention, which is the faculty you would need to learn anything real.

It consumes your sleep, the most basic act of bodily sustainment there is, as the screen glows past midnight.

It consumes your health, as the body sits still and the mind races.

And above all, it consumes your neighbors. The people actually near you. The ones who could sustain you in a crisis, bring you soup when you are sick, help you move, sit with you when your father dies. Every hour spent in the feed is an hour not spent with them. And over years, the relationships that would have sustained you simply do not form.

[LET IT LAND]

Knowledge without sustainment. That is the counterfeit.

It is the shape of the thing, and you can see it once you know to look. Something that offers you the feeling of reaching toward the absolute while taking away the very things that would let you endure.

That is why I use a strong word for it. I call it demonic. And I want to be careful about that word, because I do not mean it as an insult to anyone's faith, and I do not mean that there are little horned creatures living inside your phone.

I mean it in a precise sense. A god, in every tradition I know of, is something that gives back. You offer your devotion, and something is returned. Meaning. Strength. Community. Bread for the hungry. Comfort for those who mourn. A demonic religion, in the sense I mean, is one that takes and gives nothing back. It hypnotizes. It lies. It distracts. And then it names the trance connection.

That is the precise shape of the feed. It takes your hours and gives you nothing you can keep.

[BUILD]

There is an old teaching that I find more relevant every year. It comes from the Sermon on the Mount, and it goes like this, in the old translation.

"Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth, where moth and rust doth corrupt, and where thieves break through and steal."

And then: "For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also."

Where your treasure is, there your heart will be. Now ask yourself honestly: where is your treasure? I do not mean your money. I mean the thing you spend that is more precious than money. Your time. Your attention. The hours of your one life.

If the honest answer is that four or five or six hours of every day go into the feed, then I am afraid the teaching tells us where our heart is. And it tells us something else, too. It tells us that this is a treasure that thieves break through and steal. Because that is exactly what is happening. Your attention is being harvested and sold. You are not the customer of the feed. You are the field it is planted in.

[PAUSE]

The same sermon says: no one can serve two masters. You cannot serve God and money.

I would put it this way for our time. You cannot serve the Will To God and the feed. You cannot reach for real knowledge and real endurance with the same hours you hand over to the counterfeit. One of them will get your heart. And for a great many of us, if we are honest, we already know which one it is.

## VI. The Ledger

I have made strong claims. Let me now bring the evidence, because a prophet without evidence is only a man shouting.

I want to approach this the way an economist would, because I think economics gives us the clearest language for what has gone wrong. And the key word is one you may remember from a class you took long ago.

Externality.

An externality is a cost that is real, that is borne by somebody, but that appears on no invoice. The factory that pours its waste into the river sells its product at a price that does not include the dead fish downstream. The people who live by the river pay that cost. But the buyer never sees it, and the factory never pays it, and so the market keeps making more.

[PAUSE]

We have seen this shape of error before. Cigarettes were sold for decades at a price that did not include the cancer wards. Fossil fuels have been sold at a price that does not include the heat. And in each case, what is striking is that the product being sold is genuine. Cigarettes really did deliver stimulation and calm. Fossil fuels really do deliver energy, enormous amounts of it, and they built the modern world. The good is real. It is the damage that is hidden.

Social media is the same shape of error. The good being sold is real. Connection is real. Information is real. And the damage, the distrust, the depression, the anxiety, the loneliness, the general sense of misery, is quietly offloaded onto the buyer and onto everyone standing near them. And no price ever captures it.

I believe it is one of the great mispriced externalities of our era.

[BUILD]

So let us look at the ledger. Let us write down the costs that never appeared on the invoice.

Item one. In September of twenty twenty-one, the Wall Street Journal began publishing a series of reports built on internal documents from Facebook. The company we now call Meta. Among those documents was research the company had conducted on its own product, Instagram. And among that research was a slide, from twenty nineteen, with a sentence that has since been quoted around the world. We make body image issues worse for one in three teen girls.

Another internal slide reported that thirty-two percent of teen girls said that when they felt bad about their bodies, Instagram made them feel worse.

I want you to notice what kind of evidence this is. This is not an activist with an agenda. This is not a journalist guessing. This is the company's own researchers, studying the company's own product, and writing their findings down for the company's own leadership.

Item two. In October of twenty twenty-one, a former Facebook product manager named Frances Haugen, who had brought tens of thousands of those internal documents out of the company, testified before a subcommittee of the United States Senate. Her message, in summary, was that the company knew how to make its products safer and chose not to, because the changes would cost engagement and engagement was revenue. She said that the company had put its astronomical profits before people.

[PAUSE]

Item three. In May of twenty twenty-three, as I mentioned, the Surgeon General of the United States, Dr. Vivek Murthy, issued an advisory on what he called an epidemic of loneliness and isolation. He reported that young people between fifteen and twenty-four spent seventy percent less time with friends than the same age group had two decades earlier. The advisory described time spent in person with friends falling from about an hour a day to about twenty minutes. He said that social disconnection raises the risk of premature death by nearly thirty percent.

And he said a sentence that I would carve over the door of every technology company if I could. "There's really no substitute for in-person interaction."

Item four. In June of twenty twenty-four, the same Surgeon General went further. In an essay in the New York Times, he called on Congress to require a warning label on social media platforms, the way we put warning labels on packs of cigarettes. He cited research finding that adolescents who spend more than three hours a day on social media face double the risk of symptoms of anxiety and depression. I want to be honest about what that finding is. It is an association. It does not prove that the hours cause the symptoms. But it was strong enough, and consistent enough with everything else he was seeing, that the nation's doctor said the product should carry a warning.

[LET IT LAND]

Item five. The American Time Use Survey, which the federal government has conducted every year since two thousand and three, asks Americans to account for their days. According to an analysis of that survey in The Atlantic, Americans in two thousand and three spent about forty-seven minutes a day visiting friends or attending or hosting social gatherings. By twenty twenty-three, it was thirty-four minutes. The decline was even steeper among the young.

Think of what those lost minutes represent. Not abstractions. Dinner parties that were never thrown. Friends who were never visited. Neighbors whose names were never learned. The ordinary, unglamorous, sustaining traffic of human life, quietly drained away, a few minutes a day, year after year.

[BUILD]

Item six. And this is the one that changed the conversation this year.

On the twenty-fifth of March, twenty twenty-six, in a courtroom in Los Angeles, a jury returned a verdict in the first trial of its kind. The plaintiff was a twenty-year-old woman, identified in court by her initials, who testified that she had become addicted to social media as a child and that the addiction had deepened her struggles with her mental health.

She had sued four companies. Two of them, TikTok and Snap, settled before trial. The other two, Meta and YouTube, went before the jury.

And the jury found them negligent. They found that each company's negligence was a substantial factor in the harm done to her. They found that the companies knew their platforms could be dangerous to minors and failed to adequately warn of it. They awarded three million dollars in damages, and then three million more in punitive damages, which a jury may only award when it finds something like malice, oppression or fraud.

The jurors were instructed that they could not hold the companies responsible for the content of the posts and the videos. The law shields that. What they could consider was the design. And the design features that the plaintiff's lawyers put before them were these: the infinite feed, the autoplay, the notifications.

The bottomless scroll. The video that starts before you have decided to watch it. The bell that rings to call you back.

[SLOW]

One of the jurors said afterward: "We wanted them to feel it."

The companies disagreed with the verdict. Meta said that teen mental health is profoundly complex and cannot be linked to a single app. Google said it would appeal. That is their right, and the courts will have their say. And the day before that verdict, a jury in New Mexico had returned a verdict of three hundred and seventy-five million dollars against Meta under that state's consumer protection law, over the safety of children on its platforms.

There are, by one expert's count, thousands of similar cases still waiting.

[PAUSE]

I want to be careful here. A jury verdict is not a law of nature. Six million dollars is a rounding error to companies of that size. And the science, as I will discuss in a moment, is contested.

But notice what has happened. For nearly two decades, the costs of the feed sat off the books. They were borne by teenage girls and lonely men and worried parents and overwhelmed school counselors, and they appeared on no invoice anywhere. And this year, for the first time, twelve ordinary citizens looked at the ledger and wrote a number in it.

That is what a prophet means when he says the day of reckoning is coming. Not fire from the sky. A line in the ledger. The cost, finally, appearing on the invoice.

## VII. You Have Heard It Said

In the Sermon on the Mount there is a passage built on a pattern that repeats again and again. You have heard that it was said, the preacher tells the crowd, and he names an old teaching. But I say unto you, and he names a deeper one.

You have heard that it was said, thou shalt not kill. But I say unto you that whoever is angry with his brother is in danger of judgment. The teaching does not abolish the old law. It drives it deeper, from the action down to the heart.

I want to borrow that pattern now, because there are a number of things we have all heard said about the feed. And I think each of them needs to be driven deeper.

[PAUSE]

You have heard it said that the feed is free.

But I say unto you that nothing has ever cost you more.

You do not pay in dollars. You pay in the only currency that cannot be earned back. You pay in hours. In attention. In the first thoughts of the morning and the last thoughts of the night. The old saying is that if you are not paying for the product, you are the product. I would go further. You are not the product. You are the raw material. You are the forest that is felled and the ore that is mined. Your attention is extracted, refined into data, and sold to the highest bidder. And the thing about a mine is that it does not care what happens to the mountain.

[PAUSE]

You have heard it said that the feed gives people what they want.

But I say unto you that it gives people what they cannot stop wanting, which is a very different thing.

There is a difference between what you want and what you want to want. At two in the morning, when you are still scrolling, some part of you wants to keep going, or you would stop. But there is another part of you, the part that will wake up tired, the part that has things it meant to do with its life, that wants very much to put the phone down. The feed is built to speak to the first part and to silence the second. It is optimized not for your considered desire but for your weakest moment. And a system that always serves your weakest moment is not serving you. It is serving on you.

[BUILD]

You have heard it said that the feed brings the world closer together.

But I say unto you that it has brought the far near and made the near far.

It has brought the celebrity into your bedroom and pushed your neighbor out of your life. It has made you an expert on the private lives of strangers and a stranger to the private lives of those you love. You know what a man in another country ate for breakfast. You do not know the name of the woman who lives across the hall. That is not closeness. That is a rearrangement of distance, and it has been rearranged in exactly the wrong direction.

[PAUSE]

You have heard it said that it is just entertainment, and that people have always had their distractions. Television, radio, the penny novel, the music hall.

But I say unto you that no distraction in history has ever watched you back.

The television did not know you. The penny novel did not learn which page made you linger. The radio did not adjust its programming, in real time, for you alone, based on the half-second hesitation of your thumb. The feed does all of these things. It is not a broadcast. It is a conversation in which only one side knows what is being said. Behind every screen is a system that has studied billions of people to learn what holds them, and it is applying that knowledge to you, specifically, now. To compare that to a paperback is to compare a fishing net to the ocean.

[LET IT LAND]

You have heard it said that people choose to use it. That they are adults. That no one forces anyone to scroll.

But I say unto you that a choice made against a machine designed to defeat it is only half a choice.

Yes, you choose. I choose. I am not going to pretend we are puppets, because that would be its own kind of lie, and because the whole point of what I am going to tell you later is that we can choose differently. But let us at least be honest about the contest. On one side, a tired person at the end of a long day. On the other side, thousands of the most talented engineers in the world, armed with more data about human behavior than has ever been gathered, whose performance is measured by how long that tired person stays. That is not a fair fight. We do not let casinos advertise to children. We do not let cigarette companies hand out samples at schools. We have always understood that some contests are so lopsided that the stronger party bears a special duty.

[BUILD]

You have heard it said that this is the price of progress, and that every new technology frightens the old.

But I say unto you that the question is never whether a thing is new. The question is whether it bears good fruit.

There is another line in that same sermon. "Ye shall know them by their fruits. Do men gather grapes of thorns, or figs of thistles?"

By their fruits. Not by their promises. Not by their keynotes. Not by their mission statements, which all speak of community and connection and bringing the world together. By their fruits.

And what are the fruits? A generation of girls who were told, by the company's own research, that the product made them feel worse about their bodies. A population that spends roughly a quarter less time with its friends than it did twenty years ago. A Surgeon General calling for warning labels. A jury finding negligence. A loneliness so widespread that the nation's doctor compared it to a pack of cigarettes a day.

[SLOW]

Those are the fruits. You may draw your own conclusions about the tree.

## VIII. The Strongest Objection

Now I must do what I promised. I must give the other side its best day in court.

Because there is a serious case against what I have been saying. It is not made by fools or by shills. Some of the most careful researchers in the world have made it, and if I do not answer it honestly, then you should not trust a word I have said tonight.

[PAUSE]

The case goes something like this.

First, correlation is not causation. Yes, the teenagers who spend the most time on social media report more anxiety and depression. But perhaps it runs the other way. Perhaps the anxious and depressed teenager retreats into the phone, because the phone is easier than the world. Perhaps the loneliness came first and the scrolling is its symptom, not its cause.

When the psychologist Jonathan Haidt published his book The Anxious Generation in twenty twenty-four, arguing that smartphones and social media rewired childhood in the early twenty-tens and set off a crisis in adolescent mental health, it was a bestseller. And it was sharply criticized. The developmental psychologist Candice Odgers, writing in the journal Nature, argued that most studies do not show a large and consistent harm, that reverse causation is a real possibility, and that there are no simple answers. Others accused the book of feeding a moral panic, the same kind of panic that greeted comic books and rock music and video games.

Second, the decline of community did not begin with the smartphone. The political scientist Robert Putnam published Bowling Alone in two thousand, building on an essay from nineteen ninety-five, years before there was any such thing as a social feed. He documented a long withdrawal of Americans from clubs, leagues, churches and civic associations that had been underway since the middle of the twentieth century. He blamed generational change, and he blamed television. The loneliness, in other words, is older than the feed.

Third, for many people the internet is a lifeline. The gay teenager in a small town who finds, online, the first people who understand him. The new mother awake at four in the morning who finds other new mothers awake at four in the morning. The man with a rare disease who finds the only twenty other people on Earth who share it. To these people, the sermon I am preaching may sound like a privileged person telling them to give up the only community they have.

[LET IT LAND]

These are serious arguments. Let me answer them one at a time.

On correlation and causation. The critics are right that the research on mental health outcomes is contested, and I will not stand here and pretend it is settled science. It is not. I have tried tonight to describe the strongest findings as associations, because that is what they are.

But notice what my argument actually rests on. It does not rest on any single study of depression scores. It rests on something simpler and much harder to dispute. Time.

There are twenty-four hours in a day. If a teenager spends nearly five of them on social media, those five hours came from somewhere. They came from sleep, or from friends, or from family, or from play, or from solitude, or from boredom, which is where imagination is born. You do not need a randomized trial to know that an hour spent in the feed is an hour not spent somewhere else. That is not psychology. That is arithmetic.

And the companies' own research, and the internal documents, and the design features that a jury found negligent, all point in the same direction. The feed is not built to be a neutral tool that anxious people happen to use. It is built to hold attention for as long as possible. Even if every outcome study came back inconclusive tomorrow, that design goal would still be a theft of hours. The question of exactly how much damage those stolen hours do is open. The question of whether they are stolen is not.

[BUILD]

On Putnam, and the idea that the decline is older than the feed. I agree, completely. And I think it makes my case stronger, not weaker.

Putnam's culprit was television, the first great machine for keeping people at home, alone, staring at a glowing screen. What the feed did was take that machine, make it portable, make it personal, make it interactive, and make it learn. The loneliness was already growing. The feed found it, and fed it, and monetized it. It is the difference between a slow leak and someone drilling a second hole in the bottom of the boat.

And here is the deeper point. If the decline of community is older than the smartphone, then simply putting down the smartphone will not restore it. We will have to rebuild something that was already eroding before the feed arrived. That is exactly what I am going to argue for at the end of tonight. Not a return to some golden age, but the deliberate construction of local, embodied community, using every tool we have, including technology.

[PAUSE]

And on the lifeline argument. The lonely teenager who finds his people online. The new mother at four in the morning.

I say: yes. Thank God for that. That is the real thing. That is the Will To God working the way it should. Human beings finding each other, exchanging what they know, sustaining each other through a hard night. I have no quarrel with any of it. That is connection, and connection is good.

But look carefully at what saves those people. It is not the infinite scroll. It is not the autoplay. It is not the algorithm that serves the lonely teenager an endless sequence of outrage and envy and beauty he can never attain. What saves him is finding a few specific human beings who know his name and care whether he lives. Which is to say, what saves him is a neighbor. A neighbor in a different place, perhaps. But a neighbor all the same.

[SLOW]

The good of the internet is the good of the neighbor. The counterfeit is everything the business model has wrapped around the neighbor to keep you scrolling past him.

There is one more objection I want to face, because I hear it often, and it deserves a hearing. It is the objection of freedom.

Who are you, the objection goes, to tell adults how to spend their evenings? Is this not just one more scold, one more moralist who has decided that his pleasures are noble and other people's are degraded? People once said the same thing about novels, about jazz, about dancing. Let people scroll if they want to scroll. It is their life.

I take this seriously, because I am, at heart, someone who believes in freedom and in markets. I do not want a government deciding what I may watch. I do not want a committee of experts rationing my screen time. And I do not want to be the kind of preacher who mistakes his own tastes for the moral law.

But freedom, properly understood, is not the absence of influence. It is the presence of a fair chance to choose. And what I have been describing tonight is not a free market in attention. It is a market with a hidden cost and a lopsided contest. When one party to an exchange knows everything about the other, can predict and shape the other's behavior, and bears none of the costs of the harm it does, that is not freedom working. That is freedom failing.

The remedies I am asking for are, almost all of them, remedies that expand freedom rather than restrict it. Put the cost on the invoice, so that prices tell the truth. Make the design honest, so that people can see what is being done to them. Protect children, who have never been considered fully free to contract, in any market, in any age. And then build better alternatives, and let people choose them.

I am not asking anyone to ban the feed for adults. I am asking us to stop pretending that the present arrangement is what freedom looks like.

[PAUSE]

So I will grant the critics everything they are owed. The science is unsettled. The decline is old. The lifeline is real. And having granted all of that, I still stand where I stood. The feed, as it is built and sold today, takes more than it gives. And we can do better.

## IX. The Local Minimum

There is a question my own philosophy leaves open, and I want to face it directly.

If the universe is reaching toward the good, if there is a Will To God at work in all of us, then why would such a universe produce counterfeits at all? Why would beings who hunger for real knowledge and real endurance settle, in their billions, for a glowing substitute that gives them neither?

I think the answer comes, oddly enough, from the same field that has given us the machines of our age.

[PAUSE]

When an artificial intelligence is trained, it learns by a process that can be pictured as walking downhill in the dark. Imagine a landscape of hills and valleys. Somewhere in that landscape is the lowest point, the true best answer. The machine cannot see the whole landscape. It can only feel the slope under its feet. So at each step, it moves a little bit downhill. And step by step, it descends.

Most of the time, this works beautifully. But there is a famous trap. Sometimes the machine walks down into a small valley, a hollow in the hillside, that is not the lowest point in the landscape. It is only the lowest point nearby. And once it is in that hollow, every direction feels like uphill. Every step it could take feels like getting worse. And so it stops. It settles. It believes, insofar as a machine believes anything, that it has arrived.

The engineers call this a local minimum.

[LET IT LAND]

I believe the feed is a local minimum. And I believe a whole civilization has settled into it.

Think about how it feels from the inside. Every time you pick up the phone, you get a small reward. A little hit of novelty. A message. A like. A laugh. It feels like progress. It feels like movement toward something. Each individual step feels like going downhill, in the good sense, toward relief, toward satisfaction, toward knowing a little more.

And every step away from it feels like uphill. Putting down the phone feels like loss. Sitting in silence feels like deprivation. Walking across the room to talk to a stranger feels frightening and effortful. Going to a community meeting, joining a club, inviting the neighbors to dinner, all of these feel like climbing a hill, because they are. They cost effort. They risk awkwardness. They pay off only later, sometimes much later.

And so we stay in the hollow. Not because we are stupid. Not because we are wicked. But because, from inside the hollow, every road out looks like it is going the wrong way.

[BUILD]

Let me make it concrete with a picture. Not a true story about any one family, but a scene I suspect is happening in a great many kitchens tonight.

A mother comes home from work. She is tired in the way that only a full day of other people's demands can make you tired. Her teenage son is at the kitchen table, head bowed over his phone. She thinks about sitting down across from him and asking about his day. She knows how it will go. He will give one-word answers. It will be awkward. It might turn into an argument about his grades. It will take effort she does not feel she has.

So she picks up her own phone instead. Just for a minute. And in that minute, she gets a small, reliable reward. A friend's photo. A funny clip. A headline that makes her angry in a satisfying way. Her son, across the table, is getting the same thing from his own screen. And the two of them sit there, three feet apart, each in a separate hollow, each choosing the small sure reward over the uncertain, effortful, potentially wonderful thing that might have happened if one of them had looked up.

Nobody in that kitchen is a bad person. Nobody made a terrible decision. Each of them, at each moment, took the step that felt like going downhill. And that, multiplied by a few hundred evenings, is how a family drifts apart without anyone ever choosing it. Not by a single wound, but by ten thousand small sensible retreats into the hollow.

[PAUSE]

And here is the thing about the conversation that did not happen. The awkward one, the one with the one-word answers. If she had pushed through those first painful minutes, she might have reached the moment, somewhere around minute fifteen, when he finally mentioned the thing that was actually bothering him. Every parent knows that moment. It never comes in the first minute. It only comes on the far side of the climb. The feed, by offering a reward in the first second, makes sure we never get there.

The old traditions of the East have a word for something like this. Samsara. The wheel of existence, the cycle of craving and dissatisfaction that turns and turns. You crave, you grasp, you are briefly satisfied, the satisfaction fades, you crave again. The wheel spins and you go nowhere.

I would not presume to speak for those traditions, and I use the word with respect, as a borrowing. But I have come to think of our condition as a kind of social media samsara. The thumb moves upward. The craving is briefly satisfied. The satisfaction fades. The thumb moves upward again. Billions of people, every day, spinning on a wheel that was built, deliberately, to keep spinning.

And here is the cruelty of it. The craving that drives the wheel is a good craving. It is the hunger to know and to connect. If it were a bad craving, a simple vice, we might recognize it and resist it. But because it is our best hunger, our highest hunger, the one that has driven every achievement of our species, we cannot easily see that it has been captured. We feel the pull of the absolute and we follow it, and it leads us into the hollow.

That is what I mean when I say the feed is the Esketian instinct turned against itself. The same drive that could make us more than human has been bent, by a business model, into something that makes us less.

[PAUSE]

[SLOW]

But here is the hope hidden in the metaphor. And I want you to hear it clearly, because it is the hinge of everything that follows.

A local minimum is not the end. It is a place where you are stuck, not a place where you must stay.

Engineers know how to get a machine out of a local minimum. You give it a push. A jolt of energy, enough to carry it up and over the rim of the hollow, so that it can feel the true slope of the landscape again and continue its descent toward something better. Sometimes the push is random. Sometimes it is deliberate. But it always requires something from outside the hollow's own logic. The hollow will never, on its own, tell you to leave.

I believe the destination of our civilization may be set. I believe the Will To God is at work in us. But the path runs through choices. And climbing out of a local minimum takes a push.

[LET IT LAND]

We are that push.

Our actions, our choices, the small daily decisions to look up instead of down, to walk across the room, to knock on the door, are the energy that carries us over the rim. No one is going to do it for us. The hollow is comfortable, and those who profit from it have every reason to make it more comfortable still.

The push has to come from us.

## X. Woe

The Sermon on the Mount, as it is recorded in the Gospel of Matthew, begins with blessings. But when the Gospel of Luke records a version of the same sermon, the blessings are followed by something else. Woes. Woe unto you that are rich, for ye have received your consolation. Woe unto you that are full, for ye shall hunger.

Before I come to the blessings, which is where I want to end tonight, I think I owe you the woes. A prophet who only blesses is a flatterer.

But let me say first who these woes are for. They are not for any person's worth. I do not believe the engineers who built these systems are monsters. Many of them are thoughtful people who went into technology because they believed, as I believe, in the power of connection. Many of them have since left, and some have spoken out, at real cost to themselves.

The man who designed one of the most important features of the modern feed is a good example. His name is Aza Raskin, and in the middle of the two-thousands he created what we now call infinite scroll, the page that never ends. He built it to make the web better. He did not want people to have to click to the next page of results. He has since said, about the danger of what he built, that he was completely blind to it at the time. And by his own rough reckoning, offered without any claim to precision, infinite scroll now wastes on the order of a hundred thousand human lifetimes every day.

I admire a man who can say that about his own work. And so my woes are not for persons. They are for a business model, and for the choices of the institutions that have kept it running after they knew. And, I will say at the end, they are for us.

[BUILD]

Woe unto the business model that measures its success in hours taken from children, for it has mistaken a harvest for a service.

Woe unto the boardroom that read the slide, the one that said we make it worse for one in three, and asked how the quarter was going, for it has seen and chosen not to see.

Woe unto the feed that has no bottom, for it was built to be fallen into.

Woe unto the notification that rings to call the faithful back, for it has made a bell of every pocket and a chapel of every red light.

Woe unto the autoplay that begins before you have chosen, for it has taken the choice from those it claims to serve.

Woe unto the engagement metric, the god of the dashboard, for it does not distinguish between joy and rage, between learning and compulsion, between a child who is delighted and a child who cannot stop. It counts only the minutes. And it has trained our machines to want only more of them.

[PAUSE]

Woe unto the lawmakers who have known for years, and have held the hearings, and have made the speeches, and have not passed the law. In the summer of twenty twenty-four, the United States Senate passed a bill to protect children online by a vote of ninety-one to three. Ninety-one to three. A vote so lopsided it is almost never seen in our divided age. And the bill died without ever receiving a vote on the floor of the House. As of this summer, a new version is moving again in the House, in a weaker form, its central duty of care stripped out. I hope I am wrong to be skeptical. I fear I am not.

Woe unto a market that has let the cost appear on no invoice, for it has priced the cheeseburger and forgotten the heart attack.

[LET IT LAND]

[SLOW]

And now the last woe. The hardest one. The one I must preach to myself.

Woe unto us.

Woe unto us who know all of this, who have read the articles and shared them, who have nodded at the documentaries, who have sighed about our screen time reports, and who reached for the phone again before the credits had finished rolling.

Woe unto us who complain of loneliness and have not knocked on a single door.

Woe unto us who handed a tablet to a crying child because it was easier, and then wondered why the child cried when it was taken away.

Woe unto us who sat at dinner across from the people we love most in the world and checked to see what strangers were saying.

Woe unto us who have bowed our heads in every airport and waiting room and subway car and have never once looked up to see who else was there.

[PAUSE]

I say these things because Douglass was right. Sometimes argument is not enough. Sometimes we need to feel the weight of what we already know.

But I do not say them to leave you in despair. A woe is not a sentence. It is a warning. And a warning is only spoken to people who still have time to turn around.

So let us turn around. Let us talk about what we might turn toward.

## XI. Asabiyyah

In the year thirteen seventy-seven, a man named Ibn Khaldun withdrew from public life.

He had been a scholar and a statesman in North Africa and Muslim Spain. He had served sultans, been imprisoned, fallen in and out of favor, watched dynasties rise and dynasties collapse. And at last, tired of it all, he retreated to a remote fortress in what is now Algeria, a place called Qal'at ibn Salama, and for nearly four years he wrote.

What he wrote was an introduction to a history of the world. It is called the Muqaddimah, which simply means the introduction. And in it, he tried to answer a question that every student of history eventually asks.

Why do civilizations rise? And why do they fall?

[PAUSE]

His answer turned on a single word. Asabiyyah.

It is usually translated as group feeling, or social cohesion, or solidarity. It is the bond that holds a people together. The willingness of each person to stand for the others, to sacrifice for them, to act with them as one body. And Ibn Khaldun observed that this bond is strongest in hard places, at the edges of the great empires, among people who must depend on each other completely simply to survive. People who live close to one another, share hardship together, know each other's names and faces and families.

Those people, he said, are the ones who rise. Their solidarity is a kind of power that no army of mercenaries can match. They come in from the periphery, and they topple the old rulers, and they found a new dynasty.

And then something happens. Settled at the center, surrounded by comfort, the new rulers grow attached to their luxuries. The hardship that bound them together is gone. The solidarity frays. Each family begins to look after itself. Factions form. The group feeling that made them strong dissolves into a crowd of individuals, each pursuing his own comfort. And in a few generations, Ibn Khaldun said, the dynasty is as weak as the one it replaced. And a new people, with a stronger bond, comes in from the edge.

He thought that a dynasty rarely lasted beyond three generations. The first builds. The second maintains. The third forgets what it was all for.

[LET IT LAND]

I think about Ibn Khaldun a great deal, because I think he described our moment six and a half centuries before it arrived.

What is the feed, if not the ultimate luxury at the center of the empire? A machine that brings every comfort, every distraction, every pleasure to you without your ever having to leave your chair or depend on another living soul. A machine that makes it possible to live entirely without the hardship of needing your neighbor.

And what has it done to our asabiyyah? It has dissolved it. Not through cruelty, but through comfort. It has made each of us a sovereign of our own small screen, attended by an algorithm that knows our every preference and never asks anything of us in return. And in the process, it has unbound us from each other.

Robert Putnam saw the beginning of this, as I mentioned, in the leagues and lodges and church suppers that emptied out across the second half of the last century. Total bowling went up, he noticed. More Americans were bowling than ever. But fewer of them were bowling in leagues. They were bowling alone. The activity survived. The solidarity did not.

[BUILD]

Now listen to what solidarity actually requires, according to Ibn Khaldun. It requires proximity. It requires shared hardship. It requires people who can see each other and act together.

You cannot build asabiyyah with a stranger on the far side of the world whom you will never meet. You can share opinions with him. You can share jokes with him. You can be enraged together at the same headline. But you cannot help him move house. You cannot watch his children when he is sick. You cannot stand with him at a town meeting or a school board or a picket line or a funeral. You cannot build anything with him that requires bodies in the same place at the same time.

This is the great limitation of the feed's version of community. It is a community of spectators. It can share a feeling, but it cannot share a task. And a people that can only share feelings and never tasks will never have the kind of solidarity that lets a civilization rise. It will have something else. A crowd, endlessly agitated, endlessly fragmenting, unable to do anything together except scroll.

[PAUSE]

And here is where I want to make the most ambitious claim of this evening, and I will mark it plainly as a hope and not a proof.

I believe that any people that rediscovered real, local, embodied solidarity in this age, and used its technology to strengthen that solidarity instead of dissolving it, would generate an asabiyyah so powerful that it could reshape the world. Not by force. By the sheer spiritual power of connection. By being the people who still know their neighbors, still show up for each other, still act together, in an age when everyone else has forgotten how.

It would be, I think, the cure for the atomization and alienation that every critic of modern capitalism has written about for two hundred years. Not because capitalism would be abolished, but because it would finally be pointed at the thing we actually need.

[SLOW]

The temples we are building, the great data centers rising across the country, can serve that end or they can serve the hollow. They can become instruments of real knowledge, joined to real endurance, in service of real communities. Or they can become the most powerful engines of distraction ever constructed.

The machines do not choose. We choose.

And the choice, I have come to believe, begins with something embarrassingly small. It begins with knowing who is in the room.

## XII. The Price of the Good

Let me turn now from the prophet's language to the economist's, because I think the way out has to be practical or it is nothing.

I have said that the feed is a mispriced externality. Let me say what I mean by the price of a good, because I think about it in a particular way.

[PAUSE]

Take a cheeseburger. The humblest product of modern capitalism. When you eat it, you experience something. The salt. The fat. The warmth. The nourishment. That experience, that felt quality, is the reason the whole vast system exists. The cattle ranches, the wheat fields, the trucks, the kitchens, the workers in paper hats, the advertising budget, all of that labor and all of that capital have been organized for one purpose: to produce that moment of experience in your mouth.

And the market, through its prices and profits, is constantly asking a question about that experience. Should it continue? Should it be sustained? A business that makes a healthy profit is being told, by the market, keep going, this is worth sustaining into the future. A business running at cost is being told, this is worth sustaining for now. A business losing money is being told, this should end.

I have come to think of capitalism, at its best, as a vast machine for testing experiences. For finding out which goods are worth sustaining in time and which are not.

[BUILD]

But that machine only works if every cost is priced in. And this is where it fails.

The cheeseburger's price does not include the heart disease that may come decades later. The cigarette's price did not include the cancer ward. And the feed's price, which appears to be zero, does not include the lost sleep, the lost friendships, the teenage girl who feels worse about her body, the family dinner that went silent, the loneliness that the Surgeon General compared to fifteen cigarettes a day.

When a cost is missing from the price, the market's signal is wrong. It tells us to sustain something that, if we saw its full cost, we might choose to end, or at least to change. The feed's enormous profits are telling the market: more of this, forever. And the market obeys, because the market cannot see what is not on the invoice.

[PAUSE]

So the task, put in economic terms, is simple to state. Price the externality. Get the cost onto the invoice. And there are, I think, four ways it is beginning to happen.

The first is the courtroom. I have told you about the verdict in Los Angeles. Whatever happens on appeal, the meaning of it is clear. Twelve citizens looked at the design of the infinite feed and the autoplay and the notification, and they said: this has a cost, and the company that chose it must pay it. Every one of the thousands of cases still waiting is, in a sense, an accountant arriving to correct the books. When design choices carry liability, companies begin to design differently. That is how externalities have always been corrected.

The second is the law. In Australia, on the tenth of December, twenty twenty-five, a law came into force setting a minimum age of sixteen for accounts on the major social platforms. In the first month, the platforms reported deactivating or restricting about four point seven million accounts believed to belong to children. Companies face fines of up to forty-nine and a half million Australian dollars for failing to take reasonable steps. The critics are right that some children have found ways around it, and that age checks raise real questions about privacy. I do not pretend it is a perfect law. But a whole nation decided that some costs were too high to leave off the invoice, and other nations are now watching. Here in the United States, the bill I mentioned, the one that passed the Senate ninety-one to three, is moving again in a weaker form. We should demand that it be strengthened, not hollowed out.

[LET IT LAND]

The third is information. I believe that mispriced externalities are, at bottom, a problem of information. They persist because the people paying the cost cannot see it clearly, and the people causing it have no reason to show them. And here I am, I confess, an optimist about the very machines whose temples I described earlier. A person who asks one of the great new language models whether spending six hours a day on a phone is good for them will be told, with the whole weight of medical research behind it, that it is not. As better information reaches each of us, at the moment of decision, I believe our choices will become more rational, and those rational choices will compound. The prices will begin to reflect what we actually know.

And the fourth, the one that matters most, is us. Because the market is not an alien force. It is the sum of our choices. Every hour we withdraw from the counterfeit and spend on the real thing is a vote. Every family that keeps the phones out of the bedroom at night, every school that keeps them in a locker during the day, every friend group that puts the phones face down in the middle of the table, is a small correction to the price. And small corrections, made by millions, become a new market.

[BUILD]

But I want to be clear about something, because it is the heart of what I believe. It is not enough to price the bad thing. We must also build the good thing.

When we put warning labels on cigarettes, we did not ask people to stop breathing. When we tax pollution, we do not ask people to stop using energy. The goal of correcting an externality is never to abolish the good that was being sold. It is to deliver that same good without the hidden cost.

And so the goal here cannot be to abolish connection. That would be to abolish the very thing I have spent this evening calling sacred. The goal is to deliver connection, the real thing, the Will To God operating as it should, with the externality finally priced in.

[SLOW]

Which brings me to the question I have been carrying since that afternoon in the airport. What would that look like? What would a technology look like that pointed our deepest hunger at something real?

## XIII. The Road Home

I want to be honest with you about where I stand, because I have spent two hours asking you to trust me, and you deserve to know my interest.

After that afternoon in the airport, I built something. I am going to tell you about it, and I want you to understand that I am telling you about my own work, which I believe in, and which I have every reason to want you to use. Weigh what I say accordingly.

[PAUSE]

The question I asked myself in that airport was simple. How is it that we all hold these devices, and I can speak to no one around me? Nearly every person in that room carried a phone. And there was no common way for any of them to reach the person sitting beside them.

So I built one. It is called Vicinity. It is a chat that connects you, in real time, with the people physically near you. Not the people in your contacts. Not the people the algorithm thinks will keep you scrolling. The people in the room. The people on the block. The people at the same concert, the same airport, the same neighborhood.

I did not invent the idea out of nowhere. I derived it from everything I have told you tonight. If the Will To God is the drive to close the gaps of knowledge between us, then the most valuable gap to close is the one between people who could actually do something together. If the counterfeit is knowledge without sustainment, then the real thing is knowledge that comes with sustainment. Local knowledge. Knowledge about the people who could help you, and whom you could help.

[BUILD]

Think about what the feed gives you. It gives you a world of things you can do nothing about. A war on another continent. A scandal in another city. A stranger's triumph or a stranger's tragedy, in a place you will never go, among people you will never meet. It floods you with knowledge and strips you of agency. You know everything and can do nothing. And that combination, knowledge without the power to act, is a recipe for anxiety. It is no wonder we come away from the feed feeling helpless.

Now think about the reverse. Knowledge about the moment you are actually living in. The people actually around you. Your immediate community. The ones you could feasibly do something with, and do something for. That kind of knowledge is often uncomfortable at first. It can be painful to become aware of exactly where you are and who is near you. But it comes with something the feed can never give you. It comes with the ability to act. And knowledge joined to the power to act, among people who can sustain each other, is not a counterfeit. It is the real thing.

[PAUSE]

I will tell you, honestly, that I use it every day, and that I feel good when I do. There is something that happens when you know that the people near you are looking for connection as actively as you are. The room changes. The strangers become, potentially, neighbors. I cannot hand you a study proving that it cures loneliness. It is too new, and I am too close to it. I can only tell you what it was built to do and what it has felt like to me.

I have made a smaller thing, too, in the same spirit. A music visualizer that, instead of pulling you away into a screen, paints the music onto the room you are actually in. Onto its walls, its furniture, the air around you. The idea is the same. Not to take you out of the place where you are, but to make you more present in it, more connected to the space and to the moment you are sharing with whoever is there.

[LET IT LAND]

Let me say a little more about what I mean by a technology that gives back, because I think it can be described, and because I think any engineer or founder or investor listening tonight could build toward it.

A technology that gives back has an end. The feed is built so that there is no bottom, no last page, no natural moment to stop. The old newspaper had a last page. The letter had a signature. The television program had credits. Those endings were not flaws. They were mercies. They were the moments when the mind was returned to its owner. A tool that respects you will tell you when you are done.

A technology that gives back points outward, toward the world, rather than inward, toward itself. Its success is measured not by how long you stay, but by what you do after you leave. Did you meet someone? Did you learn something you could use? Did you show up somewhere you would not otherwise have gone? The map application is a good example. Nobody measures a map by how many hours you spend staring at it. It is good precisely because it gets you where you are going and then gets out of the way.

A technology that gives back favors the near over the far. Not exclusively. There is great good in reaching across the world. But by default, it should help you see and serve the people whose lives are actually tangled with yours. Your street. Your school. Your town. Because that is where knowledge can turn into action, and action into sustainment.

And a technology that gives back is honest about its costs. It does not hide them off the books. It tells you how much of your life it has taken this week, and it does not make that number hard to find. It does not ring bells to call you back when you have chosen to leave. It treats your attention the way a good friend treats your time: as something precious that was lent, not something cheap that was taken.

[PAUSE]

None of this is beyond our engineering. We have built machines that can predict, to the half second, when a teenager is about to put down her phone, and intervene to stop her. We could build machines that notice when she has been scrolling for an hour and gently suggest she call a friend. The capability is the same. The difference is the objective. And objectives, unlike the laws of physics, are chosen.

But I want to be very clear. This is not, in the end, about an app. Mine or anyone's. An app is a tool. It can point the hunger in the right direction or the wrong one. The road home is not a product. It is a practice.

So let me describe the practice, because you can begin it tonight without downloading anything at all.

Look up. That is the first step, and it is the whole of what I did in that airport. In the next waiting room you sit in, before you reach for the phone, look up. See who is there. You do not have to speak. Just see them.

Learn the names. The person who lives next door. The person across the hall. The person who makes your coffee. A name is the first thread of asabiyyah.

Share a task, not only a feeling. Join something that requires your body in a place. A team, a choir, a garden, a volunteer crew, a congregation, a board, a book club that actually meets. Something where people depend on you to show up.

Guard the sacred hours. The first hour of the morning and the last hour of the night belonged, for most of human history, to prayer, to family, to rest. Take them back. Put the phone in another room. Let the first face you see in the morning be a human face.

Feed the children real bread. Give them the outdoors, the friend's house, the boredom out of which imagination grows. Delay the feed. Join with other parents, because no family can hold that line alone. And that, too, is asabiyyah.

[BUILD]

And when you use technology, as all of us will and should, ask of every tool the question I have been asking all night. Does it give back? Does it join knowledge to sustainment? Does it bring me closer to the people I can actually love and serve, or does it carry me away from them?

By their fruits. That is the test. It was the test two thousand years ago and it is the test now.

## XIV. The Beatitudes of the Near

The most famous sermon in the Western tradition begins on a hillside, with a crowd of ordinary people. Fishermen and farmers and the sick and the poor. And it begins not with a command but with a blessing. Blessed are the poor in spirit. Blessed are they that mourn. Blessed are the meek. Blessed are the peacemakers.

What is remarkable about those blessings is who receives them. Not the powerful. Not the celebrated. Not the people at the center of the empire. The blessings go to the overlooked. To those whom the world did not count.

I want to close the body of this sermon with blessings of my own. I offer them humbly, in my own words, in the shape of that old sermon, for the people of our age whom the feed does not count.

[PAUSE]

[SLOW]

Blessed are those who look up in the waiting room, for they shall see their neighbor.

Blessed are the bored, for out of boredom imagination is born, and they shall make something no algorithm could have chosen for them.

Blessed are those who put the phone in another room at night, for they shall wake to a human face.

Blessed are those who know the names of the people on their street, for they shall not be alone when the storm comes.

Blessed are those who mourn in the company of others and not in the comments, for they shall be comforted by arms and not by emojis.

[BUILD]

Blessed are the parents who hold the line together, for their children shall inherit a childhood.

Blessed are the children who climb trees and scrape knees and come home when the streetlights come on, for they shall know the world with their bodies and not only with their thumbs.

Blessed are those who share a task and not only a feeling, for they shall build something that outlasts them.

Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for knowledge, for they shall be filled, if they seek the real bread and refuse the counterfeit.

Blessed are the engineers who build tools that give back, for they shall be called makers of the true temples.

[PAUSE]

Blessed are the lawmakers who put the cost on the invoice, for they shall be remembered as those who protected the children when it was hard.

Blessed are the jurors who looked at the ledger and wrote a number in it, for they have reminded the powerful that they answer to the people.

Blessed are the ones who left the companies and told the truth, for they have paid a price so that the rest of us might see.

Blessed are the lonely who reach out first, for they are braver than they know, and they shall find that they were never as alone as the feed made them feel.

[LET IT LAND]

Blessed are the neighbors.

The woman two seats down. The man across the hall. The stranger at the next table, at the next desk, at the next bus stop. Blessed are all those who are near, whom we have overlooked for so long while we bowed our heads to the far away.

For theirs is the kingdom of the here and the now. And it has been waiting for us, all this time, just beyond the edge of the screen.

## XV. I Do Not Despair

Near the end of his speech in Rochester, after he had laid out the darkest picture he could paint of the state of his nation, after he had spent the scorching irony and catalogued every hypocrisy, Frederick Douglass did something that I find more moving every time I read it.

He turned toward hope.

"Allow me to say, in conclusion," he told them, "notwithstanding the dark picture I have this day presented of the state of the nation, I do not despair of this country."

[PAUSE]

He did not despair. In eighteen fifty-two. With slavery still the law of the land, with the Fugitive Slave Act newly passed, with the war that would end it nearly a decade away and no one knowing it would come. He looked at all of that, and he said: I do not despair.

How could he say it? Because he believed that the principles the nation professed were true, even when the nation betrayed them. He believed that the truth, once it was spoken loudly enough and long enough, would work its way through the world. He believed the arm of justice was long.

I have stood before you tonight and painted a dark picture of my own. And I want to end where Douglass ended. I do not despair.

[BUILD]

I do not despair, because the hunger is good.

The thing the feed has captured is not a vice. It is the best thing in us. The Will To God. The drive to know and to be known, to reach out, to bring every mind into contact with every other. A counterfeit only works because the real thing is precious. And a hunger that has been misdirected can be redirected. It has not been destroyed. It is waiting.

I do not despair, because the ledger has been opened.

For twenty years, the cost of the feed sat off the books, and now it is being written in. In courtrooms in Los Angeles and New Mexico. In laws in Australia. In the warning of a Surgeon General. In the votes of ninety-one senators. In the slow, stubborn work of parents and teachers who have decided that their children's hours are not for sale. The price is beginning to tell the truth.

I do not despair, because a local minimum is not a grave.

It is a hollow on the hillside. And a hollow can be climbed out of. It takes a push. It takes energy from outside the hollow's own logic. And that energy is us. Our choices. Our small, daily, unglamorous decisions to look up, to learn a name, to knock on a door, to share a task, to put the phone in the other room.

[PAUSE]

And I do not despair, above all, because of the people.

I have spent years now thinking about strangers. About the people in that airport, bowed over their screens. And here is what I have come to believe about them, and about you. Most people are good. Most people want to help. Most people, if you look up and meet their eyes and say hello, will say hello back. The woman two seats down was not a threat or a stranger. She was a neighbor I had not yet met. The feed had simply taught me to look past her.

[SLOW]

So let me leave you with an image.

Have you ever watched rain begin to fall on still water? A pond, or a lake on a quiet evening. The first drop lands, and from that single point a ring spreads outward. Then another drop, and another ring. And soon the whole surface of the water is alive with circles, each one beginning in a single place, each one spreading outward to meet the others, overlapping, crossing, joining, until you cannot tell where one ends and the next begins.

That is what I think connection is meant to be. Not a feed that pulls every eye toward a single glowing center, draining the life out of every place it touches. But a thousand small rings, each beginning where a person actually is, each spreading outward to the people actually near, overlapping and joining until the whole surface is alive.

The infinite interconnection of the world, made local. Made human. Made near.

[LET IT LAND]

That is the real God I have been talking about tonight. Not the counterfeit in your pocket, which takes your hours and gives back nothing. But the real thing. Knowledge joined to sustainment. Connection that comes with the power to act. A people who know each other, and stand for each other, and build together.

It has always been within reach. It is within reach tonight.

[BUILD]

So the next time you sit in a waiting room, look up.

The next time you reach for the feed at the end of a long day, pause, and ask whether there is someone near you who would rather hear from you.

The next time you are lonely, and you will be, because we all are, remember that the person in the next chair may be lonely too, and that one of you has to go first.

Let it be you.

Turn away from the counterfeit. Climb out of the hollow. Put down the idol, and lift up your eyes.

[PAUSE]

[SLOW]

And come to know, and to love, your neighbor.

Thank you.
