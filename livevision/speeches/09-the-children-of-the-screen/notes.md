## Rhetorical approach

Borrowed from the Philippics, in my own words:

- The creeping threat that grows while the assembly debates and waits. Philip took one town at a time; the attention economy took one hour, one habit, one grade level at a time.
- Direct, blunt address to the audience as citizens who hold the power and are failing to use it ("you", "we", "men and women of this assembly").
- The "barbarian boxer" image from the First Philippic (4.40, J. H. Vince translation): the fighter who clutches where he was last hit and never guards. Quoted once, briefly, and attributed; then developed as the speech's recurring figure for reactive policy.
- Answering the opponent's case before the audience can raise it (the critics of Haidt get a full section and a real answer).
- Shame at inaction paired with hope: "the fault is ours, and therefore the remedy is ours" (paraphrase of the Philippic stance, not a quotation).
- Concrete proposals, not just alarm (Demosthenes named the ships and the money; this speech names the four norms, the laws, the school policies).
- Honest ending: Chaeronea (338 BC) as the cost of acting too late; the peroration argues we still have the time Athens lost.
- Anaphora, tricolon and antithesis throughout; build to a peroration.
