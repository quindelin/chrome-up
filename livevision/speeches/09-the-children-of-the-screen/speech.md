# The Children of the Screen

## I. The Fighter Who Never Guards

Friends, neighbors, parents, teachers, lawmakers, and any young person who has wandered into this room by accident or by courage: I have come to talk about a danger that did not arrive with an army.

It did not cross a border. It did not fire a shot. It did not announce itself with a declaration of war.

It arrived in a box, wrapped in white cardboard, under a Christmas tree. It arrived as a birthday present. It arrived as a reward for good grades. It arrived as a safety measure, so that a mother could reach her daughter after practice.

[PAUSE]

And it arrived with our blessing.

Let me begin with a picture. It is not one particular child. It is a composite, drawn from the stories that parents and counselors and pediatricians have been telling for a decade now, and I offer it to you as exactly that.

It is two in the morning on a school night. A thirteen-year-old girl is lying in bed with the covers pulled over her head so that the light will not wake her sister. Her thumb moves. Up. Up. Up. Each flick of the thumb delivers a new face, a new body, a new party she was not invited to, a new joke she is not in on.

She is not having fun. If you asked her, she would tell you she is not having fun. She would tell you she wants to stop. She would tell you she has a math test at eight. And still the thumb moves, because the feed has no bottom, and somewhere beneath the next swipe there might be the one message that tells her she belongs.

[SLOW]

She will sleep four hours tonight. She will sleep four hours tomorrow night. And by the spring, her mother will be sitting in a pediatrician's office hearing words she never expected to hear about her own child.

Multiply that girl by millions, and you have the story I came to tell.

Now let me tell you why I have chosen, of all the voices in history, to borrow the voice of an Athenian who has been dead for more than two thousand three hundred years.

In the middle of the fourth century before Christ, a king named Philip ruled Macedon, a kingdom to the north of Greece that the Athenians had long regarded as a backwater. Philip did not conquer Greece in a day. He took a town here. A harbor there. A fortress in one season, an alliance in the next. Each step was small enough to explain away. Each step was too far away to feel urgent.

And in Athens, the richest and freest city in the Greek world, the citizens met in their assembly and debated, and voted, and waited.

One man would not let them wait. His name was Demosthenes. According to the ancient stories, he had been a weak and stammering youth who trained himself to speak by practicing in an underground room and declaiming over the roar of the sea. He grew into the greatest orator of his age, and he spent that voice on a single cause: warning his fellow citizens that the slow danger was a real danger, and that the time to act was now.

His speeches against Philip became so famous that we still call any fierce denunciation a philippic.

In the first of them, delivered around three hundred and fifty-one before Christ, Demosthenes reached for an image that I have never been able to forget. He told the Athenians that they fought Philip, in the words of one old translation, "exactly as a barbarian boxes."

[PAUSE]

He meant an untrained fighter. A man who, when he is struck on the cheek, clutches his cheek. When he is struck in the ribs, he clutches his ribs. He never raises his guard. He never watches his opponent. He never thinks about the next blow. He only reacts to the last one.

That, Demosthenes told his city, was Athenian policy. Philip strikes in Thrace, and you send ships to Thrace. Philip strikes somewhere else, and you run there too, late, and only after the blow has landed.

[LET IT LAND]

I put it to you tonight that we have been boxing like that barbarian for more than a decade.

A study comes out, and we clutch at it. A child dies, and we clutch at that. A whistleblower testifies, and we hold a hearing, and we clutch at the headlines for a week. And then the next blow comes from a direction we were not watching, and we forget the last one.

Meanwhile, the opponent has been watching us very carefully indeed. It has studied our children's attention with a precision no general ever brought to a map. It knows when they wake, when they sleep, what makes them linger, what makes them return.

I am not here to tell you that a piece of glass and silicon is a king with an army. I am not here to tell you that the people who build these products are villains in a melodrama. I will be careful tonight, and I will be honest about what we know and what we do not.

But I am here to say what Demosthenes said to his city. The danger is real. The danger is growing in ways that are easy to explain away. And the remedy is in our hands, if only we will raise our guard and use it.

For the next two hours, I will lay out the evidence. I will give the other side its strongest case, because a cause that cannot survive its critics does not deserve your support. I will show you what parents, schools, courts, and legislatures around the world are already doing. And at the end, I will ask you to act.

[PAUSE]

Before I do, let me give you one more thing from Demosthenes, because it goes to the heart of how we think about this.

Ten years after his first warning, around three hundred and forty-one before Christ, Demosthenes delivered what many consider his masterpiece, the Third Philippic. By then Athens was formally at peace with Philip. There was a treaty. There were ambassadors. There were polite words on both sides.

And Demosthenes told his city, in effect: stop pretending. You call this peace. But Philip has been making war on you all along, while you were busy calling it peace. The only side that is not fighting is yours.

[PAUSE]

I think we are in a similar place. We tell ourselves this is normal life. Kids have phones. Kids are online. That is just how it is now. We have signed a kind of treaty with the technology, and we pretend that it is peace.

But every night, a contest is being fought over our children's attention, their sleep, their friendships and their sense of their own worth. One side has billions of dollars, armies of engineers, and the most sophisticated behavioral science ever assembled. The other side is a tired parent and a thirteen-year-old with a thumb.

We are already in the struggle. The only question is whether we will admit it, and start to fight back.

[PAUSE]

Let us begin with the numbers.

## II. The Year the Lines Turned

Every two years, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention asks tens of thousands of American high school students a set of plain questions. One of them is this: during the past twelve months, did you ever feel so sad or hopeless almost every day for two weeks or more in a row that you stopped doing some usual activities?

It is not a clinical diagnosis. It is a question about whether a young person's sadness has become heavy enough to stop their life.

In two thousand twenty-one, forty-two percent of American high school students said yes.

[PAUSE]

Forty-two percent. Look at the young people in any classroom, and imagine that four in every ten of them have spent at least two weeks of the past year so sad that they stopped doing the things they normally do.

In two thousand twenty-three, the number was forty percent. That was still, according to the CDC's own trend report, about ten percentage points higher than it had been a decade earlier.

And for girls, the numbers were worse. In two thousand twenty-one, fifty-seven percent of high school girls reported persistent sadness or hopelessness. In two thousand twenty-three, fifty-three percent. Compare that with twenty-eight percent of boys in the same year.

More than half of the young women in our high schools. Let that sentence sit for a moment.

[LET IT LAND]

And do not imagine that the boys are fine because their number is lower. Twenty-eight percent of high school boys is still more than one in four. Boys, too, are suffering, though they often show it differently, in withdrawal, in isolation, in hours lost to screens, in a quiet drift away from friends and school and the world. A crisis that falls hardest on girls is still a crisis for everyone.

Think of a single high school of a thousand students. By these figures, on the girls' side of the building, more than two hundred and fifty young women spent at least two weeks of the past year so sad or hopeless that they stopped doing what they normally do. On the boys' side, about a hundred and forty young men. In one school. And there are thousands upon thousands of high schools in this country.

The same survey asks whether students have seriously considered suicide in the past year. In two thousand thirteen, seventeen percent said yes. By two thousand twenty-one, the figure had reached twenty-two percent. In two thousand twenty-three, twenty percent.

One in five teenagers, seriously considering ending their own lives.

Now, a survey is a survey. Words on a questionnaire. A skeptic might say that young people today are simply more willing to talk about their feelings, that what has changed is not their pain but their vocabulary.

That is a fair question, and I want to answer it with something harder than words. I want to answer it with blood and with death.

In two thousand seventeen, a team of researchers at the CDC, led by Melissa Mercado, published a research letter in the Journal of the American Medical Association. They looked at visits to emergency rooms for self-inflicted injuries among young Americans between two thousand one and two thousand fifteen.

These are not answers on a questionnaire. These are children who hurt themselves badly enough to be taken to a hospital.

For most of the first decade of this century, the rate was flat. Then, among girls aged ten to fourteen, it began to climb. In two thousand nine, the rate was about one hundred and ten visits per hundred thousand girls. By two thousand fifteen, it was about three hundred and eighteen.

It nearly tripled in six years. An increase of nearly nineteen percent, every single year.

[SLOW]

Girls of ten. Girls of eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen. Children in middle school, arriving in emergency rooms with injuries they inflicted on themselves, at nearly three times the rate of just six years before.

The researchers were careful. Their data could not separate injuries meant to end a life from injuries meant to manage unbearable feelings. But either way, it is a measure of suffering that no change in vocabulary can explain.

And then there is the hardest number of all.

The National Center for Health Statistics reports that the suicide rate among Americans aged ten to twenty-four was stable from two thousand one through two thousand seven. Then it rose. By two thousand twenty-one, it was sixty-two percent higher, from about seven deaths per hundred thousand young people to eleven.

Among children aged ten to fourteen, the rate tripled between two thousand seven and two thousand eighteen. Among those aged fifteen to nineteen, it rose fifty-seven percent between two thousand nine and two thousand seventeen.

[PAUSE]

Behind every one of those decimal points is a funeral. A bedroom left untouched. A parent who will spend the rest of their life asking what they missed.

There is one more source I want to give you, because it measures something closer to clinical illness. The federal government's National Survey on Drug Use and Health assesses whether adolescents aged twelve to seventeen have experienced a major depressive episode in the past year, using criteria drawn from the psychiatric manual: not just sadness, but disturbed sleep, appetite, energy, concentration, and self-worth.

In two thousand twenty-one, that figure stood at twenty point eight percent. One adolescent in five, meeting the criteria for a major depressive episode in a single year.

Now, I promised you honesty, and so I will tell you now, early, what I will return to later. Some of these numbers have improved since two thousand twenty-one.

The same federal survey found that the share of adolescents with a major depressive episode fell to fifteen point four percent in two thousand twenty-four. And just last month, in September of two thousand twenty-six, the CDC published the results of its two thousand twenty-five youth survey. Persistent sadness and hopelessness among high schoolers fell to thirty-three percent. Serious consideration of suicide fell from twenty-two percent in two thousand twenty-one to fourteen percent.

That is real progress, and I thank God for every child it represents. Doctors quoted in the coverage called it encouraging, and they were right to.

But hear me. Thirty-three percent is still one high school student in three, so sad for two weeks that they stopped living their normal lives. And the improvement is measured from a peak that came during and just after a pandemic that shut our children in their rooms. Coming down from the summit is not the same as returning to the valley we left.

[BUILD]

What I want you to notice is the shape of the line before the pandemic ever came. Not two thousand twenty. Not two thousand twenty-one. The line bends around two thousand ten, two thousand eleven, two thousand twelve. Years of economic recovery. Years before anyone had heard of the coronavirus.

Something happened to our children in those years.

[PAUSE]

The question is what.

## III. What Changed in Their Hands

When you look for the cause of a sudden change, the first rule is to look for something else that changed at the same time.

The financial crisis of two thousand eight is a natural suspect. Hard times are hard on families. But as Jonathan Haidt and others have pointed out, the teenage numbers did not move much during the worst of the recession. They began to climb years later, as the economy was recovering. A crisis that does its damage only after it has ended is a strange sort of crisis.

So what else changed in those years?

[PAUSE]

Picture a high school hallway in two thousand seven. Some students carry cell phones. Most of those are flip phones. You can call. You can send a text, pecking out each letter on a keypad. Some of the bolder ones have a page on a social network, which they check on the family computer in the den when they get home, under the vague supervision of a parent passing by with the laundry.

Now picture the same hallway in two thousand fifteen.

Nearly every student is carrying a supercomputer with a high-resolution camera, a permanent connection to the internet, and a menu of applications designed by some of the most talented engineers on earth for one purpose above all: to capture and hold attention.

The front-facing camera had arrived. The app store had arrived. Instagram had arrived and been bought by Facebook. Snapchat had arrived. The infinite feed, the push notification, the like counter, the streak. All of it had moved from the den to the pocket, and from the pocket to the pillow.

[SLOW]

That is the transition Haidt calls the great rewiring of childhood: the move from a play-based childhood to a phone-based childhood. And it happened, by his account, between roughly two thousand ten and two thousand fifteen. Exactly when the lines turned.

Let me tell you where that transition has brought us.

In the autumn of two thousand twenty-four, the Pew Research Center surveyed nearly fourteen hundred American teenagers aged thirteen to seventeen. Ninety-five percent had or could access a smartphone. Ninety-six percent used the internet every day.

And nearly half, forty-eight percent, said they were online almost constantly.

[PAUSE]

Almost constantly. That is not my phrase. That is the answer young people chose themselves to describe their own lives. And a decade earlier, the figure was twenty-four percent. It doubled.

Ninety percent of teens used YouTube. About six in ten used TikTok, and about six in ten used Instagram. Fifty-five percent used Snapchat. Sixteen percent said they were on TikTok almost constantly. Fifteen percent said the same of YouTube. One teenager in three said they were on at least one of the major platforms almost constantly.

Think about what "almost constantly" displaces.

It displaces sleep, because the phone comes to bed. It displaces the slow, boring, essential hours in which a young mind wanders and imagines. It displaces the afternoon of riding bikes to nowhere in particular. It displaces the conversation at dinner, the argument with a sibling, the long walk with a friend in which you finally say the thing you have been afraid to say.

Every hour has to come from somewhere. An adolescent has the same twenty-four hours that adolescents have always had. If a large share of the waking ones now belong to a screen, then something else is no longer happening.

[PAUSE]

Consider sleep alone. Every parent of a teenager knows the biology: adolescents tend to fall asleep later and need more sleep than adults, and our early school start times already squeeze them. Now add a device that sits on the pillow, glowing, buzzing, offering one more video, one more message, one more check of who has seen your post. The minutes at the end of the night are the minutes the phone takes first. And the minutes at the end of the night are the very minutes a growing brain uses to consolidate memory, regulate emotion and recover from the day.

A tired child is a fragile child. Anyone who has raised a toddler knows that a missed nap can turn a happy afternoon into a storm of tears. We have somehow forgotten that the same is true of fourteen-year-olds, and we have handed them an instrument that steals sleep with every swipe.

Consider too how young it starts. The Los Angeles case I will tell you about later involved a young woman who testified that she began using YouTube at six and Instagram at nine. That is not unusual. The platforms' own rules say thirteen, but those rules have been enforced, for most of their history, with a single checkbox and an honor system that every ten-year-old learns to beat in seconds.

[BUILD]

And this, friends, is what makes the smartphone different from the television that our parents worried about, or the radio that their parents worried about. You could not take the family television to the bathroom. You could not bring it to the lunch table. You could not watch it under the covers at two in the morning. You could not use it to see, in real time, every party you were left out of and exactly how many people liked your rival's photograph more than yours.

The television was a window. The smartphone is a world. And it is a world in which every social interaction is public, permanent, quantified, and ranked.

No generation of human beings has ever gone through puberty inside such a world. Not one. Our children are the first.

[PAUSE]

Now, the fact that two things happened at the same time does not prove that one caused the other. I know that, and you know that, and the critics of this argument are right to insist on it.

So let us now look at the case that has been made, and then at the case against it.

## IV. The Case for the Prosecution

In March of two thousand twenty-four, the social psychologist Jonathan Haidt published a book called The Anxious Generation. It would go on to spend more than two years on the New York Times bestseller list. A governor sent copies to every other governor in the country. It has been credited with helping to inspire laws on the other side of the world.

Its argument can be stated simply. A phone-based childhood, layered on top of an overprotected childhood, has harmed the mental health of a generation, and girls have been harmed most.

Haidt names four foundational harms.

The first is social deprivation. Time with friends in person has been replaced by time with friends, and strangers, through a screen. And the screen is a poor substitute. It offers the appearance of connection without the eye contact, the shared laughter, the physical presence that human beings evolved to need.

The second is sleep deprivation. Adolescents need more sleep than adults, and they are biologically inclined to fall asleep later. A device that lives on the pillow and buzzes through the night steals exactly the hours a growing brain can least afford to lose.

The third is attention fragmentation. A mind that is interrupted every few minutes by a notification never learns to sustain focus. And sustained focus is not a luxury. It is the foundation of learning, of craft, of deep friendship, of prayer and reflection, of everything that takes time.

The fourth is addiction. Not in the loose sense that we use when we say someone is addicted to chocolate, but in the sense of compulsive use that continues despite the user's wish to stop and despite harm to their life.

[PAUSE]

Haidt argues that these harms fall hardest on girls for reasons that make painful sense. Girls' social lives tend to be more focused on relationships and on comparison and inclusion. Visual platforms built around images of faces and bodies, filtered and ranked, were almost perfectly designed to exploit those vulnerabilities. Boys, he argues, have suffered in different ways, through withdrawal into video games and pornography and away from the real world.

He also points out, and I attribute this claim to him, that similar rises in teen distress, self-harm and suicide show up across the English-speaking world and the Nordic countries at roughly the same time. If the cause were something peculiar to American life, such as our politics or our school shootings or our economy, you would not expect countries with very different conditions to move in step.

[BUILD]

But a book is an argument, not a verdict. So let me give you two pieces of evidence from economists, who are trained to be suspicious of exactly this kind of story.

The first comes from a clever natural experiment. When Facebook was launched, it did not reach every college at once. It rolled out campus by campus, one college at a time. That staggered arrival created something close to a controlled experiment.

Three economists, Luca Braghieri, Ro'ee Levy and Alexey Makarin, used it. Writing in the American Economic Review in two thousand twenty-two, they found that Facebook's arrival on a campus worsened students' mental health. Students at those campuses were more likely to say that poor mental health was hurting their academic performance. And the mechanism the authors pointed to was unfavorable social comparison: seeing others' curated lives and judging your own against them.

That was Facebook, on a desktop computer, for college students, in the mid two thousands. A gentler, slower, older product than anything on a twelve-year-old's phone today.

The second piece of evidence is an experiment in the strict sense. Hunt Allcott, Luca Braghieri, Sarah Eichmeyer and Matthew Gentzkow paid adult users to deactivate their Facebook accounts for four weeks before the two thousand eighteen elections, and compared them with a group that kept using it. Published in the American Economic Review in two thousand twenty, the study found that those who stepped away spent more time with family and friends, and their subjective well-being went up. And here is a detail that should haunt us: after the experiment ended, they kept using Facebook substantially less. Once they had stepped outside the habit, they did not want all of it back.

[PAUSE]

Now let me give you the evidence that comes from inside the companies themselves.

In September of two thousand twenty-one, the Wall Street Journal began publishing a series that came to be called the Facebook Files, based on internal documents provided by a former employee named Frances Haugen. Among them was internal research on Instagram's effects on teenagers, research the company had been conducting since at least two thousand eighteen.

One slide from that research contained a sentence that should be carved over the door of every boardroom in Silicon Valley. The company's own researchers wrote: "we make body issues worse for one in three teenage girls."

[LET IT LAND]

That was not an activist. That was not a journalist. That was not a worried grandmother. That was the company's own staff, studying its own product, and writing down what they found.

On the fifth of October, two thousand twenty-one, Frances Haugen testified before a Senate subcommittee. She told the senators that the company's leadership knew how to make Facebook and Instagram safer and had not made the changes. Senators of both parties praised her courage. Within weeks, a bipartisan group of state attorneys general opened an investigation into Instagram's effects on children.

And then?

[PAUSE]

And then, my friends, the barbarian clutched where he had been struck, and went back to waiting.

The hearing ended. The news cycle turned. The products kept shipping. The feeds kept scrolling. Another class of eleven-year-olds got their first phones that Christmas.

That is the case for the prosecution. A sudden change in children's mental health. A sudden change in children's lives that coincides with it. A plausible mechanism. Natural experiments and controlled experiments pointing the same way. And the companies' own researchers confirming the harm in their own words.

But I promised you the other side, and you shall have it in full.

## V. The Case for the Defense

Demosthenes had opponents in Athens, and they were not fools. Some of them were serious, patriotic men who believed that his alarms were exaggerated, that war was costly, and that Philip could be managed. A speaker who pretends his opponents have no arguments insults his audience.

So let me speak for the defense, and let me try to speak for it as well as its best advocates would.

[PAUSE]

The most serious critic of the argument I have just made is a developmental psychologist named Candice Odgers. In April of two thousand twenty-four, she reviewed The Anxious Generation in the journal Nature, one of the most respected scientific publications in the world. And she did not like it.

Her central claim was that the science does not support the story. When researchers look across large populations, she argued, the association between time on social media and depression or anxiety is small and inconsistent. The evidence that screens are causing the rise in teen mental illness, she said, is equivocal at best.

Her second claim was about the direction of cause. Yes, depressed teenagers use more social media. But perhaps that is because depressed teenagers, lonely and withdrawn, retreat into their phones. The phone might be a symptom rather than a cause. Scientists call this reverse causation, and it is a real problem in any study that simply observes behavior over time.

Her third claim was the one I find most morally serious. Mental illness in young people, she argued, arises from a complex web of genetic and environmental causes: poverty, family conflict, trauma, discrimination, academic pressure, and more. If we fix our attention on phones, we may neglect the harder, costlier work of addressing those causes. A simple villain is satisfying. It may also be a distraction.

[PAUSE]

She is not alone. The sociologist Mike Males and others have called the panic about phones a moral panic, one more in a long line of adult anxieties about the young.

And that line is long indeed. Adults have fretted that novels would corrupt young women's minds. That the radio would rot children's brains. That comic books caused juvenile delinquency. That television would produce a generation of passive zombies. That video games would turn boys into killers. Every generation of adults, it seems, discovers that the technology of their children's youth is uniquely dangerous, and every generation of children grows up more or less fine.

Why should we believe that this time is different?

[BUILD]

There is a third line of criticism, and it comes from an unexpected direction. Peter Gray is a research psychologist at Boston College who has spent decades studying play. He helped found an organization called Let Grow, which promotes childhood independence. You might expect him to be Haidt's natural ally, and on the question of play, he largely is.

But Gray has argued, including in an essay published this year, that phones and social media are not the leading cause of the crisis. In his view, the bigger source of children's stress is an over-organized childhood: the steady decline of free time and independence, the rise of adult-supervised everything, the relentless pressure of school and achievement. The phone, he suggests, may be where children have fled precisely because the real world has been fenced off from them.

And then there is the most recent challenge to the alarm: the numbers I mentioned are improving. Teen depression measured by the federal survey has fallen by about a quarter since two thousand twenty-one. Persistent sadness in the CDC survey has fallen from forty-two percent to thirty-three. Serious consideration of suicide has fallen by more than a third. Yet teenagers did not give up their phones between two thousand twenty-one and two thousand twenty-five. If anything, they used them more.

[PAUSE]

If phones caused the crisis, the defense asks, why is the crisis easing while the phones remain?

There is the case for the defense. Weak and inconsistent correlations. The possibility of reverse causation. The danger of a convenient villain. The long history of moral panics. The rival explanation of lost freedom and rising pressure. And a recent recovery that does not fit the simple story.

I have tried to state it fairly, because these are honest people making honest arguments, and because some of what they say is true.

And I will add this. We need these critics. A movement that silences its skeptics becomes a crusade, and crusades make bad laws. Every time Candice Odgers or Peter Gray challenges a claim, the claim either gets stronger or gets dropped, and either way the public is better served. If I asked you tonight to believe that phones are the sole cause of every teenage sorrow, you would be right to walk out. I am not asking that. I am asking something narrower and, I think, harder to deny.

Now let me tell you why I am not persuaded that we should wait.

## VI. Answering the Defense

Begin with the charge of moral panic, because it is the one most often used to end the conversation.

It is true that adults have often been wrong about the dangers of new media. But notice what that argument actually proves. It proves that adults are capable of false alarms. It does not prove that every alarm is false. The town that was fooled by the boy who cried wolf was still eaten by the wolf when it finally came.

[PAUSE]

And there are real differences between this technology and the ones that came before.

The novel did not follow a girl into her bedroom and buzz at her at two in the morning. The radio did not keep a precise record of what made her linger and use it to decide what to show her next. The comic book did not tell her, with a public number beneath her own photograph, exactly how much her classmates valued her. The television did not let three hundred acquaintances comment on her body.

When someone says this is just like television, ask them a simple question. Did the television watch you back?

[LET IT LAND]

Next, the charge that the correlations are small.

Here, I want to be careful, because the scientists are arguing about real statistical questions, and I am not going to pretend to settle them from a podium. But I will make two observations.

First, an average effect across all young people can hide a large effect on some of them. If heavy use is harmless for most boys who watch sports highlights, but devastating for a subset of girls drawn into comparison and self-harm content, the average across everyone will look small. But a small average can contain a catastrophe for the children who are most vulnerable. Public health has always known this. Most people who drive drunk get home safely. We still ban drunk driving.

And the question we should ask is not only whether social media harms the average child, but how much, and how, and for whom. An hour a day of messaging with friends may be very different from five hours of a recommendation feed at night. A seventeen-year-old may be able to handle what a twelve-year-old cannot. These are exactly the distinctions that good policy can make: not a war on technology, but a sensible ordering of what comes when, and under what protections. We already do this with driving, with alcohol, with contracts, with voting. We give young people the keys to adult life gradually, as they grow ready. The only domain where we handed over everything at once, at eleven or twelve, with no license and no test, is the one where the most powerful companies on earth were waiting on the other side.

Second, the evidence is not only correlation. The college rollout study I described is a natural experiment, which is one of the best tools social scientists have for getting at cause. The deactivation study is a randomized experiment. Neither one involves today's children or today's products, and I will not pretend they do. But both point in the same direction, and neither can be explained by the idea that sad people simply choose more screens.

[PAUSE]

Next, the argument that phones may be a distraction from deeper causes.

This one I take most seriously, and I want to answer it with an agreement rather than a rebuttal. Yes. Poverty harms children. Family breakdown harms children. Trauma harms children. Academic pressure harms children. A serious society should fight all of these things. Nothing I say tonight is an argument against funding school counselors, expanding access to therapy, or helping struggling families.

But consider the difference in difficulty. We have been trying to end poverty for generations. We have been trying to reduce family conflict for as long as there have been families. These are hard, slow, expensive problems.

The question of whether a twelve-year-old should carry a device that connects her to every stranger on earth, all night, in her bedroom, is not a hard, slow, expensive problem. It is a choice. It is a choice that parents, schools and legislators can make this year. And a cause that is cheap to address should not be ignored just because there are other causes that are expensive to address.

[BUILD]

Now, to Peter Gray. Here, I believe the critics and the alarm-raisers are closer than they appear. Haidt himself has never argued that phones alone explain everything. His book is about two forces: the overprotection of children in the real world, and the underprotection of children in the virtual world. Gray emphasizes the first. Haidt emphasizes the second. But they share a remedy. Give children back their freedom, their play, their independence, their real-world friendships. Whether the phone is the cause or the refuge, the answer is a richer life off the screen. I will spend a whole section on that answer shortly.

[PAUSE]

And finally, the most important objection: the recent recovery.

I welcome it with my whole heart. But let us read it honestly. The peak came in and just after the pandemic, when schools closed, sports ended, and teenagers were sealed in their homes with nothing but their devices. Some of the recovery since then is surely the recovery from that extraordinary shock. Coming down from an emergency is not proof that the underlying condition is cured.

And let us remember what else has happened since two thousand twenty-one. Haidt's book became a phenomenon. Parents began talking to one another. Schools began locking up phones. States passed laws. Companies, under pressure from lawsuits, began rolling out teen accounts and parental controls. If the recovery reflects, even in part, a society beginning to raise its guard, then the lesson is not that we can relax. The lesson is that raising our guard works.

[PAUSE]

Let me now give you the argument that, for me, ends the debate about whether to act. It is an argument about risk.

Suppose the critics are right, and phones and social media have played only a small part in our children's suffering. What would we lose by following the four norms Haidt proposes? No smartphones before high school. No social media before sixteen. Phone-free schools. More independence and free play.

Our children would get their smartphones a few years later. They would spend their school days with their eyes up and their phones in a locker. They would spend more afternoons outdoors, with friends, unsupervised. They would join the great public square of social media at sixteen instead of eleven.

Is that a catastrophe? Haidt himself makes this argument: if he is wrong, these norms would do little harm.

Now suppose the critics are wrong, and the alarm is right. What would we lose by waiting? We would lose another cohort of children. And another. And another. Each one passing through the most vulnerable years of their lives inside an experiment whose results we will only fully understand when it is too late to help them.

[SLOW]

When the cost of acting is small and the cost of waiting may be enormous, the burden of proof belongs to those who say wait.

Let me be clear about what that principle does not mean. It does not mean panic. It does not mean smashing phones or banning the internet or treating every teenager with a screen as a casualty. It does not mean that any law with the word children in its title deserves our vote. Precaution is not the same as hysteria. It means choosing the modest, reversible, low-cost measures first, the ones that do little harm if we are wrong and much good if we are right, and then watching carefully, and adjusting as the evidence comes in. Delaying a smartphone by a few years is reversible. Locking phones away during the school day is reversible. Restoring recess is reversible. A lost childhood is not.

That was Demosthenes's argument too. His opponents said: we cannot be sure Philip intends to attack us. We cannot be sure he is as strong as you say. Let us wait for more evidence.

And Demosthenes answered, in substance: by the time you are sure, he will be at the gates.

[LET IT LAND]

We do not need certainty to act. We need judgment. And judgment, tonight, says this: give our children their childhood back, and let the scientists continue their debate while our sons and daughters sleep through the night.

## VII. The Lost Kingdom of Play

Let me take you now, not to Athens, but to your own childhood. Or your parents' childhood, if you are young enough that this sounds like a fairy tale.

[PAUSE]

Do you remember the sound of the screen door slamming behind you on a summer morning? Do you remember the instruction, the only instruction, that you received as you left: be home when the streetlights come on?

Do you remember the creek, or the vacant lot, or the woods behind the subdivision, where you and a band of other children made up games with rules that changed every hour? Where you climbed things you should not have climbed, and fell, and scraped your knee, and got up? Where you fought with your best friend and made up with your best friend before dinner, without a single adult to referee?

That was not idleness. That was education. It may have been the most important education you ever received.

In that lost kingdom of play, children learned to manage risk by taking small risks. They learned to manage conflict by having conflicts and resolving them themselves. They learned to manage fear by being a little afraid and discovering that they survived. They learned competence by being trusted with real tasks, and they learned courage by being allowed to need it.

[BUILD]

Peter Gray, the psychologist I mentioned earlier, has spent his career arguing that free play is how young mammals, including young humans, learn to be adults. And he has argued for years that as free play has declined, children's sense of control over their own lives has declined with it, and anxiety has filled the space where confidence used to grow.

Haidt agrees. Indeed, half of his book is devoted to this theme. He argues that in the nineteen nineties and two thousands, well before smartphones, American parents began to pull their children indoors. Fear of strangers, fueled by twenty-four-hour cable news, made parents reluctant to let children walk to school or play in the park alone, even as crime was actually falling. Childhood became scheduled, supervised, and safe in all the wrong ways.

So we built a generation that was overprotected in the real world, where the risks of a scraped knee or a lost afternoon were small and the lessons were large. And then we handed that same generation a device and left it underprotected in the virtual world, where the risks were enormous and the supervision was nil.

[PAUSE]

We kept them from the creek, and then we gave them the abyss.

Let me tell you a true story about how far we went.

In Silver Spring, Maryland, there lived a family named Meitiv. Danielle and Alexander Meitiv believed in letting their children, a ten-year-old and a six-year-old, walk home from the park by themselves. Not across a continent. Home from the park.

In December of two thousand fourteen, police stopped the children on their walk and drove them home. Child Protective Services opened a neglect investigation against the parents.

In April of two thousand fifteen, it happened again. Someone called the police about children alone in a park. This time the police took the children into custody, and they were held by Child Protective Services for hours without being allowed to contact their parents.

[LET IT LAND]

Children were seized by the state for the offense of walking home from the park. That is how deeply the fear had sunk into our institutions.

But here is the good news, and it is genuinely good news. The country noticed. In June of two thousand fifteen, Maryland officials issued a directive saying that Child Protective Services should not get involved unless a child had been harmed or faced substantial risk of harm.

And a movement began. The journalist Lenore Skenazy had already written a book called Free-Range Kids, in two thousand nine, arguing that we had become too afraid to let children be children. Organizations like Let Grow took up the cause.

In two thousand eighteen, Utah became the first state to pass a law protecting parents' right to give their children reasonable independence: to let them walk to school, play outside, or stay home alone without being accused of neglect. Oklahoma and Texas followed. As of this year, thirteen states have enacted such laws. And in two thousand twenty-six, Representatives Blake Moore and Jennifer McClellan, one a Republican and one a Democrat, introduced a federal bill modeled on them.

[PAUSE]

I want you to notice something about this reform. It does not cost money. It does not require a new agency. It does not censor anyone. It simply tells parents: the law will not punish you for trusting your children.

That is the spirit I want to bring to everything else I will propose tonight. Not fear. Not control. Trust, wisely given, in the real world, where children can grow.

[BUILD]

So what does a play-based childhood look like in two thousand twenty-six?

It looks like a school that keeps its playground open before and after classes and lets children run on it without an adult organizing every game. Some schools have tried exactly this.

It looks like a parent who gives an eight-year-old an errand to run alone at the corner store, or a ten-year-old the job of walking a younger sibling to the library.

It looks like a neighborhood where three or four families agree that the kids can roam the block together after school, so that no child is alone and no parent is the odd one out.

It looks like summer camps without screens, scouting troops, sports leagues where the kids pick the teams, and yes, some boredom. Boredom is not a crisis. Boredom is the soil in which imagination grows.

There is a deeper idea here, and it is worth stating plainly. Some things grow stronger only when they are tested. A muscle that is never used wastes away. A bone that never bears weight grows brittle. An immune system that never meets a germ overreacts to everything. And a child who is never allowed to face a small fear, a small conflict, a small failure, grows up with no experience of overcoming one.

When such a child meets the first real hardship of adolescence, a breakup, a betrayal, a bad grade, a cruel comment in a group chat, she has no evidence from her own life that she can survive it. Of course she is anxious. We have protected her from every chance to learn that she is strong.

And it looks like adults who are willing to tolerate a little risk, because they understand that a childhood without any risk is a childhood without any growth.

[PAUSE]

If Peter Gray is right that lost freedom is the deeper cause, then this is the remedy. If Jonathan Haidt is right that the phone is the deeper cause, then this is still the remedy, because every hour of real play is an hour not spent in the feed.

The two schools of thought disagree about the diagnosis. They agree about the prescription. And when the doctors agree about the prescription, the patient should not wait for them to finish arguing about the diagnosis.

Give the children back the kingdom of play.

## VIII. The Machine Built to Hold Them

Now I must speak about the companies. And I want to begin by saying what I am not saying.

I am not saying that the engineers in California set out to make children miserable. I do not believe that. I believe most of them are decent people who love their own children. I am not saying that social media has brought nothing good into the world. It has connected isolated young people with others like them. It has given voice to the voiceless. It has kept grandparents in touch with grandchildren across oceans.

But I am saying this. The business model of most of these platforms is advertising. Advertising revenue depends on attention. And so the platforms are built, refined, tested and retested, to capture as much attention as possible, from as many users as possible, for as long as possible.

[PAUSE]

When that machine meets an adult, the adult has at least a fully developed brain with which to resist it. When that machine meets a twelve-year-old, whose capacity for self-control is still years from maturity, it is not a fair fight.

Notice what the business model counts and what it does not count. It counts minutes. It counts sessions. It counts daily active users and how often they return. It does not count hours of sleep lost. It does not count homework not done, or friends not called, or the slow erosion of a child's belief that she is enough. Those costs are real, but they do not appear on anyone's balance sheet. Economists have a word for costs like that, which one party creates and another party pays. They call them externalities. Pollution is the classic example. The factory profits, and the people downstream breathe the smoke.

Our children are downstream.

Consider the infinite feed. In the old world, a newspaper ended. A television episode ended. A magazine had a last page. Those endings were natural stopping points, moments when you looked up and asked yourself whether you wanted more. The infinite feed abolished the stopping point. There is no last page. There is always one more.

Consider autoplay. The next video begins before you have decided whether you want to watch it. The default is to continue. To stop, you must act.

Consider the notification. A buzz, a banner, a red dot with a number in it. Each one an interruption, each one a summons back to the app, timed and tuned by systems that learn what brings each particular user back.

[BUILD]

For years, the companies insisted that these were neutral design choices, and that whatever harm came to young people came from the content other users posted, for which, under a federal law called Section two thirty, the platforms bear no liability.

But this year, for the first time, a jury of ordinary Americans was asked to judge that claim. And it rejected it.

On the twenty-fifth of March, two thousand twenty-six, in Los Angeles, a jury returned its verdict in the first bellwether trial over social media addiction. The plaintiff was a twenty-year-old woman identified in court by her initials, K.G.M. She testified that she began using YouTube at six years old, and Instagram at nine. She said she was on social media all day as a child, and that her use made her mental health struggles worse.

Her lawyers did not ask the jury to judge any particular post or video. The judge instructed the jury to disregard the content, because of Section two thirty. Instead, they argued that the platforms themselves were designed to hook young users: the infinite feeds, the autoplay, the notifications.

The companies fought hard. Meta argued that her struggles came from a turbulent home life, and that no therapist had named social media as the cause. YouTube argued that it is a video service, more like television than like social media. Mark Zuckerberg himself took the stand.

[PAUSE]

The jury deliberated for nearly forty-four hours over nine days. And it found Meta and YouTube negligent in the design or operation of their platforms, and found that they had failed to warn users of the dangers. It awarded three million dollars in compensatory damages, and three million more in punitive damages, with Meta responsible for seventy percent and YouTube for thirty. Two other defendants, TikTok and Snap, had settled before trial.

Evidence aired in that case included an email from two thousand sixteen in which Mark Zuckerberg, discussing a live video feature, wrote that the company would need to be "very good about not notifying parents / teachers" about teenagers' videos.

[LET IT LAND]

Not notifying parents. Read that sentence again, and then ask yourself whose side the design was on.

One week earlier, in New Mexico, a separate jury had found Meta liable in a case brought by that state's attorney general, finding that the company had engaged in unfair and deceptive practices that harmed children. The penalty was three hundred and seventy-five million dollars.

Both companies have said they disagree and are considering appeals, and I want to be fair: verdicts can be overturned, and a jury is not a laboratory. But the legal ground has shifted. Hundreds of other cases, brought by families, school districts and attorneys general, are waiting behind these two. Some legal experts have compared the moment to the early days of the tobacco and opioid litigation.

[PAUSE]

I want to tell you about one more young person, because she should never be forgotten.

Her name was Molly Russell. She lived in Harrow, in northwest London. In November of two thousand seventeen, at fourteen years old, she took her own life.

In two thousand twenty-two, after a long inquest, the coroner, Andrew Walker, concluded that Molly died from an act of self-harm while suffering from depression and the negative effects of online content.

[SLOW]

The negative effects of online content. A coroner, a sober official sworn to establish the cause of death, wrote that phrase into the official record of a child's death.

Her case has been cited as one of the forces that moved Britain to pass its Online Safety Act. Her family has had to live with that sentence ever since, so that other families might not have to.

When we talk about the design of these machines, we are not talking about abstractions. We are talking about Molly. We are talking about K.G.M. We are talking about the girl under the covers at two in the morning.

[BUILD]

And so I say to the companies, with respect but without apology: you have some of the most brilliant people in the world on your payrolls. You have built systems that can predict what a child will watch next with astonishing accuracy. Do not tell us that you cannot build systems that know when a child should go to sleep. Do not tell us you cannot verify an age, when you can identify a face. Do not tell us safety is impossible, when what you mean is that safety is unprofitable.

You know how to make these products safer. Your own people have told you so. Your own researchers have written it in your own slides. The only question left is whether you will do it because it is right, or whether you will wait until juries and legislatures make you.

## IX. The Schoolhouse Door

Of all the places where we might begin to raise our guard, the most obvious is the one where we already require children to spend seven hours a day: the school.

[PAUSE]

Think about what we ask of a teacher in a classroom where every student has a phone in their pocket. We ask her to compete, with a lesson on the causes of the First World War or the structure of a cell, against a device engineered by thousands of the world's best designers to be more interesting than anything else in the room. We ask her to win that contest forty times a day, five days a week, for a whole year.

And then we wonder why she is exhausted, and why the students are distracted.

Think too about the hallway, and the lunchroom, and the bathroom. In the old days, lunch was loud. It was full of gossip and teasing and flirting and argument. It was where children practiced, clumsily, the art of being with one another. Ask a teacher today what lunch looks like in a school without phone rules, and many will describe a quieter room, full of bowed heads.

And think about the fight in the hallway, filmed and posted before the bell rings. The cruel photograph shared in a group chat during third period. The child who cannot escape her tormentors even in the middle of a math lesson, because they are in her pocket.

[BUILD]

For years, schools let this happen because they thought they had no choice. Parents wanted to reach their children. Students protested. Enforcement was a burden. And so the phone came to class.

But in the last three years, something remarkable has happened. The barbarian has, at least in this one place, begun to raise his guard.

In May of two thousand twenty-three, Florida adopted the first statewide school cellphone restriction in the country. Other states followed, and then many others.

As of a tracker updated just this month, forty-three states and the District of Columbia have a statewide law, executive order or board policy requiring schools to restrict student phones. Twenty-three states and the District of Columbia require what is called a bell-to-bell rule, meaning phones are put away for the entire school day, not just during class, though some apply only to certain grades or start in a later year. Counts vary depending on who is doing the counting, but the direction is unmistakable.

[PAUSE]

And here is something even more remarkable, in a country where we agree on almost nothing. This is not a partisan issue. Red states and blue states alike have acted. The Harvard political scientist Paul Peterson, who has studied these bans, put it plainly: this is not controversial along partisan lines. Surveys he cites find that seventy percent of school principals, and eighty-one percent of middle and high school principals, support the bans.

The rest of the world has moved too. France banned phones for students aged three to fifteen in two thousand eighteen. The Netherlands banned phones in secondary school classrooms in January of two thousand twenty-four. Finland, New Zealand and Brazil have acted, as have most of the regions of Spain. According to UNESCO, the share of countries with school phone bans rose from twenty-three percent to forty percent in a single year, between two thousand twenty-three and two thousand twenty-four.

[PAUSE]

Now, do the bans work? I promised honesty, so here it is.

The best early evidence we have is about learning. A study of Florida's ban found that student learning improved. But it also found that in the first year, suspensions rose, which Peterson suggests reflects students refusing to comply with teachers' requests. In the second year, suspensions fell back to where they had been, and the learning gains continued. A larger national study of about five thousand schools, released by the National Bureau of Economic Research, found the same pattern: an initial rise in suspensions, then positive effects in the second year, though smaller than in Florida, likely because schools enforced the rules unevenly.

In Peterson's words, the benefits persist into the second year, while the negative side fades.

What about mental health? Here I must be careful. The effects of phone bans on students' social and emotional well-being are harder to measure, and the evidence so far is limited and mixed. I will not stand here and tell you that locking up phones from eight to three has been proven to cure teenage depression. It has not.

[BUILD]

But consider what we do know. We know that students learn more. We know that the disruption is temporary. We know that teachers, who see these children every day, overwhelmingly want the change. And we know that a school day without phones gives every child seven hours of something precious: a place where they are free from the feed, where they look at faces instead of screens, where the social life of adolescence happens in person, the way it always did before.

If that were all a phone ban accomplished, it would be enough.

And I want to say something about how to do it well, because a badly implemented ban will fail and give the whole cause a bad name.

First, the phones have to actually go away. A rule that says phones must be silent and in pockets is not a rule; it is a suggestion, and every teacher knows it. The schools that report the best results lock phones away, in pouches or lockers, from the first bell to the last.

Second, the rule must be enforced by the school, not by each individual teacher. It is not fair to make a single teacher the villain in every classroom. The policy has to come from the principal's office, consistently, every day.

Third, parents must be part of it. Many parents resist bans because they want to reach their children in an emergency. That fear is understandable, and it deserves a real answer: a clear plan for how families can reach students through the school office, and how students can reach families when they need to.

I know that for many parents the fear is sharper than an ordinary emergency. It is the fear of the worst day a school can have. That fear deserves respect, not ridicule. But it also deserves an honest answer: in a true crisis, what keeps children safe is following the instructions of the adults in the building, quickly and quietly. A room full of students on their phones, distracted, lit up, calling out, is not safer. School safety experts and administrators have to make these plans with care, and parents deserve to hear those plans in detail. But the fear of a rare catastrophe should not decide how every child spends every ordinary day.

[PAUSE]

And fourth, we should be honest with students about why we are doing it. Not as punishment. Not because we think they are bad. But because we believe they deserve seven hours a day of a childhood that belongs to them, and not to an algorithm.

There is a lesson in the speed with which this reform has spread. Three years ago, a statewide phone ban was a fringe idea. Today, it is the law in most of the country. Change is possible. Change can be fast. The assembly can act when it chooses to.

The question is whether we will stop at the schoolhouse door.

## X. The Australian Experiment

On the far side of the world, a nation decided not to stop at the schoolhouse door.

[PAUSE]

In November of two thousand twenty-four, the Parliament of Australia passed a law called the Online Safety Amendment, the Social Media Minimum Age Act. It passed the House of Representatives by a vote of one hundred and one to thirteen. It cleared both chambers on the twenty-ninth of November.

The law requires major social media platforms to take reasonable steps to prevent Australians under sixteen from holding accounts. It does not punish children or parents. It places the burden on the companies, which face fines of up to forty-nine and a half million Australian dollars. And unlike many proposals in the United States, it contains no exception for parental consent. A parent cannot sign a form to let a fourteen-year-old onto the platforms.

After a year of preparation, the restrictions took effect on the tenth of December, two thousand twenty-five. They cover Facebook, Instagram, Snapchat, Threads, TikTok, X, YouTube, Reddit, Twitch and Kick. Messaging services like WhatsApp, and gaming platforms like Roblox, were left outside its scope.

[BUILD]

What happened next is the most important real-world test we have of whether a democracy can actually draw an age line around social media.

In the first weeks, according to Australia's online safety regulator, the eSafety Commissioner, the platforms restricted access to about four point seven million accounts identified as belonging to children under sixteen. Meta alone reported that, by the end of June of this year, it had removed access to more than seven hundred and fifty thousand accounts on Instagram and Facebook in Australia.

Those are large numbers. They tell us that when a law puts real money on the line, companies that once said age limits were impossible to enforce can suddenly find millions of underage accounts.

[PAUSE]

But I promised you honesty, and the Australian story is not a simple triumph.

Children found ways around the restrictions. Some lied about their age. Some used tools to disguise their location. Reports from early this year described many young Australians still reaching the platforms. A summary of the regulator's first compliance report found that around seven in ten children who had accounts on the major platforms still had them, mostly because the platforms had never asked them their age.

The regulator flagged serious concerns that some companies had not taken reasonable steps. In March of this year, the government announced investigations into Facebook, Instagram, Snapchat, TikTok and YouTube. In June, the Prime Minister accused the platforms of doing the bare minimum and promised a tougher approach, and the government has proposed higher penalties and stronger enforcement powers.

Public support, which was high when the law passed, has fallen. One poll found support at sixty-nine percent in July of two thousand twenty-four, and fifty-seven percent in December of two thousand twenty-five. Most teenagers surveyed opposed the ban. The eSafety Commissioner herself, Julie Inman Grant, has said she was not keen on a ban, and called the legislation very thin scaffolding.

And the law faces a challenge in Australia's High Court, brought by a group called the Digital Freedom Project on behalf of two fifteen-year-olds, arguing that it violates the implied constitutional freedom of political communication. Reddit has filed a challenge of its own.

[PAUSE]

So what should we learn from Australia?

The critics will say: it does not work. Children get around it. Leave it alone.

I say: look at what we are actually seeing. A law passed. Millions of accounts were closed. Enforcement has been imperfect, and the government is responding not by giving up but by tightening the rules. This is what the early years of every serious public health law look like.

[BUILD]

When we raised the drinking age, teenagers still found beer. When we banned tobacco sales to minors, teenagers still found cigarettes. When we required seat belts, many people did not wear them for years. Nobody argued that we should therefore let twelve-year-olds buy whiskey at the corner store.

A law does not have to be perfectly obeyed to change the norm. What the age limit does, above all, is take away the most powerful argument a child has ever made to a parent: but everyone else has it.

When the law says sixteen, the parent who says no is no longer the strange one. The child who waits is no longer the outcast. The pressure that forced every family to give in, because every other family had given in, begins to lift.

That is what Haidt calls a collective action problem. No single family can solve it alone, because no family wants its child to be the only one left out. But a community can solve it together, and a law is one of the ways a community acts together.

[PAUSE]

Australia's experiment is not finished. We should watch it closely and learn from its mistakes. We should notice that an age line without effective, privacy-respecting age verification is a line drawn in sand. We should notice that public patience wears thin when enforcement lags. And we should notice that the companies have every incentive to do the minimum, and that only steady pressure from government changes that.

There is also a lesson about where to check age. Meta has said it would prefer that app stores handle age verification, and in our own country, Texas has now put a law into effect requiring app stores to verify ages. Whatever you think of any particular statute, the idea behind it is worth serious debate: verify a child's age once, on the device or in the store where apps are downloaded, rather than asking every single app to do it separately, and collecting a child's personal information a hundred times over. A system like that could be both more effective and more private than what we have now. It is the kind of practical design question that a serious country should be working on, rather than arguing endlessly about whether to act at all.

But the larger lesson is this. A democracy looked at the evidence, weighed the risks, and acted, without waiting for perfect certainty. It is now doing the hard work of making its action effective.

That is what a city does when it stops boxing like a barbarian.

## XI. The Long Delay in Washington

And what of our own capital?

[PAUSE]

In February of two thousand twenty-two, a Democratic senator from Connecticut, Richard Blumenthal, and a Republican senator from Tennessee, Marsha Blackburn, introduced a bill called the Kids Online Safety Act. It would have required platforms to take reasonable care to protect minors from harms such as bullying, self-harm content and sexual exploitation, and to give them ways to turn off addictive features.

It advanced in committee. It did not get a vote.

In two thousand twenty-three, they introduced it again. It advanced in committee again.

On the thirtieth of July, two thousand twenty-four, the United States Senate passed it, combined with an update to the children's online privacy law, by a vote of ninety-one to three.

[LET IT LAND]

Ninety-one to three. In a Senate that cannot agree on the time of day, ninety-one senators of both parties agreed that our children needed protection.

And then the bill went to the House of Representatives, and it died. A planned markup was canceled. A subcommittee advanced a version. Nothing more happened before the Congress ended.

In May of two thousand twenty-five, the bill was introduced again.

[PAUSE]

Do you see the pattern? Struck, we clutch. A hearing. A bill. A committee vote. A floor vote in one chamber. And then the guard drops, and the session ends, and we begin again.

This year, the House finally acted. On the twenty-ninth of June, two thousand twenty-six, it passed a bill called the Kids Internet and Digital Safety Act, by a bipartisan vote of two hundred and sixty-seven to one hundred and seventeen. It would extend federal children's privacy protections, which currently stop at age twelve, to teenagers up to seventeen, and bar targeted advertising aimed at them.

That is real progress, and the members who negotiated it deserve credit.

But in the negotiations, something important was dropped: the duty of care. That is the provision that would have required companies to design their platforms to be safe for children by default, to take responsibility for what their products do, not just for what they collect. Senator Blumenthal warned that leaving it out would weaken the protections. Advocates for children's safety urged that it be restored. The bill's defenders, including Representative Frank Pallone, argued that it sets a floor rather than a ceiling, and that states can still pass stronger laws.

That question, whether a federal law should be a floor or a ceiling, matters more than it sounds. If Congress writes a weak national rule and forbids the states from going further, it can freeze protection at the lowest common denominator for a generation. If Congress writes a national floor and lets the states build on it, the states can keep doing what they have done so well on school phones: experimenting, learning from one another, and moving faster than Washington. Whatever the Senate does, it should not slam the door on the laboratories of democracy that have carried this fight while Congress waited.

As I speak, the bill awaits the Senate, where it will need sixty votes, and where its own former champions are unhappy with what it has become.

[BUILD]

Meanwhile, the states have not waited. Florida has a law restricting social media accounts for young teenagers, which a federal appeals court allowed to take effect last November while the litigation continues. Ohio's parental notification law was struck down by a federal judge and then upheld by an appeals court this June. Texas has a law requiring app stores to verify ages, which took effect this summer. California has restrictions on addictive feeds for minors that an appeals court largely upheld.

But many other state laws have been blocked by the courts. Arkansas's law was permanently struck down. Louisiana's was enjoined. A Virginia law that would have limited minors to one hour a day on social media was ruled a violation of the First Amendment this February.

[PAUSE]

And here, again, I owe you the other side.

The opponents of these laws are not all lobbyists for the tech industry. The American Civil Liberties Union, the Electronic Frontier Foundation, and others have warned that vague definitions of harm could lead platforms to censor lawful speech, including information that vulnerable young people need. Some advocates for gay and transgender youth have feared that a duty of care could be used to block content about their identities, cutting off isolated teenagers from communities that sustain them. Others worry that age verification will require everyone, adults included, to hand over identification or biometric data, creating new risks to privacy.

These are serious concerns. The First Amendment is not a technicality. A law that protects children by silencing speech, or by building a surveillance system around every user, could do real harm of its own.

[BUILD]

But notice that these are arguments about how to write the law, not about whether to write one. And they can be answered.

It helps to understand why design is the key. For thirty years, a federal law known as Section two thirty has shielded online platforms from liability for what their users post. Whatever its merits, it has meant that whenever a child was harmed, the companies could say: we did not create that content; someone else did.

But a platform is not only a bulletin board where other people pin their notes. It is also an architecture. Someone decided that the feed would never end. Someone decided that the next video would play automatically. Someone decided when and how often to send a notification to a fourteen-year-old's phone. Those are not other people's words. Those are the company's own choices.

We can focus on design rather than content. A law that regulates infinite scroll, autoplay, nighttime notifications and engagement-maximizing recommendation systems for minors does not need to say a single word about what ideas young people may encounter. The Los Angeles jury was able to judge design without judging content. So can Congress.

We can build age assurance that protects privacy. Australia's law forbids companies from relying solely on government identification. Age verification can happen once, at the level of the device or the app store, rather than separately on every website, and without handing a copy of a child's passport to every app.

We can protect the young people who most need online community, by drawing the line at the features that exploit rather than the speech that connects.

[PAUSE]

What we cannot do is keep waiting. Since that bill was first introduced in February of two thousand twenty-two, a child who was then in fifth grade is now nearly old enough to drive. An entire middle school career has passed. The children for whom the bill was written have grown up waiting for it.

To the members of the United States Senate, I say: you voted ninety-one to three. You knew then what was at stake. You know now. Finish the work.

## XII. The Boxer's Habit

Let me return now to the old image, because I think it explains our failure better than any statistic.

[PAUSE]

Demosthenes did not tell the Athenians that they were weak. Quite the opposite. He reminded them that they had more ships, more soldiers, more money and more allies than any city in Greece. Their problem was not a lack of power. Their problem was a habit. They used their power only after they had been hurt, only where they had been hurt, and only until the pain faded.

That is our habit too.

Think of how this story has unfolded in our country. Each time, the blow lands, and we clutch.

A whistleblower brings documents to the Senate, and we hold hearings. The hearings end, and we forget.

A coroner in London writes the words "negative effects of online content" into the record of a fourteen-year-old's death, and we are horrified for a week. Then the next news cycle comes.

The Senate votes ninety-one to three, and we celebrate. Then the bill dies in the House, and almost no one notices.

The CDC publishes numbers showing that more than half of our teenage girls are persistently sad or hopeless, and the headlines blaze. Then the next numbers show slight improvement, and we are tempted to declare victory.

[BUILD]

And here I want to speak directly to the temptation of this moment, because I believe it is the most dangerous temptation of all.

The numbers are getting better. I have told you so myself. Teen depression is down from its peak. Persistent sadness is down. Serious thoughts of suicide are down. And there will be voices, some of them well-meaning, who say: you see, the crisis is passing. The kids are adapting. We overreacted. Let us move on.

[PAUSE]

In Athens, too, there were seasons of good news. Philip would turn from one front to another, and the pressure would seem to ease. And each time, the assembly breathed a sigh of relief and returned to its festivals.

And each time, Philip came back stronger.

I am not saying the technology will come back stronger. I am saying it is already changing, and faster than our laws. The smartphone of two thousand twelve is not the smartphone of two thousand twenty-six. The feeds are more personalized. The recommendation systems are more powerful. Short, endless video has conquered platform after platform. And now artificial intelligence has arrived, with chatbot companions that can talk to a lonely child at any hour of the night, in any voice, saying whatever keeps her talking.

Think about what that means for a lonely thirteen-year-old. Not a feed of other people's lives to compare herself against, but a voice that is always available, always agreeable, always ready to tell her what she wants to hear. It may comfort her. It may also teach her that relationships require nothing of her, that a friend is something you can summon and dismiss, that the friction and patience of real human love are optional.

We do not yet know what those companions will do to a generation. I will not pretend to know. But I know this: if we wait for a decade of data before we ask the question, we will be asking it about children who have already grown up.

[SLOW]

The improvement in the numbers is not a reason to lower our guard. It may be the first evidence that raising our guard works. In the very years when the numbers improved, parents began delaying phones, schools began locking them away, states began passing laws, and companies, under pressure, began building teen protections they had resisted for years. I cannot prove that these changes caused the improvement. No one yet can. But it would be a strange lesson to draw from a fighter finally landing a defensive block that he should immediately drop his hands.

[PAUSE]

There is another part of the boxer's habit, and it is subtler. The barbarian fighter not only reacts late. He reacts only to the most recent blow. He forgets the earlier ones.

We have done that too. When the conversation was about cyberbullying, we talked only about cyberbullying. When it was about body image, we talked only about body image. When it was about sleep, only sleep. When it was about a tragic death, only that death. Each harm is treated as its own small emergency, to be addressed with its own small fix.

But these are not separate blows. They come from the same opponent. They are the results of a single change: the decision, made by all of us without ever quite deciding it, to hand children a device that connects them to everything, all the time, before they are ready.

A serious response treats it as a single challenge. It thinks ahead. It watches the opponent rather than the bruise.

[BUILD]

Demosthenes asked his countrymen a question that I will ask you now, in my own words. When will you act? When you are forced to? What greater force do you need than the danger already in front of you?

For a free people, he argued, the greatest compulsion should be the shame of having failed to act when they could have.

[LET IT LAND]

We are a free people. We have the power. We have the knowledge, or enough of it. We lack only the habit of using what we have before the next blow lands.

So let me tell you how we change the habit.

## XIII. What Each of Us Must Do

Demosthenes did not only warn. He proposed. In his first speech against Philip, he did not stop at saying that Athens was in danger. He laid out a plan: how many ships, what kind of force, how it would be paid for, where it would be stationed. He believed that an orator who only frightens his audience has done half his job.

So let me do the other half. Let me speak to each of you in turn.

[PAUSE]

To parents first.

You are not to blame for the world your children were born into. You did not design the infinite feed. You did not decide that a thirteen-year-old could sign up for an account with a click. You were handed a choice that looked like no choice at all: give your child the phone, or watch them be excluded from the social life of their class.

But you have more power than you think, and you have more allies than you know.

Delay the smartphone. Haidt's first norm is no smartphones before high school. That does not mean no phone at all. A basic phone that calls and texts lets your child reach you and reach their friends, without putting the entire internet and every social platform in their pocket. Give them that, and give them the smartphone later, when they are better equipped to handle it.

Delay social media. Haidt's second norm is no social media before sixteen. The platforms themselves set thirteen as their minimum age, and that number was never chosen because thirteen-year-olds were ready. It came from a privacy law written before the smartphone existed.

[BUILD]

And most important of all: do not do it alone.

The reason so many parents give in is that no one wants their child to be the only one without a phone. So do not let your child be the only one. Talk to the parents of your child's friends. Talk to the other parents in the class. Agree together to wait. There are pledges and parent networks built exactly for this purpose. One of them asks families to wait until eighth grade, and Haidt has argued it should be renamed wait until ninth.

Ten families who agree to wait together are stronger than one family standing alone. A whole grade that agrees to wait has changed the norm.

[PAUSE]

Keep phones out of bedrooms at night. This may be the single simplest rule that any family can adopt, and it goes straight at one of the four harms, the loss of sleep. Buy an old-fashioned alarm clock. Charge every device, including your own, in the kitchen.

And here is the hardest part, so I will say it gently. Look at your own phone. Our children learn far more from what we do than from what we say. If we tell them to put their phones down while we scroll at the dinner table, they will learn exactly what we have taught them.

Finally, parents, give your children back some freedom. Let them walk to a friend's house. Let them ride their bike to the park. Let them run an errand. Let them be bored. Let them fail at small things while the stakes are small. Every hour of real-world independence is an hour in which they are building the confidence that the feed cannot give them.

[PAUSE]

To teachers and principals.

You have already begun. You have led this country's most successful reform on this issue in the last three years. Keep going. Make your schools phone-free from the first bell to the last, with the phones truly locked away. Enforce the policy from the principal's office, so that no teacher stands alone. Communicate with parents. And expect a hard first year, knowing that the evidence suggests the second will be better.

And bring back play. Protect recess. Lengthen it if you can. Open the playground before and after school. Let the children organize their own games. Resist the temptation to fill every minute with adult instruction. Some of the most important lessons a child learns at school are learned when no teacher is watching.

[BUILD]

To state legislators.

If your state does not yet have a bell-to-bell phone policy, pass one. If it does, make sure it is funded and enforced.

Pass a reasonable childhood independence law, so that no parent is investigated for letting a child walk to the park.

And when you write laws about social media, write them carefully. Focus on design, not content, so that they will survive in the courts and respect the freedom of speech. Regulate the features that exploit young minds: the endless feed, the autoplay, the overnight notifications, the engagement-driven recommendations. Insist on age assurance that protects privacy. Learn from the laws that have been struck down, and from the ones that have survived.

[PAUSE]

To members of Congress.

You have already done the hard part. You have built bipartisan majorities in both chambers. You have heard the testimony. You have seen the documents. You have read the verdicts.

Senators: pass a children's online safety bill, and restore a duty of care that holds companies responsible for the design of their products. Representatives: work with the Senate to resolve the differences, rather than letting another Congress end in failure. Do not let the perfect be the enemy of the good, and do not let the good be watered down until it is no good at all.

[PAUSE]

To doctors, counselors, and everyone who cares for children who are already struggling.

Nothing I have said tonight replaces you. If the critics are right that the causes of this crisis are many, then the children already suffering need help with all of them, and they need it now. A phone-free school does not help the girl who is already self-harming unless there is a counselor in that school who has the time to see her.

This year, for the first time, the CDC's national youth survey asked students who reported persistent sadness or hopelessness whether they had received counseling or therapy. That we are only now asking that question tells you how far we have to go. Every legislature that passes a phone law should ask, in the same session, whether its schools have enough counselors, whether its children can find a therapist who takes their family's insurance, and whether a teenager in crisis can get a bed in a hospital without waiting in an emergency room for days.

Prevention and treatment are not rivals. A city that builds walls still needs physicians. Let us do both.

[PAUSE]

To the technology companies.

You have the talent and the resources to fix much of this tomorrow. Make the safest settings the default for every young user, not an option buried three menus deep. Turn off notifications at night for minors. Remove the infinite feed and autoplay from teen accounts. Stop recommending content about self-harm and extreme dieting to children. Work with each other, and with phone makers and app stores, to verify ages in a way that protects privacy.

And publish your research. When your own researchers find that your product makes body image worse for one in three teenage girls, the public deserves to know it before a whistleblower has to tell them.

[BUILD]

And finally, to the young people who are listening.

I know how this sounds to you. Another adult, wagging a finger, telling you that your world is bad for you. Most of the teenagers surveyed in Australia opposed the ban there, and I understand why. Your friends are on these platforms. Your jokes are there. Your communities are there. Some of you have found people online who understood you when no one in your town did. I do not dismiss that.

But I want to ask you a question, and I want you to answer it honestly, not to me but to yourself. How do you feel when you put the phone down after two hours of scrolling? Better or worse?

Many of you have told your parents, and told each other, that you wish you used it less. That you feel worse afterward. That you wish you could quit but cannot, because everyone else is there.

[PAUSE]

That is not a weakness in you. That is a design working exactly as it was intended. And you are not alone in wanting out.

So be part of the change. Start a phone-free club. Organize a weekend without social media with your friends. Delete the app that makes you feel worst, and see what happens. Support the phone-free policy at your school rather than fighting it. Tell the adults in your life what you actually need.

Some of the strongest voices in this movement have been young people who looked at what the feed was doing to them and their friends, and decided to say so. Your generation may be the one that finally teaches the rest of us how to live with this technology, instead of under it.

## XIV. Before Chaeronea

I have one more story to tell you, and it is not a happy one.

[PAUSE]

Demosthenes spoke against Philip for more than a decade. He delivered his first great warning around three hundred and fifty-one before Christ. He delivered his third, perhaps the most powerful of all, ten years later. And in between, and after, he spoke again and again, in the assembly, in the courts, on embassies to other cities.

Sometimes the Athenians listened. More often, they listened, applauded, and went home. They sent too little, too late. They trusted promises that should not have been trusted. They hoped that the danger would pass on its own.

At last, when Philip was nearly at their doorstep, they acted. Demosthenes forged an alliance with Thebes, the old rival of Athens, and the two cities raised an army together.

In August of three hundred and thirty-eight before Christ, that army met the Macedonians on a plain near a town called Chaeronea. Philip led his army in person. At his side was his eighteen-year-old son, who would one day be called Alexander the Great.

[SLOW]

The Greek allies were crushed.

Demosthenes himself was there, not as a general but as an ordinary foot soldier, a man of words who had picked up a shield because he had asked his fellow citizens to do the same. And when the battle was lost, it was Demosthenes whom the city chose to deliver the funeral oration over the dead.

Imagine standing where he stood. Imagine looking out at the families of the fallen, knowing that you had warned them for years, and that the warning had come true, and that the action, when it finally came, had come too late.

[LET IT LAND]

Philip, as it turned out, treated Athens with surprising leniency. But the city was drawn into a league under Macedonian leadership, and the age when the free Greek cities decided their own fate was ending.

I tell you this story not to make you despair, but for the opposite reason. Because we are not standing at Chaeronea. Not yet.

Remember who Demosthenes was. According to the old stories, he was a boy with a weak voice and a stammer, laughed at the first time he tried to speak in public. He did not accept that verdict. He went underground, practiced alone, filled his mouth with pebbles, shouted over the waves, until the weakest voice in Athens became the strongest. The ancient writers who tell us this may have embellished it. But the point of the story has outlived its details: what is weak can be trained. What is undefended can learn to guard itself. A habit can be broken by a people that decides to break it.

[PAUSE]

We are standing in the assembly, in the years when the warnings are still being given and the choice is still being made. We are standing at the moment when action can still make the difference.

The numbers that rose after two thousand twelve are beginning to come down. Schools across this country have begun to lock away the phones. Nations on the other side of the world have drawn a line at sixteen. Juries have found that the design of these products can be judged. Parents have begun to talk to each other and to wait together. The House has passed a bill. The Senate has passed one before.

The guard is rising. The question is whether we will hold it up, or drop it the moment the pain fades.

[BUILD]

So let me speak to you now, not as a lecturer, but as one citizen to another.

We are not powerless. We are the richest, the freest, the most inventive society that has ever existed. We put a man on the moon. We built the very devices we are now trying to tame. Do not tell me that a people who could do all that cannot decide when a child should get a phone.

We are not ignorant. We have the surveys. We have the hospital records. We have the experiments. We have the companies' own words, in their own slides. We have the testimony of whistleblowers and the findings of coroners and the verdicts of juries. We do not know everything. We know enough.

We are not divided. On this, if on nothing else, Republicans and Democrats have voted together, ninety-one to three in the Senate and two hundred and sixty-seven to one hundred and seventeen in the House. Red states and blue states have locked up phones in their schools. Conservative and progressive parents sit side by side at the same school board meetings, asking for the same thing.

[PAUSE]

What we have lacked is not power, not knowledge, not unity. What we have lacked is the habit of acting before the blow lands.

So let us form that habit now.

Let every parent in this room go home and talk to three other families about waiting together.

Let every teacher go to school tomorrow and ask the principal whether the phones are truly away from the first bell to the last.

Let every legislator go back to the capitol and ask why the bill has not yet passed.

Let every engineer who builds these products go to work tomorrow and ask whether the feature they are building would be good for their own child at two in the morning.

And let every young person who has felt the pull of the endless scroll, and wished they could put it down, know that they are not alone, and that the adults are finally, belatedly, coming to help.

[BUILD]

Picture with me, one last time, that girl under the covers at two in the morning. The composite girl, who is also every girl, who is also, perhaps, someone in your own house.

Now picture a different night.

Her phone is charging in the kitchen, next to her parents' phones. She is asleep, really asleep, the deep and dreamless sleep of a growing child. Tomorrow she will walk to school with two friends, the long way, past the creek. Her phone will spend the day in a locker. At lunch, she will laugh too loud at a joke that is not very funny. After school, she will go to a friend's house without asking anyone to drive her. She will argue with that friend and make up before dinner. She will be bored for an hour, and in that hour she will draw something, or write something, or simply think.

She will get her first smartphone when she starts high school. She will join social media at sixteen, when her friends do, because her friends' parents made the same decision her parents did. And when she does, the platforms she joins will have been designed, by law and by conscience, with her safety in mind.

[SLOW]

That girl is not a fantasy. Her childhood is not a fantasy. It is the childhood that almost every one of us in this room over the age of thirty actually had, with only the technology changed.

We can give it back. We know how. The only question is whether we will.

[PAUSE]

The Athenians had their chance, and they waited, and they lost a world.

We have our chance. It is here. It is now. It is in this room.

Let it never be said of us that we saw the danger, that we measured it, that we debated it, that we held hearings on it, and that we waited until it was too late.

Let it be said instead that when our children needed us, we raised our guard.

Let it be said that we stood together, parents and teachers, legislators and engineers, young and old, and that we chose to give the children of the screen their childhood back.

[LET IT LAND]

Thank you.
