# The Great Decoupling

## I. Two Lines on a Chart

Friends, I want to begin with two numbers from this year.

On the fifteenth of September, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that the share of American business output paid to the people who do the work had fallen to the lowest level it has ever recorded.

And this summer, four American companies told their investors that between them they would spend more than seven hundred billion dollars this year, most of it on the machinery of artificial intelligence.

[PAUSE]

A record low for work. A record high for the machines.

Tonight I am going to show you how those two numbers are connected, and why the connection should matter to anyone who works for a living. The story begins about fifty years ago. And to tell it, I need a picture. Not a photograph. A chart. Two lines on a sheet of paper.

The first line measures how much an American worker produces in an hour. The second line measures how much a typical American worker is paid for that hour. That is all. Output and pay. What you make, and what you take home.

[PAUSE]

For a generation after the Second World War, those two lines climbed the page together. They rose like two climbers roped to one another on the same mountain. When one went up, the other followed. When the country got better at making things, the people who made them got better at living.

The Economic Policy Institute has put numbers on that climb. From nineteen forty-eight to nineteen seventy-three, net productivity in this country rose by about ninety-seven percent. Over the same years, the hourly pay and benefits of ordinary production and nonsupervisory workers rose by about ninety-one percent. Ninety-seven and ninety-one. Close enough to touch.

And then, somewhere in the nineteen-seventies, the rope was cut.

[SLOW]

The productivity line kept climbing. The pay line flattened. Not fell, flattened. It wandered sideways across the decades like a man who has lost the trail and is too tired to look for it. And between those two lines a gap opened, and the gap has been widening ever since.

That gap has a name. Economists call it the decoupling. I call it the great decoupling, because it is the largest quiet fact of American economic life in my lifetime, and maybe in yours.

[LET IT LAND]

Consider what that gap contains. It contains every raise that was earned and never paid. It contains the second job your mother took. It contains the house your father could have bought on one income and you cannot buy on two. It contains the pension that became a four-oh-one-k and the four-oh-one-k that became a prayer. It contains the town whose factory did not close because it failed, but because it succeeded, and the success was shipped somewhere else.

The gap is not a mystery to the people living inside it. They feel it every month. They feel it at the gas pump and the pharmacy counter and the rent office. What they have not always been told is that the gap was not an act of God. It was not weather. It was the result of choices, choices about law, about taxes, about who gets to bargain and who does not, about who owns the machines and who merely tends them.

I have come here tonight to make an argument about those two lines. And my argument is this.

[BUILD]

We are standing at the beginning of the largest investment boom in the history of this republic. The companies building artificial intelligence are pouring money into data centers, chips and power plants at a scale that makes the railroads look modest. They tell us this technology will make us more productive than any generation in history. I believe them. I believe the productivity line is about to climb faster than it has climbed in fifty years.

But I ask you to look again at the chart.

If the productivity line goes up, and the pay line stays where it has been for fifty years, then what we call a gap today will become a chasm tomorrow. And the chasm will not be empty. It will be full. It will be full of returns, and those returns will flow to whoever owns the models, the chips and the data. Unless we decide otherwise, that will not be you.

[PAUSE]

So here is the whole argument in a sentence you can carry out of this room. If these machines make the country richer, and we leave the rules as they are, the new wealth will go to the people who own the machines and not to the people who do the work.

And here is the road we will travel tonight. First, how the rope between work and pay was tied, and how it was cut. Second, what the AI boom is about to pour into that gap, and who owns it. Third, the strongest case against me, and what history says about it. And last, what we can do about it, because the rope was tied by law once, and it can be tied again.

Now, I know how speeches like this one are usually received. Somebody stands up and says the rich are getting richer, and somebody else stands up and says that is the politics of envy. Somebody says the machines are coming for our jobs, and somebody else says people have been saying that since the spinning jenny and they have always been wrong.

I want to take both of those responses seriously tonight. I will give you the strongest case against my own argument, and I will answer it. I will tell you where the evidence is solid and where it is thin. I will not invent a number to make you angry. The real numbers are enough.

And I will not ask you to hate anyone. I am not here to tell you that the engineers building these systems are villains, or that the people who invest in them are wicked. Many of them are brilliant. Some of them are among the most honest voices warning us about what is coming. My quarrel is not with people. My quarrel is with a set of rules that sends the rewards of a nation's work into fewer and fewer hands, and with a habit of mind that treats those rules as if they were laws of nature.

In eighteen ninety-six, a thirty-six-year-old former congressman from Nebraska named William Jennings Bryan stood before a convention in Chicago and said that there were two ideas of how a nation grows prosperous. One idea held that if you legislate to make the well-to-do prosperous, in his words, "their prosperity will leak through on those below." The other idea held that you build prosperity from the bottom, and it finds its way up through every class that rests upon it.

That argument is a hundred and thirty years old. It has never been more urgent than it is right now. Because we are about to run the largest experiment in leak-through economics ever attempted. And I have come to tell you how the last fifty years of that experiment turned out.

## II. The Bargain That Built the Middle

To understand how the rope was cut, you first have to understand how it was tied. Because it was tied. It did not grow there.

Go back to January the fifth, nineteen fourteen. Detroit. The Ford Motor Company announces that it will pay qualifying workers five dollars a day. Before that announcement, the minimum daily pay for those workers had been two dollars and thirty-four cents. Ford more than doubled it overnight.

Now, Henry Ford was no saint. The five-dollar day came with strings. The company set up what it called a Social Department, with investigators who visited workers' homes to judge whether they drank too much or gambled or neglected their families. And the reason for the raise was not charity. It was turnover. The assembly line was so punishing that men walked off the job in droves. Some departments, by one account, hired three hundred men a year just to keep a hundred positions filled.

But hold on to the lesson inside that story, because it is the lesson of this whole speech. When the workers had leverage, when they could walk away and the company could not easily replace them, the wage went up. The wage did not rise because somebody calculated a fair share of productivity. It rose because the people doing the work had something the owner needed and could not get any other way.

[PAUSE]

For the next two decades, that leverage came and went. Then came the crash, and the Depression, and a quarter of the workforce out of work. And out of that catastrophe, this country did something it had never done before. It wrote the bargain down.

On July the fifth, nineteen thirty-five, Franklin Roosevelt signed the National Labor Relations Act, the Wagner Act. For the first time, federal law protected the right of workers to organize and to bargain collectively. In nineteen thirty-three, there were about three million union members in this country. By the end of that decade there were about eight million.

On June the twenty-fifth, nineteen thirty-eight, Roosevelt signed the Fair Labor Standards Act. A minimum wage. A forty-hour week, with time and a half for every hour beyond it. Limits on putting children into mines and factories.

None of that was a gift from the market. All of it was written into law by people who decided that a country's productivity is not only a private possession of whoever owns the factory, but a public achievement whose fruits ought to be broadly shared.

Roosevelt understood exactly what he was fighting. In his acceptance speech in Philadelphia in June of nineteen thirty-six, he told the nation that the revolutionaries of seventeen seventy-six had freed us from political tyranny, but that a new kind of tyranny had grown up in the age of the corporation and the great bank. He said that what had been called free enterprise had become, in his phrase, "privileged enterprise, not free enterprise." And he quoted an English judge who had put the whole matter in five words: "Necessitous men are not free men."

[SLOW]

Necessitous men are not free men.

Five words. A man who must take whatever wage is offered, because his children are hungry and there is no other job and no union and no floor, is not free in any sense that matters. He can vote. He can speak. He can worship. But in the place where he spends most of his waking life, he has no power at all.

The New Deal was an attempt to give him some.

And then came the war, and after the war, the boom. In nineteen fifty, Walter Reuther of the United Auto Workers sat down with General Motors and signed a five-year contract that Fortune magazine called the Treaty of Detroit. It kept a cost-of-living adjustment, so that inflation could not quietly eat the wage. It raised what was called the annual improvement factor, a raise every year tied to the idea that as the company grew more productive, the workers would share in it. It created a pension, fully funded and actuarially sound. It covered half the cost of health insurance for workers and their families. Ford and Chrysler soon followed.

Do you hear what that annual improvement factor was? It was the rope. It was the literal, contractual link between the productivity line and the pay line. Written down. Signed. Enforced.

That is how the rope was tied. Not by nature. By law.

[LET IT LAND]

That is the world in which those two lines climbed together. It was not a natural world. It was a negotiated world. It had serious failures. It shut out many Black workers and many women from its best jobs, and the South was largely left outside its union gains. I am not asking you to be nostalgic for nineteen fifty-five. I am asking you to notice how it worked.

It worked because three things were true at once. Workers had bargaining power, through law and through unions. The government set floors beneath which no one could be pushed. And the companies that grew rich needed vast numbers of people to make them rich. General Motors could not make cars without hundreds of thousands of workers on its lines. Its profits and its payroll rose together because they had to.

Hold on to that third condition. It is the one artificial intelligence is designed to remove.

## III. The Break

So what happened?

The honest answer is that many things happened at once, and economists still argue about how much weight to put on each. But the outline is not in dispute. Somewhere between nineteen seventy-three and nineteen seventy-nine, the two lines came apart, and they have never come back together.

Here are the numbers, again from the Economic Policy Institute. From nineteen seventy-nine to twenty twenty, net productivity in this country grew by about sixty-two percent. Over the same four decades, the hourly compensation of a typical worker, wages and benefits together, grew by about seventeen and a half percent. Productivity grew roughly three and a half times as fast as pay.

[PAUSE]

Let me put that in human terms. Imagine you work in a shop with ten people. Over forty years, new tools and better methods let the same ten people produce sixty percent more. That extra output is real. It is sold. It becomes money. And of that new money, a sliver finds its way into your paycheck, and the rest goes somewhere else.

Where did it go? It went up. It went to the top of the wage ladder, to executives and to a small professional class whose pay soared. And it went out of wages altogether, into profits, into dividends, into stock buybacks, into the value of the shares owned overwhelmingly by people who were already wealthy.

Now let me walk you through the causes, because the causes are what tell us whether artificial intelligence will make this better or worse.

The first cause is the collapse of bargaining power. In nineteen eighty-three, the first year the Bureau of Labor Statistics published comparable figures, about twenty percent of American wage and salary workers belonged to a union. Last year, in twenty twenty-five, it was ten percent. In the private sector, it was five point nine percent. Fewer than six private-sector workers in a hundred now have a union contract behind them.

Think back to the Treaty of Detroit and its annual improvement factor. That link between productivity and pay did not disappear because economists disproved it. It disappeared because the institutions that enforced it withered. When there is no one across the table, there is no table.

The second cause is automation. And here I turn to two of the most careful economists in the world, Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo, both of whom have studied this question for years. In a paper published through the National Bureau of Economic Research in twenty twenty-one, they found that between fifty and seventy percent of the changes in the American wage structure over the previous four decades can be traced to one thing: the falling relative wages of workers who specialized in routine tasks in industries undergoing rapid automation.

[SLOW]

Fifty to seventy percent.

Not trade alone, though trade mattered. Automation. Machines and software taking over the tasks in which particular groups of workers had their advantage. The welders and the typists and the bookkeepers and the switchboard operators and the machinists. Their tasks were handed to capital, and their wages were left behind.

And here is the part of their finding I cannot get past. Acemoglu and Restrepo found that these large changes in inequality came alongside only modest gains in productivity. In other words, much of the automation of the last forty years did not make the economy dramatically richer. It simply moved income from the people who did the work to the people who owned the machines that replaced them.

The third cause is concentration. In twenty seventeen, David Autor of MIT and his colleagues published a study of what they called superstar firms. Their finding was that in industry after industry, a small number of highly productive companies had captured a growing share of the market. Those companies earn high profits and employ relatively few workers per dollar of sales. As they grow, the share of the whole economy that goes to labor falls, not because any single company slashed its wages, but because the economy's weight shifted toward firms that need fewer people.

Picture the difference between a department store and an online marketplace, between a newspaper and a search engine. The new firm may be wonderful for consumers. But it can serve a nation with a fraction of the workforce the old one needed.

And the fourth cause is the price of machines themselves. Two economists, Loukas Karabarbounis and Brent Neiman, looked at the decline of labor's share not just in America but around the world. They found that it had fallen in most countries and most industries since the early nineteen-eighties. And they found that roughly half of that decline could be explained by one force: the falling price of investment goods, a fall often credited to information technology. When computers got cheaper, companies substituted them for people.

[PAUSE]

Now lay those four causes side by side. Weaker bargaining power. Automation of human tasks. A shift toward superstar firms that need few workers. And ever-cheaper machines that make substitution profitable.

I want you to ask yourself a simple question. Which of those four forces does artificial intelligence weaken?

[LET IT LAND]

None of them. It strengthens every one.

It strengthens automation, because it reaches tasks that no machine could touch before: writing, analysis, coding, customer service, design, translation, legal research, medical documentation. It strengthens the superstar firms, because the frontier models are built by a handful of companies with the money to put up a single data center campus worth tens of billions of dollars. It strengthens the substitution of capital for labor, because every year the cost of a given amount of machine intelligence falls. And it weakens bargaining power, because a worker who can be replaced by a subscription has very little to bargain with.

Every force that pulled those two lines apart for fifty years is about to be put on a stronger engine.

## IV. The Honest Objection

Now, I promised you the strongest case against me. Let me give you the first part of it right here, because it goes to the very chart I opened with.

There are serious economists who say the productivity-pay gap is exaggerated. One of the most distinguished was Martin Feldstein, who chaired the Council of Economic Advisers under Ronald Reagan. In two thousand eight he published a paper with a pointed title: did wages reflect growth in productivity? His answer was yes, mostly.

His argument went like this. When you compare productivity to pay, you have to measure them the same way. Productivity is measured in the prices of the things workers produce. Pay is usually adjusted by the prices of the things workers buy. Those price indexes are not the same. If you deflate both by the same index, and if you count total compensation, including health insurance and other benefits, not just wages, then from nineteen seventy to two thousand six, compensation per hour grew at about the same rate as productivity in the business sector. And labor's share of national income, he found, had stayed within a fairly narrow band, from about sixty-six percent to about sixty-four.

[PAUSE]

That is a real argument, made by a serious man, and I want you to understand it rather than wave it away. Three things are true about it.

First, Feldstein was measuring average compensation, and the gap I showed you is about typical compensation. An average can rise because the people at the top got enormous raises while the middle stood still. If one person in a room of ten gets a raise of a million dollars, the average raise in that room is a hundred thousand dollars, and nine people got nothing. Much of the measured gap between productivity and typical pay is exactly that: the difference between the average and the middle, the difference between the corner office and the shop floor.

Second, the benefits point is true, and it is also bitter. Yes, a larger share of compensation now goes to health insurance. But much of that increase reflects the soaring cost of American health care, not a richer package of care. A worker whose raise was swallowed by a rising insurance premium did not experience that as a raise. He experienced it as standing still while running.

Third, and this is the decisive point, the price-index debate is a debate about how to measure the gap. It is not a debate about whether labor's share of the national income is falling. And on that question, the data since Feldstein wrote his paper have spoken loudly.

Because the narrow band he described did not hold.

[SLOW]

Labor's share is the simplest measure of all. Of every dollar of output produced in the business sector, how many cents go to the people who work, in wages, salaries and benefits? It does not depend on which consumer price index you prefer. It is a share of the same pie, measured in the same dollars.

And when Feldstein looked at it in two thousand eight, it had wobbled but not collapsed. What has happened since is the subject of my next section. But I will tell you now: it fell through the floor of his narrow band and kept falling, until this year it reached the lowest level the government has ever recorded.

So when someone tells you the productivity-pay gap is a statistical illusion, you can say this. Fine. Set the gap aside. Set aside every argument about deflators and benefits and averages and medians. Look only at the share. Look only at how the pie is cut. And you will find that the cut going to work is smaller than at any time since the government began to measure it.

[PAUSE]

There is a second objection worth hearing, and I want to be fair to it too. Some will say: even if workers' share of income is falling, their absolute living standard is rising. Phones are better. Televisions are cheaper. Food takes a smaller share of a family's income than it did a generation ago.

All true. And I will not insult you by pretending that a family in twenty twenty-six lives no better than a family in nineteen seventy-three in every respect. But notice which things got cheaper and which things got dear. The things that can be made in a factory or delivered over a wire got cheaper. The things that make a middle-class life secure, a house, a college education, health care, child care, the ability to retire, got more expensive, sometimes far more expensive. A cheaper television is not a substitute for a down payment.

And notice something deeper. The question is not whether workers are better off than their grandparents. The question is whether they are getting a fair share of what they produce. A worker who produces sixty percent more and receives seventeen percent more has been shortchanged, whatever the price of a television. You do not answer a man who has been underpaid by pointing out that his shoes are more comfortable than his grandfather's.

## V. The Lowest Share Ever Recorded

I opened tonight with a short note from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Let me come back to it now. It was published on the fifteenth of September, and it was the kind of thing that appears on a government website in the morning and is forgotten by lunch. A few paragraphs and a chart.

It said that in the second quarter of twenty twenty-six, labor's share of output in the nonfarm business sector was fifty-two point eight percent. And it said, in the dry language of a statistical agency, that this was the lowest level ever recorded. The records go back to nineteen forty-seven.

[PAUSE]

Let me give you the shape of that history, because the shape is the story.

For most of the years after the Second World War, labor's share of business output sat between sixty and sixty-six percent. Its high point, according to the Bureau's own data, came in the fourth quarter of nineteen sixty, at sixty-six point two percent. Two of every three dollars produced by American business went to the people who worked there.

It bounced around through the decades. It sagged in some years and recovered in others. Then, as the Bureau itself describes it, a sustained decline began in the years after the turn of this century. It dropped substantially after the recession of two thousand one, and again after the great recession of two thousand seven through two thousand nine. During the pandemic, when output collapsed faster than payrolls, it briefly jumped to nearly fifty-nine percent. And then it resumed its fall.

At the end of last year, fifty-four point one percent. In the first quarter of this year, fifty-three point six. In the second quarter, fifty-two point eight.

[SLOW]

From two dollars in three, to barely more than one in two.

Let me make that concrete. More than thirteen percentage points, from the high of nineteen sixty to today, does not sound like much when you say it fast. But the nonfarm business sector produces many trillions of dollars of output every year. A shift of even a few points in how that output is divided moves hundreds of billions of dollars a year from paychecks to profits. Not once. Every year. Compounding.

That is not a rounding error. That is a transfer.

[PAUSE]

And here is what should worry us most. The latest drop is happening at a moment when productivity is rising. When Indeed's Hiring Lab looked at the first-quarter numbers this spring, it found that output per hour in the nonfarm business sector was up two point nine percent from a year earlier, the thirteenth straight quarter of positive year-over-year productivity growth. That is good news. That is the productivity line doing exactly what we want it to do.

But real hourly compensation was up only one point four percent over the same year. And it had actually fallen from the previous quarter.

So productivity was growing about twice as fast as real pay. The rope is still cut.

The Hiring Lab economists went further. They noted that the productivity gains seemed to be coming mainly through capital spending, through companies pouring money into equipment and software, rather than through broad improvements in efficiency. And they suggested, carefully, that the gains may be flowing to business owners and investors rather than to workers.

[LET IT LAND]

So this is not the old story of a stagnant economy where everyone suffers together. This is an economy that is getting more productive, quarter after quarter, in which the share going to work is shrinking quarter after quarter. The pie is growing. The worker's slice is not keeping pace.

In January, when the share had hit a then-record low of fifty-three point eight percent, a labor economist at Texas A&M named Raymond Robertson put it plainly to Fortune magazine. A falling labor share, he said, has got to be either falling earnings or falling numbers of people. And then he put it more bluntly still. He said things are getting worse for workers and much better for billionaires.

That was in January. The share has fallen a full point since.

Fortune reported something else in that same story. The Fortune Five Hundred companies had earned a record one point eight seven trillion dollars in profit in twenty twenty-four. A record. In the same years in which the share going to labor was falling to a record low.

A record low for work. A record high for profit. Those are the same fact, seen from two sides of the table.

[PAUSE]

Now, a word of caution, because I promised you honesty. The labor share is affected by many things. Depreciation. The way self-employment income is counted. The business cycle. Economists will argue for years about how much of the latest drop is due to artificial intelligence and how much to other forces. I am not going to stand here and tell you that every point of decline is the work of a chatbot.

What I will tell you is this. The labor share was already falling before the AI boom began. It is now falling faster, at a time of record investment in a technology whose explicit purpose is to do work that people used to do. You do not need to know the exact proportions to see the direction of travel. When the tide is already going out and someone opens the floodgates, you do not need a hydrologist to tell you which way the water will run.

## VI. The Largest Bet in Our History

So let us talk about the floodgates.

This year, the four largest American technology companies, Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta, are on track to spend more than seven hundred billion dollars on capital investment. That is the sum of the guidance they gave investors this summer, and most of it is going into the infrastructure of artificial intelligence: data centers, the chips that fill them, and the power that runs them.

Let me give you a sense of the pace. Alphabet, the parent company of Google, spent about ninety-one billion dollars on capital expenditure in twenty twenty-five. For twenty twenty-six, it has told investors it expects to spend between one hundred and ninety-five and two hundred and five billion. More than double in a single year.

[PAUSE]

Seven hundred billion dollars. Let us sit with that number, because numbers that large stop meaning anything to us. So let me compare it to things we know.

In September, the Brookings Institution published a paper by Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh of Columbia Business School, who tried to measure the whole buildout: data centers, power and chips. His estimate was that this country will invest about ten point three trillion dollars in this infrastructure between twenty twenty-five and twenty thirty-two. Ten trillion dollars. That works out to about three point six percent of the entire American economy, every year, for eight years.

Then he compared it to the great infrastructure booms of American history.

The canals, in the age of the Erie Canal, at their peak, absorbed about two-thirds of one percent of the economy each year. Electrification, about half of one percent. The interstate highways, in the nineteen-fifties, about one point one percent. The telecommunications and internet buildout around the turn of this century, about one point one percent.

And the railroads, the great railroad boom of the eighteen-seventies and eighteen-eighties, the boom that created the robber barons and the Gilded Age and the whole political upheaval out of which William Jennings Bryan rose, absorbed about two point two percent of the economy at its peak.

[BUILD]

The artificial intelligence buildout, by this estimate, is about fifty percent larger than the railroads. More than three times the internet boom. More than three times the interstates. It is, as one news headline put it this month, the largest financial bet in American history.

[LET IT LAND]

Now remember who rode the railroads to fortune. Remember what the farmers of Nebraska were shouting about in eighteen ninety-six. They were not angry that the railroads had been built. They were angry that the railroads had been built in such a way that a few men owned the track, set the rates, bought the legislatures, and squeezed the farmer at both ends, on the price of what he bought and the price of what he sold.

The question in eighteen ninety-six was never whether to have railroads. The question was who would own them and on whose terms they would run. That is precisely our question now.

[PAUSE]

And look at what the bet is crowding out. A Stateline analysis of Census data, published just this month, found that the value of data center construction in this country has more than doubled in two years, to about eighty-five billion dollars a year, while all other construction fell by eight percent. Since twenty twenty-four, data center construction is up forty-eight billion dollars a year, and the construction of new houses and apartments is down twenty-nine billion.

In a country where young families cannot afford a home, we are pouring concrete for server farms and pulling back on housing for people.

An economist at the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, Adam Shapiro, observed in September that investment is shifting away from residential building and toward computers. That is a polite way of saying we are building homes for machines faster than homes for families.

And here is the thing about those server farms. They are enormous, and they are nearly empty of people. Paul Krugman wrote this month that because data centers employ so few workers, the spending will reduce the demand for labor. A railroad needed armies of men to lay the track and armies more to run the trains. A factory needs a workforce. A data center, once built, hums along with a small crew of technicians and security guards.

So the largest investment in our history is going into an asset class that, by its nature, employs almost no one, in order to build a technology whose purpose is to do the work that people now do.

[SLOW]

If you were designing a machine to cut the rope between productivity and pay, you could hardly design a better one.

There is one more thing about this bet you should know, because it is your bet too, whether you agreed to it or not. More and more of it is being financed off the companies' balance sheets, through joint ventures, special-purpose vehicles, leases and private credit. Van Nieuwerburgh describes one such deal for a Meta data center in which a special-purpose vehicle issued about twenty-seven billion dollars of debt against an asset worth about thirty billion. That is about ninety percent borrowed. He estimates that about one point four trillion dollars of debt has been issued on these kinds of structures so far, roughly half of it from banks.

He is careful. He says it is premature to call this a systemic risk. But the International Monetary Fund warned in October that weaker or less durable investment and profitability in AI could trigger a correction. And Josh Bivens, the chief economist at the Economic Policy Institute, called our economy's reliance on this spending, in his words, "a narrow base of growth, basically one sector, and a pretty fragile one."

So consider the two ways this can go. If the bet pays off, the returns flow overwhelmingly to the owners. If the bet fails, the losses will find their way, as they did in two thousand eight, into the banking system and the broader economy, and from there into the lives of people who never owned a single share. Heads, they win. Tails, we all lose.

## VII. Who Owns the Machine

Every economy answers one question before it answers any other. Who owns the things that make the money?

In an economy where most of the value is created by human labor, the answer to that question matters, but it does not settle everything. Because even if you own nothing but your hands and your mind, you can sell your labor, and if you can bargain, you can sell it for a decent price.

But in an economy where more and more of the value is created by machines, the question of ownership becomes the only question. Because if the machine does the work, the person who owns the machine gets the income. And the person who used to do the work gets whatever the owner decides to give him, or whatever the law requires.

[PAUSE]

So who owns the machine?

The Federal Reserve keeps a set of accounts that answer this question with unusual precision. They are called the Distributional Financial Accounts, and they tell us how the nation's wealth is divided among households. I went to the most recent figures, for the second quarter of this year, and I looked at one line: corporate equities and mutual fund shares. Stocks. The ownership of American business.

Here is what they show.

The wealthiest one percent of American households own about fifty-one percent of all the corporate stock and mutual fund shares held directly by households. More than half. The wealthiest tenth of one percent, by themselves, own about a quarter.

The top ten percent together own about eighty-eight percent.

And the bottom half of American households, tens of millions of families, own about six-tenths of one percent.

[SLOW]

Six-tenths of one percent.

I want to be precise, because precision is a form of respect. Those figures count stocks that households own directly or through mutual funds. Retirement accounts and pensions are counted separately in the Fed's accounts, and many middle-class families have some stake in the market through a four-oh-one-k. That is real, and it matters. But it does not change the basic picture. When the value of American corporations rises, the gains flow overwhelmingly to the top tenth of households, and to the top one percent most of all.

Now put that alongside everything I have told you. If artificial intelligence moves income from wages to profits, and profits flow to shareholders, and shareholders are concentrated at the top, then the AI economy is a machine for moving income from the many to the few. Not through any conspiracy. Not through anyone's malice. Simply through the arithmetic of who owns what.

[PAUSE]

Let me show you what an AI-era company looks like from the inside.

Nvidia makes the chips on which most of the world's frontier AI systems are trained. In July of last year it became the first company in history to be worth four trillion dollars. In October of last year it became the first to be worth five trillion. In its fiscal year that ended this January, Nvidia reported revenue of about two hundred and sixteen billion dollars, and net income of about one hundred and twenty billion dollars.

It did that with about forty-two thousand employees.

Do the arithmetic. That is roughly two point nine million dollars of profit for every person on the payroll. Not revenue. Profit.

[LET IT LAND]

I am not criticizing Nvidia for being good at what it does. It makes extraordinary products, and its engineers are among the best in the world. I am pointing out what kind of economy this is. Remember General Motors in nineteen fifty, whose profits rose together with a payroll of hundreds of thousands, because it could not make a car without them? This is the opposite. This is a company whose profits are almost entirely detached from the number of people it employs. And it is the template for the industry it serves.

The great fortunes of the industrial age were built by companies that needed armies of workers. Those armies, once organized, could demand a share. The great fortunes of the AI age are being built by companies that need relatively few workers, and whose central product is designed to reduce the number of workers that everyone else needs.

[PAUSE]

You do not have to take my word for this. Take the word of the people building it.

In March of twenty twenty-one, before most Americans had heard of a large language model, the chief executive of OpenAI, Sam Altman, published an essay called "Moore's Law for Everything." In it he predicted that AI would do much of the work that people now do, and he wrote a sentence that I want every working person in this country to hear. He wrote: "Even more power will shift from labor to capital."

[SLOW]

Even more power will shift from labor to capital.

That was not a critic of the industry. That was the man running its most famous company. And to his credit, he did not stop there. He proposed a remedy, which I will come back to later. He proposed that large companies be taxed a small share of their market value every year, paid in stock, into a fund that would pay a dividend to every adult citizen. Whatever you think of that idea, notice what it concedes. It concedes that the market, left alone, will not share these gains. It concedes that ownership is the whole game.

In eighteen ninety-six, Bryan told the gold men in that Chicago hall that the man who is employed for wages is as much a businessman as his employer. He was widening the circle of who counts. He was saying that the farmer and the miner and the clerk were not just costs on somebody else's ledger. They were participants in the enterprise of the nation, with a rightful claim on its rewards.

That is the argument we must make again. The worker is as much a stakeholder in American productivity as the shareholder. The truck driver whose routes trained the logistics model, the nurse whose notes trained the medical system, the writer whose sentences trained the language model, the programmer whose public code taught the machine to code. Their work is in that machine. Their work is the machine. And they own none of it.

## VIII. The Canaries

So far I have talked about shares and lines and ledgers. Let me now talk about people. Young people, especially. Because if you want to know where an economy is going, do not look at the people who already have the good jobs. Look at the people trying to get in the door.

In August of last year, three economists at Stanford's Digital Economy Lab, Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar and Ruyu Chen, published a paper with a title that told you everything: "Canaries in the Coal Mine." They had access to something remarkable: monthly payroll records from ADP, one of the largest payroll processors in the country.

They used those records to ask a simple question. Since generative AI arrived, what has happened to employment in the jobs most exposed to it, compared to jobs that are less exposed? And does the answer differ by age?

[PAUSE]

The answer was striking. For most workers, they found no evidence of widespread displacement across the economy. Experienced workers in exposed occupations were doing fine. But for the youngest workers, those aged twenty-two to twenty-five, in the occupations most exposed to AI, employment had fallen sharply relative to their peers in less-exposed jobs. In the first version of the paper, they estimated a relative decline of about thirteen percent.

This August, they updated the study with a larger dataset running through June of this year. The gap had grown. Employment for young workers in AI-exposed occupations is now, by their estimate, nineteen percent below where it would be if it had kept pace with less-exposed peers. And the divergence, they report, has widened steadily since they first documented it.

[SLOW]

Nineteen percent. In the jobs where a young person's career begins.

Now listen to how it is happening, because the mechanism matters. They found that the gap is opening mainly through reduced hiring, not through layoffs. Nobody is being marched out of the building. The door is simply not opening for the next person in line.

They found that the declines are concentrated in occupations where AI primarily substitutes for human tasks. Where AI mainly complements workers, making them more capable rather than replacing them, employment is flat or rising, especially for experienced workers.

And they found that the adjustment is happening through employment, not through wages. Companies are not paying the young less. They are hiring fewer of them.

[PAUSE]

I promised honesty, so here are the caveats, in the authors' own spirit. They describe their findings as early, descriptive indicators, not proof of cause. The pattern weakens when they control for education. Some of the trends began before generative AI arrived. Other economists have argued that interest rates and the tech-hiring slowdown explain part of it, and the authors have published a response addressing exactly those arguments. This is a live debate among serious people.

But think about what a canary is. A canary does not prove the mine is full of gas. A canary is the first creature to stop singing. And when the canary stops singing, a sensible miner does not wait for a peer-reviewed study before he heads for the shaft.

[LET IT LAND]

Now picture the ladder. Every profession has a bottom rung: the junior associate who reviews documents, the entry-level coder who fixes small bugs, the first-year analyst who builds the spreadsheet, the assistant who drafts the first version. Those jobs were never just jobs. They were apprenticeships. They were how a person became the experienced worker that AI, for now, still complements.

If the machine takes the bottom rung, where does the next generation of experienced workers come from? You cannot become a senior engineer without first being a junior one. A ladder without a bottom rung is not a ladder. It is a wall.

[PAUSE]

And the people building these systems are telling us to expect more. Dario Amodei, who leads Anthropic, one of the companies at the frontier, has warned publicly that AI could displace half of all entry-level white-collar jobs within one to five years. I want to give him credit for saying it. It is not in the commercial interest of an AI company to frighten the public. He said it because he believes it, and because he believes we should prepare.

Analysts at Goldman Sachs have estimated that if AI raises productivity by fifteen percent, it could displace six to seven percent of American jobs over the transition. Six to seven percent of the American workforce is roughly ten million people.

[PAUSE]

Now here is a point that I think gets lost. Some economists are skeptical that AI will produce the giant productivity boom its champions promise. Daron Acemoglu, whom I quoted earlier, published a paper in twenty twenty-four estimating that AI might raise total factor productivity by no more than about two-thirds of one percent over ten years. Modest, by the standards of the hype.

You might think that is good news for workers. If the machines are not that powerful, maybe the jobs are safe.

But read the rest of his paper. Acemoglu predicted that even with modest productivity gains, AI would widen the gap between capital income and labor income. Remember what he and Restrepo found about the last forty years: large changes in inequality alongside modest gains in productivity. That is the nightmare scenario. Not a technology so powerful that it makes us all rich, but one powerful enough to replace workers and not powerful enough to create the abundance that would make up for it. All of the disruption, and little of the dividend.

So whichever forecast you believe, the optimists' or the skeptics', the direction for the worker's share is the same. If AI is transformative, the gains are enormous and they flow to owners. If AI is only modestly productive, the gains are smaller and they still flow to owners. In neither case does the rope retie itself.

## IX. We Have Been Here Before

Let me tell you where we are. We have seen how the rope was tied and how it was cut. We have seen what the AI boom is pouring into the gap, who owns it, and who is already being shut out. Now we come to the third part of tonight: the case against me.

Whenever someone raises an alarm about technology and work, someone else reaches for history. And history, they say, is on the side of the machines. Every great wave of invention has frightened people, and every time, the economy grew, new jobs appeared, and living standards rose.

I agree that we should reach for history. But I want to reach for all of it. Not just the happy ending. The middle, too. Because the middle is where people actually lived.

[PAUSE]

Go back to England at the dawn of the industrial revolution. The steam engine, the power loom, the factory. It was the most productive transformation the world had ever seen, and in the long run it made ordinary people richer than kings had been. That much is true.

But the economic historian Robert Allen studied what happened in the meantime, and he gave the period a name. He called it Engels' pause, after Friedrich Engels, who in eighteen forty-five wrote about the condition of the working class in England.

Here is what the pause looked like. Between seventeen eighty and eighteen forty, by one standard estimate, output per person in Britain grew by about forty-six percent.

And the real wages of working people? Over roughly the same years, by another standard estimate, they grew by about twelve percent.

Forty-six for the nation. Twelve for the people who worked in it.

Meanwhile labour's share of the national income slipped, from about half at the start of the century to about forty-five percent by the eighteen-forties. The returns to capital rose. The profits went to the owners of the mills.

[SLOW]

Does that pattern sound familiar? Output rising. Wages lagging. Labor's share falling. Profits soaring. It is the same chart I showed you at the beginning of this speech. It is just two hundred years older.

And how long did the pause last? Allen dates it from about seventeen ninety to about eighteen forty. Half a century. A child who went into the mill at the start of it could have spent his whole working life inside it. When people say technology always works out in the long run, remember that the long run, in that case, was longer than a working life.

[PAUSE]

What ended the pause? Allen's work shows that in the second half of the nineteenth century, wages finally began to catch up. From eighteen forty to nineteen hundred, output per worker rose by about ninety percent, and real wages rose by about one hundred and twenty-three percent. Workers finally began to share in the gains, and then some.

But that catching up did not fall from the sky. It came in an age of organizing and reform. It came as working people fought for the right to combine, for limits on the hours of children and women in the factories, for the vote. The long run did not arrive on its own schedule. People dragged it into the present.

[PAUSE]

And now let me tell you about the people we remember as the villains of that story. The Luddites.

The word has become an insult. Call someone a Luddite and you mean he is afraid of technology, too slow to adapt, standing in front of progress with his arms crossed. But the real Luddites were not that. They were textile workers in Nottinghamshire and Yorkshire and the North West of England who, beginning in March of eighteen eleven, broke into workshops and smashed machines.

Here is what is usually left out. Many of them were skilled machine operators themselves. They were not against machines as such. What they targeted were manufacturers who were using the new machines to bypass the standard labor practices of their trades: to cut pay, to replace skilled workers with cheaper unskilled ones, to produce shoddy goods that undercut the honest ones. Their quarrel was not with the loom. It was with the terms on which the loom was used.

[SLOW]

And how did the government respond? It sent troops. Around twelve thousand of them. The historian Eric Hobsbawm noted that this was a larger force than the army Wellington took to the Peninsula in eighteen-oh-eight. Parliament passed a law in eighteen twelve making the breaking of machines a crime punishable by death. In January of eighteen thirteen, more than sixty men were charged at York. Some were sent to the gallows. Others were transported to the other side of the world.

[LET IT LAND]

I do not tell you this story because I want anyone to break a machine. Violence is wrong, and it does not work. I tell it because of what it reveals. Two centuries ago, when working people demanded a say in how new technology would be used, the state answered with soldiers and the gallows. It decided, by force, that the owners of the machines would set the terms. And then the victors wrote the history, and the name of those workers became a synonym for foolishness.

The Luddites were not wrong that the gains of the new machines were going to someone else. They were right about that. They were wrong about the remedy. The remedy turned out to be not smashing the machines but organizing the people, not destroying the factory but changing the laws that governed it.

That is the lesson I draw from history. Not that technology always works out. Not that it never does. But that whether it works out for working people depends on whether working people have the power to shape the terms. When they have that power, the lines climb together. When they do not, the pause can last a lifetime.

## X. The Strongest Case Against Me

I have been building my case for more than an hour. Now I owe you the best version of the other side. Not a straw man. The real thing.

Here it is.

[PAUSE]

The optimist says: you are making the oldest mistake in economics. You are treating the amount of work as fixed, as if there were only so many jobs, and every job a machine takes is a job a human loses forever. But that has never been how it works.

When the tractor came, farm employment collapsed, but people did not become permanently unemployed. They moved to cities and took jobs in factories and offices that had not existed. When the automatic teller machine came, banks did not stop hiring people; they opened more branches and gave tellers new tasks. Whole professions exist today that no one could have imagined a century ago.

And the optimist has strong evidence. David Autor and his colleagues built a remarkable database of new job titles from nineteen forty to twenty eighteen. Their finding was that the majority of American employment today is in job specialties that did not exist in nineteen forty. The majority. New work keeps being invented.

The optimist goes on. AI will make goods and services cheaper. If a lawyer's research costs a tenth as much, everyone can afford legal help. If a medical diagnosis costs a fraction of what it does now, everyone gets better care. Even if workers' share of income falls, their real living standards will rise, because what they buy will be so much cheaper.

And in nineteen thirty, the great economist John Maynard Keynes wrote a famous essay imagining the economic possibilities for his grandchildren. He foresaw a world, a century on, so productive that the main human problem would be what to do with all our leisure. We are nearly at that century. The optimist says: we should be planning for abundance, not panicking about scarcity.

That is a serious case. I believe parts of it. Let me tell you which parts, and then tell you why it does not answer my argument.

I believe that new work will be invented. I believe that some goods and services will become dramatically cheaper. I believe that in the very long run, AI could make our descendants richer than we can imagine.

But notice what the optimist's case is about. It is about whether there will be jobs, and whether total wealth will grow. My case is about who will own the wealth, and whether the people doing the work will get a fair share of it. Those are different questions. And the optimist's answers to the first do not answer the second.

[SLOW]

Go back to Autor's study of new work, because it contains a warning the optimists rarely mention. He and his colleagues found that new work tends to appear when technology complements what people do, and slows when technology automates what people do. And they found that over the last four decades, the job-destroying effects of automation have intensified, while the job-creating effects of augmentation have not kept pace. The engine of new work is real, but it has been running slower, while the engine of displacement has been running faster.

Erik Brynjolfsson, the same Stanford economist who led the canaries study, has a name for the danger here. He calls it the Turing Trap. When we build machines to imitate and replace human beings, he argues, workers lose economic and political bargaining power and become, in his words, "increasingly dependent on those who control the technology." When we build machines to augment human beings, people keep the power to demand a share of the value they help create. And he warns that our technologists, our business leaders and our policymakers face excess incentives to automate rather than to augment.

So the question is not whether AI could create new work. It could. The question is whether we are building it to do so, and whether our rules reward that choice. Right now, they do not.

[PAUSE]

Now, the tractor. The optimist tells the tractor story from the farmer's side: the farmhand who moved to the city and found a better job. But there is another side to the tractor story, and economists tell it too. It is about horses, and I think it should haunt us.

In the early twentieth century, the horse was one of the most important workers in the economy. Then came the internal combustion engine, and the tractor, and the truck. And the horses did not move into new jobs. There were no new jobs for horses. Their numbers simply fell.

The Nobel laureate Wassily Leontief, one of the great economists of the last century, drew the comparison directly. He wrote that the role of humans as the most important factor of production is bound to diminish in the same way that the role of horses was diminished by the tractor.

I do not believe human beings are horses. People are adaptable in ways horses are not. People can learn, organize, vote and invent. But that is precisely my point. What saves human workers from the fate of the horse is not the market. It is our capacity to act together, to change the rules, to insist on a share. Horses could not vote. We can.

As for cheaper goods. Yes, AI may make some services cheaper. But a cheaper service does you little good if you have lost the income with which to buy it. And the things that make a family secure, land, housing, health, energy, are not obviously the things AI makes cheaper. Some of them, like electricity, it is making more expensive, as I will show you in a moment.

And as for Keynes. He imagined a world of leisure, and he was right that we would become vastly more productive. But the leisure never came for most people. The productivity came. The leisure went to those who owned the productivity. The rest of us got the second job.

[LET IT LAND]

That is the heart of my answer. The optimists ask whether AI will make the economy richer. Yes. I concede it. I hope it does. But a richer economy is not the same thing as a richer people. For fifty years we have had a richer economy and a stagnant middle. The question is not whether the pie grows. It is who gets to hold the knife.

## XI. The Rulebook Is Not Neutral

That is the case against me, and my answer to it. Now we turn to the last part of tonight: what we can do. And before we can fix the rules, we have to see them clearly.

I have said several times tonight that the decoupling was not an act of nature. Let me now prove it, by showing you how our own laws tilt the field.

Imagine you run a company, and you are deciding whether to hire a person or buy a machine to do the same task. Before you even think about which one does the job better, the government has already put its thumb on the scale.

[PAUSE]

If you hire the person, you pay payroll taxes on every dollar of their wages. Your share of Social Security and Medicare. Unemployment insurance. And the worker pays income tax and payroll tax on the same dollars.

If you buy the machine, or the software, the tax code lets you write off the cost, often quickly, sometimes immediately. There is no payroll tax on a server. There is no Social Security contribution for an algorithm.

Put a face on it. Picture a claims manager at a mid-sized insurance company in Ohio. She is not a real person. She is a composite, the kind of person who makes this decision somewhere in America every week. She has twelve people on her team and a budget meeting on Monday. A vendor has shown her software that can handle a third of their files.

When she runs the numbers, the software comes out cheaper than the four people it would replace. Partly because it does the work well. And partly because the tax code has already shaved the price of the software and padded the cost of the people. She is not cruel. She is doing her job. The thumb was on the scale before she ever opened the spreadsheet.

Daron Acemoglu, together with Andrea Manera and Pascual Restrepo, studied exactly this question in a paper published by the Brookings Institution in twenty twenty. Its title asked: does the U.S. tax code favor automation? Their answer was yes. They found that the American tax system is biased against labor and in favor of capital, that this bias had grown in recent years, and that it encourages more automation than is efficient. Not more automation than is kind. More automation than makes economic sense.

[SLOW]

Hear what that means. Even by the cold standard of efficiency, the standard economists use when they set aside all questions of fairness, our tax code pushes companies to replace people with machines more than they should.

And they estimated what would happen if we fixed it. Moving from the tax system of the twenty-tens to an optimal tax on capital and labor, they calculated, would raise employment by about four percent and raise labor's share of income by nearly eight-tenths of a percentage point. Four percent of American employment is millions of jobs. Not created by a government program. Simply unblocked by removing a bias we wrote into the law ourselves.

Brynjolfsson makes the same point from another angle. He warns of excess incentives for automation rather than augmentation. Translate that phrase into plain English. It means that when an executive faces a choice between using AI to make her workers more productive and using AI to replace them, our rules make replacement look cheaper than it really is. We are subsidizing the very outcome I have spent this evening warning you about.

[PAUSE]

And the tilt goes further than the tax code. Consider where those data centers are going and who is paying for them.

A data center consumes staggering amounts of electricity. The Brookings study estimates the buildout will double American electricity consumption. And someone has to pay for the new power plants and transmission lines. An analysis from Carnegie Mellon and North Carolina State University this summer found that data centers and cryptocurrency mining could raise electricity bills by about eight percent nationally, and by as much as twenty-five percent in parts of Virginia.

Who pays an electric bill? Everybody. The retiree on a fixed income. The single mother. The family in a small apartment. A higher power bill is the most regressive tax there is. And if those bills rise to pay for the infrastructure of companies earning record profits, then working families are not merely being left out of the gains. They are being asked to subsidize them.

[LET IT LAND]

The states have started to notice. Many of them spent years offering data centers generous tax breaks to lure them in. Now some are pulling back. Nevada's governor signed an executive order in September trimming tax abatements and requiring data centers to cover their own energy and water costs. In Oregon, a report in September found that utility affordability was the public's top concern, and approvals were frozen. Virginia imposed a surcharge on data center electricity this summer, even as it kept its tax breaks.

That is the beginning of wisdom. But it is happening piecemeal, state by state, with every state competing against the others for the next facility. And in that kind of competition, the companies have the leverage, because they can always build somewhere else. That is exactly the kind of race to the bottom that only a national standard can stop.

So here is the picture. We tax work more heavily than we tax machines. We give companies incentives to replace people that go beyond what even efficiency would justify. We let the costs of the AI buildout land on ordinary ratepayers. And we let localities bid against one another to hand out tax breaks to some of the richest companies on earth.

And then, when the labor share falls to a record low, we are told that this is simply the market at work. That nothing can be done. That this is the price of progress.

[SLOW]

No. This is the price of a rulebook. And a rulebook can be rewritten.

## XII. The Freedom Question

Before I tell you how I would rewrite it, I want to talk about something larger than taxes and wages. I want to talk about freedom.

When Roosevelt stood in Philadelphia in nineteen thirty-six, he did not frame the fight over economic power as a fight about money. He framed it as a fight about liberty. He said the Americans of seventeen seventy-six had won their freedom from political tyranny, and that a new generation faced a different kind of tyranny: the concentration of control over the economic life of the nation in a few private hands. He argued that political equality means little in the face of economic inequality, that a man may have the vote and still have no say over the conditions of his own life.

[PAUSE]

That argument is not old. It is about to become new again.

Consider what it means for a society when the most important productive technology of an age is owned and controlled by a handful of companies. These systems will increasingly mediate how we learn, how we work, how we get medical advice, how we search for information, how we form our opinions. The firms that control them will set the terms for every business that depends on them, and every business will depend on them.

I am not accusing anyone of plotting. I do not think the people running these companies are sitting in a room scheming to rule the world. Most of them, I suspect, sincerely believe they are building something good, and some of them are working hard to make it safe. But good intentions are not a system of government. The founders of this country did not build checks and balances because they thought every king was evil. They built them because they knew that concentrated power, held long enough, is dangerous no matter who holds it.

[SLOW]

Concentrated economic power does not stay economic. It becomes political. It hires lobbyists. It funds campaigns. It writes the first draft of the regulations meant to govern it. It shapes what is considered reasonable and what is considered radical. That was true of the railroads and the oil trusts. It was true of the banks in Roosevelt's day. There is no reason to think it will be less true of the companies that own the most valuable technology ever created.

[PAUSE]

And there is a deeper danger, the one Brynjolfsson pointed to. For all of human history, ordinary people have had one source of power that no king or baron could fully take from them. Their labor. The powerful needed them. Needed them to farm the fields and fight the wars and work the mills and staff the offices. And because they were needed, they could bargain. They could strike. They could withhold their work and bring the whole machine to a halt.

Every expansion of democracy in the industrial age was won, in part, on the strength of that leverage. The weekend. The eight-hour day. The end of child labor. Workplace safety. The right to organize. All of it was won by people who could say: you need us, and you will treat us decently or you will not have us.

[LET IT LAND]

What happens to that leverage in an economy where the powerful no longer need the many? Where the work that once required millions of people can be done by systems owned by a few? That is the question beneath every number I have shown you tonight. It is a question of income, yes. But underneath, it is a question of whether ordinary people will keep any power at all over the economy they live in.

That is the darkest question I will ask you tonight. I have brought you down to it on purpose, because you cannot climb out of a hole you refuse to look at. But it is not the last word.

If we let the returns of artificial intelligence concentrate the way the returns of the last fifty years have concentrated, we will not only have a more unequal economy. We will have a less free people. Necessitous men, Roosevelt reminded us, are not free men. And an economy that no longer needs most of its citizens is an economy in which most of its citizens will be necessitous.

[PAUSE]

So I say to you: this is not a fight about envy, and it is not a fight against technology or the people who build it. It is a fight about whether the American people will remain the owners of their own economic future, or become tenants in a house built by others, on land owned by others, under rules written by others.

That is a question of freedom. And this country has answered questions of freedom before.

## XIII. A New Bargain

So what do we do?

I am not going to stand here and tell you that I have a perfect plan. Anyone who claims to know exactly how the AI economy will unfold is selling something. But I know the principle, because it is the same principle that tied the rope the first time. When a new technology makes a nation more productive, the people of that nation should share in the gains. Not as charity. As a matter of right. Because their work, their data, their public investments and their laws made those gains possible.

And I know the method, because we have used it before. You tie the rope with law.

Let me give you six commitments for a new bargain. Not slogans. Commitments, each with a precedent, each with a reason.

[PAUSE]

The first commitment: stop taxing people more heavily than machines.

If our tax code is biased toward replacing workers, and three of the most careful economists in the country say it is, then the first thing we do is remove the bias. Bring the tax treatment of labor and capital closer together. That could mean lowering payroll taxes on ordinary workers and making up the revenue with higher taxes on capital income, on very large fortunes, or on the profits of the most capital-intensive companies. It could mean ending the most generous write-offs for automation that exist mainly to cut headcount. The details are for legislators and economists to work out. The principle is simple. The law should not pay companies to get rid of people.

I know the objection. Tax capital more and you will slow investment. Perhaps a little. But remember what Acemoglu, Manera and Restrepo found: the current bias is so large that correcting it would increase employment, not reduce it. We are not choosing between growth and fairness here. We are correcting a distortion that harms both.

The second commitment: give every American a stake in the machine.

If the fundamental problem is that the returns to AI will flow to the owners of capital, then the fundamental solution is to make more Americans owners. Not in theory. In fact.

We already have a model, and it comes from a place that is not exactly a hotbed of radicalism. Alaska. For decades, Alaska has paid its residents an annual dividend out of a fund built from the state's oil wealth. The idea is that the oil beneath Alaska belongs to all Alaskans, and so every Alaskan should share in its returns. This year, every eligible Alaskan received twelve hundred dollars, including a two-hundred-dollar energy relief payment.

[SLOW]

Now ask yourself. If the oil under Alaska belongs to all Alaskans, to whom does the knowledge embedded in an AI model belong? These systems were trained on the accumulated writing, speech, code, art and record of the whole human community. They were built on decades of publicly funded research. They run on an electric grid paid for by ratepayers. They are protected by courts, patents and laws that the public maintains. If any wealth has ever been a common inheritance, it is this.

Sam Altman, as I mentioned, proposed something along these lines himself: a national fund, fed by an annual levy of a small percentage of the market value of the largest companies, paid in shares, with a dividend to every adult. He estimated, under his own assumptions, that it could pay each adult around thirteen and a half thousand dollars a year a decade after launch. I am not endorsing his exact design, and I would not take his estimate as a promise. But I want you to notice that when the architect of the machine and the critic of the machine arrive at the same basic idea, a public stake in AI's returns, it deserves to be taken seriously.

Call it an American Dividend. A sovereign wealth fund for the age of intelligence. The name does not matter. What matters is that when the productivity line climbs, every citizen holds a share of the rope.

[PAUSE]

The third commitment: restore the power to bargain.

Remember the four causes of the decoupling. The first was the collapse of bargaining power. No dividend, however generous, can replace the dignity of a fair wage negotiated by people who have a real voice at work. A dividend is a check. A union is a seat at the table.

That means modernizing labor law so that workers who want to organize can do so without years of delay. It means exploring ways for workers across a whole industry to bargain together, so that one company cannot simply undercut another by squeezing its workforce. And it means giving workers a say in how AI is brought into their workplaces.

We have already seen what that can look like. In twenty twenty-three, the screenwriters of Hollywood went on strike for nearly five months, from May into late September. One of the issues at the center of that strike was artificial intelligence. And when they settled, they had won contract protections on how the studios could use AI, including protections against using it to reduce the number of writers or their pay.

[LET IT LAND]

That is the Treaty of Detroit for a new age, in miniature. Not a ban on the technology. A negotiated set of terms for how it will be used, and who will share in its benefits. Every worker in America deserves the chance to bargain for terms like that. Most of them, today, have no way to do it.

The fourth commitment: keep the frontier open.

In eighteen ninety, the Senate passed the Sherman Antitrust Act by a vote of fifty-two to one. The House passed it unanimously. Benjamin Harrison, a Republican, signed it into law on July the second. Twenty-one years later, the Supreme Court used it to break up the Standard Oil Company.

Fifty-two to one. Concern about concentrated economic power was not the property of one party or one region. It was American common sense. People understood that a free market requires competition, and competition requires that no single company be allowed to own the whole field.

The AI industry is concentrating at astonishing speed. A few firms own the frontier models. A few own the cloud platforms on which they run. One company dominates the chips they are trained on. Those positions reinforce one another. We need antitrust enforcement that understands this new terrain: that watches the partnerships and investments through which the giants absorb their would-be rivals, that keeps the essential infrastructure open to competitors on fair terms, and that prevents the owners of the platforms from favoring their own products at everyone else's expense.

Competition is not just good for consumers. It is good for workers. When many firms compete for talent, wages rise. When one firm owns the field, it sets the terms.

The fifth commitment: make augmentation pay, and protect the bottom rung.

Brynjolfsson's warning was that our incentives push toward replacing workers rather than empowering them. So let us change the incentives. The federal government is an enormous buyer of technology. It can favor AI systems designed to make workers more capable. Public research funding can tilt toward tools that augment human work. And where the bottom rung of a profession is disappearing, we can build new ones: paid apprenticeships, entry-level hiring credits, partnerships between employers and schools, so that a young person still has a way in the door.

If the canaries study is right that the young are losing their first jobs, then protecting the first job is not a luxury. It is how we make sure there is a next generation of experienced workers at all.

The sixth commitment: the builders pay their own way.

If a company wants to build a data center that will draw as much power as a city, it should pay the full cost of the power plants and transmission lines it requires. Not the retiree down the road. Not the family in the apartment. And we should set national standards for this, so that the states are not forced into a race to see who can give away the most to the richest companies on earth.

This is the simplest commitment of all, and in some ways the most basic. Those who reap the profits should bear the costs. That is not socialism. That is the first principle of a fair market.

And beneath all six, one more thing: we should measure it. We should require the largest companies to disclose how their deployment of AI is changing their workforce. Which jobs are being eliminated. Which are being created. What is happening to wages. We cannot manage what we refuse to see, and right now we are flying blind into the largest economic transformation of our lives.

[SLOW]

Lower the tax on work. Give every citizen a stake. Restore the power to bargain. Keep the frontier open. Protect the bottom rung. Make the builders pay their way.

None of these ideas is radical. Each has roots in our own history: in the Wagner Act, the Treaty of Detroit, the Sherman Act, the Alaska dividend. Each has been proposed or supported by serious people across the spectrum, including some of the people building AI themselves. What would be radical is to do nothing, and to let the largest investment in our history widen the largest gap in our economy into something we can no longer cross.

[PAUSE]

Some of you may be wondering why I have not simply called for a universal basic income. Send every American a check, the argument goes, and let the machines do the work. It is a tempting idea, and some of the people building AI have floated versions of it.

I support a public stake in AI's returns, as I have said. But let me be careful about what a check can and cannot do.

A check can put food on the table. It cannot give a person a seat at the table. If the only thing standing between a family and destitution is a payment that a future Congress can cut, or a future company can lobby to shrink, then that family is not secure. It is dependent. It has traded the power of a worker for the position of a petitioner.

That is why the dividend I am describing must be paid out of ownership, not out of charity. A share in a fund is a property right. It grows when the economy grows. It does not depend on the goodwill of anyone. Alaskans do not receive their dividend as a favor. They receive it as owners.

And that is why the dividend cannot stand alone. It must come alongside the power to bargain, the protection of the bottom rung, and a tax code that stops paying companies to discard people. Work is not only how we earn a living. It is how most people find structure, purpose, community and a sense of being needed. A country that tells its people they are no longer needed, and then mails them a check, has not solved the problem of the great decoupling. It has only priced it.

So I do not want a future in which the many are paid to stay out of the way. I want a future in which the many are owners of the machine, partners in its use, and beneficiaries of its gains. A check is a floor. Ownership and bargaining power are the house.

Now, I hear the final objection. If we do these things, they say, the companies will move overseas. The investment will go to other countries. We will lose the race.

Let me answer that directly. The great advantage of this country has never been that it lets its powerful do whatever they please. It has been that it built the largest, richest consumer market in the world, a market of people with money in their pockets. The companies building AI need that market. They need our customers, our universities, our courts, our capital markets, our electric grid, our rule of law. They are not going to abandon the most valuable market on earth because we ask them to share a fraction of the gains with the people who make that market valuable.

And if the race we are running is a race to see who can build the most powerful machines while leaving the most people behind, then it is not a race worth winning. A nation that wins the technology and loses its middle class has not won anything.

The rope was tied by law once. It can be tied by law again.

## XIV. Three Generations

Let me close the argument where all arguments about the economy should close. Not with a chart. With a family.

I am going to tell you about three people. They are not real individuals. They are a composite, drawn from the numbers I have shared with you tonight. But I suspect that many of you will recognize them, because many of you are related to them.

[PAUSE]

The first is a grandfather. Call him Walt. In nineteen fifty-five, Walt works on an assembly line in Michigan. The work is hard and loud and sometimes dangerous. He did not go to college. But he has a union, and his union has a contract, and his contract has a cost-of-living adjustment and an annual improvement factor. Every year the plant gets more productive, and every year Walt gets a raise. He has a pension the company pays for. Half his family's health insurance is covered.

On one income, Walt buys a small house. His wife stays home with their three children, by choice or by custom. They take a car trip every summer. When Walt retires, his pension and his Social Security carry him through to the end.

Walt is not rich. But Walt is secure. And Walt is free in the way Roosevelt meant. He is not necessitous. If the foreman treats him badly, he has a steward to call. If the company wants more from him, it has to sit across a table and bargain. Walt's productivity and Walt's pay are roped together, by contract and by law.

The second is Walt's daughter. Call her Diane. Diane goes to work in nineteen eighty-five, in an office, processing claims for an insurance company. She is good at it. She is careful and quick and she never misses a detail.

But Diane works in the era of the break. There is no union in her office. Over the next twenty years, the company installs one system after another, each one automating some part of what she does. Her productivity soars. Her pay barely moves. Each round of new software is followed by a round of layoffs, and each time she survives, she is doing the work of two people for the wage of one.

Diane is a routine-task worker in an industry undergoing rapid automation. She is, without knowing it, a data point in the study Acemoglu and Restrepo would one day publish: the fifty to seventy percent of the change in the American wage structure explained by exactly what happened to her.

Her pension becomes a four-oh-one-k. Her health insurance premiums climb faster than her raises. She and her husband need both incomes to afford a house not much bigger than Walt's. She works until she is sixty-seven because she cannot afford not to.

Diane is not poor. But Diane is anxious. The rope that held her father's pay to his productivity was cut before she ever started work, and she has spent her life running to stand still.

[PAUSE]

The third is Diane's daughter. Call her Kiara. Kiara graduated from college in the spring of twenty twenty-four with a degree, a good record and a fair amount of debt. She did everything she was told to do. She studied. She took the internships. She learned to code a little, because everyone told her that was the future.

Kiara wanted an entry-level job as an analyst, or a junior writer, or a customer support lead on the way to something bigger. The kind of job that used to be the bottom rung of a career.

But Kiara is twenty-four, and she is applying for work in exactly the occupations that the Stanford researchers identified as most exposed to AI. She is one of the canaries. The door she is knocking on has not been slammed in her face. It simply does not open. The companies are not firing people like her. They are just not hiring them. Her applications vanish into systems that may themselves be run by AI.

[SLOW]

Kiara is not unemployed in the way her great-grandfather would have recognized. She is driving for a delivery app, freelancing when she can, living with her mother, waiting. She is not on any picket line, because there is no line to picket. She has no table to sit at, because no one has set one.

And somewhere, a few hundred miles away, a data center is rising out of a cornfield. It cost billions of dollars. When it is finished it will employ a small crew of technicians. It will draw as much electricity as a city, and Kiara's mother's power bill will go up to help pay for it. Inside it, models will be trained that can do much of what Kiara went to college to learn to do. The profits from those models will flow to shareholders, and about half of the stock that American households hold directly belongs to the richest one percent of them.

[LET IT LAND]

Walt. Diane. Kiara. Three generations. The first had the rope. The second watched it cut. The third is being told that the machine will make everyone rich, while she cannot find the door.

Now I ask you to imagine a fourth generation. Kiara's children.

In one version of their future, the great decoupling becomes the great chasm. The productivity line rockets upward, faster than at any time in our history. The pay line does not follow, because there are fewer and fewer jobs for which a person is needed, and those few jobs carry no bargaining power. The returns flow to a tiny class of owners, the people who hold the models and the chips and the data. Everyone else lives on whatever the owners decide to share. Kiara's children are not exactly poor. But they are dependent. They are tenants in their own country. And they have no leverage left with which to change it.

In the other version, Kiara's generation decides that it will not accept that future. It does what Walt's generation did. It writes the bargain down. It taxes work no more heavily than machines. It gives every citizen a stake in the nation's new wealth. It restores the right to bargain. It keeps the frontier open to competition. It protects the bottom rung. It makes the builders pay their own way. And Kiara's children grow up in a country where the machine is a servant of the many, not a fortress for the few.

[SLOW]

Both futures are possible. Neither is inevitable. The technology does not choose between them. We do.

## XV. The Rope and the Chasm

My friends, we have come a long way tonight. From a chart with two lines to a family with three generations. From Detroit in nineteen fourteen to a data center in a cornfield in twenty twenty-six. From the mills of Nottinghamshire to the chips of Silicon Valley. And I want to end where Bryan ended, and where Roosevelt ended, with a choice put plainly before a free people.

[PAUSE]

There are those who will tell you that nothing can be done.

They will tell you that the gap between productivity and pay is an illusion of price indexes. We reply that the labor share has fallen to the lowest point ever recorded, and no price index can explain that away.

They will tell you that the AI boom will lift all boats. We reply that we have heard that promise for fifty years, and for fifty years the yachts have risen while the rowboats took on water.

They will tell you that if we make the well-to-do prosperous enough, their prosperity will leak through to those below. We reply that it has been leaking for half a century, and the people below are still waiting for the drip.

They will tell you that technology always works out in the long run. We reply that the long run in Britain lasted half a century, and that it ended only when working people organized and changed the law.

They will tell you that to ask for a share of the gains is envy. We reply that to ask for a share of what you helped create is not envy. It is justice.

And they will tell you that the market has spoken. We reply that the market speaks in the language of the rules we write. And we are the ones who write them.

[PAUSE]

I want to speak now to the people building these systems. The engineers and researchers and founders. Some of you are among the most thoughtful people in this country, and some of you have been more honest about the risks of your own work than any politician. I am not your enemy. I want what you say you want: a technology that makes human life richer, longer, freer and more creative for everyone.

But I ask you to remember that the value you are creating did not come from nowhere. It came from the books and articles and code and conversations of millions of people. It came from public research, public universities, public infrastructure, and the public order that protects your property. You are standing on the shoulders of a whole civilization. And a civilization has a claim on what is built upon it.

Some of you have already said so. Some of you have proposed public dividends and national funds and taxes on your own industry's wealth. I take you at your word. Now help us write it into law.

And I want to speak to the people who own the shares. Many of you are not villains either. Many of you are retirees, or pension funds, or families who saved and invested wisely. But I ask you to consider what kind of country you want your returns to come from. A country whose wealth rests on a thinning middle class and a generation locked out of work is not a stable foundation for anyone's portfolio. The postwar boom made shareholders rich precisely because it also made workers prosperous. A broadly shared prosperity is not a cost to capital. It is the ground capital stands on.

And now I want to speak to everyone else. To the Walts and the Dianes and the Kiaras. To the people who have been told for fifty years that their stagnant wages were the price of progress, and who are now being told that their vanishing jobs are the price of a still greater progress.

You are not the cost of this economy. You are this economy.

Every chip was designed by human minds. Every data center was built by human hands. Every model learned from the words and work of human beings. Every dollar of profit was spent by a human customer. There is no artificial intelligence that did not begin in human intelligence. There is no machine that does not rest on the labor of people.

You built this. And you are entitled to share in it.

Bryan told that convention in eighteen ninety-six that you could burn down the cities and leave the farms, and the cities would spring up again as if by magic, but destroy the farms, and grass would grow in the streets of every city in the country. He meant that the whole edifice of prosperity rests on the people who do the productive work of the nation. Take them away, impoverish them, and nothing built on top of them will stand.

That is as true of the AI economy as it was of the agricultural one. Build all the data centers you like. Train all the models you can. But if you hollow out the working families who buy the goods and pay the taxes and raise the next generation, then the grass will grow in the server halls too. An economy that does not need its people will soon find that its people have no means to need it.

Roosevelt told his generation in nineteen thirty-six that they had a rendezvous with destiny. He did not mean that their fate was written in the stars. He meant that history had handed them a choice that no other generation would get to make, and that the way they made it would shape the lives of their children and grandchildren.

[BUILD]

We have our own rendezvous now. The largest investment in our history is being poured into the ground as I speak. The productivity line is about to climb as it has not climbed in fifty years. The labor share is at the lowest point ever recorded. The youngest workers are already feeling the door close. And the rules that will govern who owns the most powerful technology ever built are being written right now, this year and next, in boardrooms and agencies and legislatures, whether or not we are in the room.

This is the moment. Not after the chasm opens. Now, while it is still a gap. Now, while the rope can still be retied.

I began tonight with a chart. Two lines that climbed together for a generation, and then came apart.

Someday someone will draw that chart again, with our years on it. Twenty twenty-six. Twenty thirty. Twenty forty. Our grandchildren will look at it the way we look at nineteen seventy-three, and in the space between those two lines they will be able to read exactly what we chose.

I want them to see the lines come back together. I want them to find the year the gap stopped growing, and to ask what happened that year. And I want the answer to be simple. The people decided. The people wrote it down.

So let me say it plainly, in the spirit of the men whose words have echoed through this speech, but in the words of our own time.

[SLOW]

To those who would build a new economy on the old rule that the gains go up and the costs go down, we say: not this time.

To those who would tell a generation of young people that the bottom rung is gone and they must simply adapt, we say: we will build a new ladder.

To those who would ask working families to pay higher power bills to subsidize some of the most profitable companies on earth, we say: pay your own way.

To those who would say that the knowledge of the whole human race, gathered into a machine, belongs only to whoever owns the machine, we say: it belongs to all of us, and all of us will share in it.

[BUILD]

You shall not take the work of a hundred million hands, teach it to a machine, and then tell those hands they are no longer needed. You shall not build the greatest engine of wealth the world has ever seen and leave the people who fueled it standing outside the gate.

We do not fear the machine. We built it. We will not smash it. We will own it.

We will tie the rope again.

[LET IT LAND]

Thank you.
