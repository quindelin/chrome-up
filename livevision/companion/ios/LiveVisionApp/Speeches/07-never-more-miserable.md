# Never More Miserable

## I. Where We Stand

I want to start with a question. It is a plain one. You have been asked it a hundred times, by a neighbor at the mailbox or a cousin on the phone.

Taken all together, how would you say things are these days?

[PAUSE]

In nineteen seventy two, interviewers from a research center at the University of Chicago sat down in American living rooms and asked exactly that. Very happy, pretty happy, or not too happy? And for nearly half a century, through wars and recessions and scandals, Americans gave almost the same answer. About three in ten said very happy. Far fewer said not too happy.

Then, in the middle of the pandemic, the answer flipped. For the first time since anyone started asking, more Americans said not too happy than said very happy.

Hold on to that question. I am going to ask it again before the night is over, and when I do, I am going to ask you to do something with it.

[SLOW]

Here is the claim I will spend the next two hours defending. By every instrument we have built to measure the inner life of this country, Americans are more depressed and less happy than at any point since those instruments were switched on. And they are lonelier than any people this rich has a right to be.

Not worse in one survey that a clever critic can wave away. Worse in the private polls and in the federal health statistics. Worse in the prescription records. And, most terribly, worse in what the death certificates record.

And all of it is happening in the richest society that has ever existed on the face of the earth.

I did not come here tonight to cheer you up. But I will not leave you in the dark, either. A diagnosis is not a death sentence. It is the first thing a good doctor gives you, and the first thing a serious nation needs.

[LET IT LAND]

A long time ago, in a hall in Springfield, Illinois, a lawyer who had not yet become a president stood up and said, in effect, that before we can decide what to do, we must first understand where we are and where we are heading. It was the sixteenth of June, eighteen fifty eight. The country was coming apart over slavery, and Abraham Lincoln believed that most of the bad advice of his time came from people who had skipped that first step. They had prescriptions without a diagnosis.

I think we are in a similar position. Not in the substance; nothing in our moment compares to the moral catastrophe Lincoln faced. But in the method.

We have a thousand prescriptions for American unhappiness. Put down your phone. Go to therapy.

Take a walk. Take a pill. Stop taking pills.

Blame the economy. Blame the culture. Blame the other party.

What we do not have, very often, is the patience to look at the whole picture first.

So that is how this evening will go. For the first half of our time together, I will be a witness rather than an advocate. I will call four witnesses to the stand.

The first is one of the best known polling firms in the country. The second is the federal government's own health statisticians. The third is the world, looking back at us in the mirror of an international survey.

And the fourth is the question I opened with, the oldest continuous record of American happiness that exists, asked of ordinary people since the year Richard Nixon was re-elected.

Then I will call a fifth witness, the one nobody wants to hear from: the ledger of the dead.

After the witnesses, I will give the other side its day in court. Because there is a serious argument that all of this is an illusion, that we are not sadder, only more willing to say so. I take that argument seriously, and I will answer it with care.

And then, in the second half, I will try to do what Lincoln did next in that Springfield hall. I will try to show you how the separate pieces fit together into a frame. And I will borrow from another president, who stood on the steps of the Capitol in the worst year of the Great Depression and told a frightened nation that its problem was not a shortage of anything it truly needed.

That was Franklin Roosevelt, on the fourth of March, nineteen thirty three. The banks were failing. Farm prices had collapsed.

Millions of men and women could not find work. And he looked out at all of it and said that the country was stricken by no plague of locusts, that nature still offered her bounty and plenty was at the doorstep, that the problem lay not in the supply of good things but in how they were being used and how they were being measured.

[SLOW]

I am going to argue that the same is true of us. We are not poor. We are not starving.

We are not short of medicine, or information, or entertainment. We are not short of goods.

We are short of each other.

[PAUSE]

That is the whole argument in a sentence, and if you remember nothing else from tonight, remember that. Everything that follows is evidence for it, or an answer to someone who doubts it, or a proposal for what to do about it.

One more thing before we start. I am not going to tell you that the past was a golden age. It was not.

The America of nineteen fifty was crueler in many ways than the America of today, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling nostalgia. Women had fewer choices. Black Americans lived under legal apartheid in much of the country.

People with mental illness were locked away and forgotten. Many of the people who answered the happiness surveys of the nineteen seventies were happy in a country that was not fair to their neighbors.

So this is not a speech about going back. It is a speech about a paradox that ought to trouble every one of us, whatever our politics. We fixed many of the old injustices, though not all of them.

We became vastly richer. We cured diseases that killed our grandparents. We put the sum of human knowledge into every pocket.

And somewhere along the way, we stopped being happy.

[LET IT LAND]

Let us first know where we are.

## II. The Richest Nation That Ever Lived

Before we look at the sorrow, I want you to see the wealth, because the one makes no sense without the other.

Economists have a measure called real gross domestic product per person. It takes everything the country produces in a year, adjusts it for inflation, and divides it by the number of people. It is a crude measure, and I will have harsh words for it later. But it tells you, roughly, how much stuff the average American has access to.

In the first quarter of nineteen seventy two, the year our oldest happiness survey was getting started, that figure stood at about twenty six thousand nine hundred dollars per person, measured in two thousand seventeen dollars.

In the second quarter of this year, it stood at about seventy one thousand two hundred.

[PAUSE]

Adjusted for inflation, the average American today lives in an economy that produces roughly two and a half times as much per person as the economy their parents or grandparents lived in half a century ago.

Two and a half times.

If you had gone to an American in nineteen seventy two and told them that their grandchildren would live in a country two and a half times richer, they would have imagined paradise. They would have imagined the end of want. They would have assumed, quite reasonably, that a people so rich would surely be content.

And it is not only the averages. Look at the things that money buys that matter most. The typical American in twenty twenty four could expect to live seventy nine years, according to the National Center for Health Statistics.

That figure rose by more than half a year from twenty twenty three alone, as the country recovered from the pandemic and as deaths from accidents and unintentional injuries, overdoses among them, began, at last, to fall. Our grandparents would have counted those extra years as a miracle.

Look at the houses, with their dishwashers and central air, things that were luxuries for many families of the nineteen seventies. Look at the cars, with their airbags and backup cameras and crumple zones. Look at the grocery stores, with aisles of food from every continent in every season. Look at the phone in your pocket. In nineteen seventy two it would have been science fiction. Today it calls up nearly any song ever recorded, any book in the public domain, any lecture a great university has chosen to share.

[BUILD]

We have medicines for conditions that were death sentences. We have air conditioning in places where summer used to kill. We have instant communication with anyone, anywhere, at any hour. We have more choices of career, of partner, of faith, of place to live, than any people who ever walked the earth.

I am not saying this to be smug. I know, and I will talk about it later, that the gains have not been shared evenly. Median household income in twenty twenty four, according to the Census Bureau, was about eighty three thousand seven hundred dollars, barely changed from the year before.

Rents are punishing. Health care is expensive and confusing. Millions of families live one emergency away from ruin.

All of that is real and all of that matters.

But I want you to hold two facts in your mind at the same time, because the whole argument of this evening depends on it.

The first fact is that this is, by the cold arithmetic of output, the richest large society in the history of the species.

The second fact is the one I am about to prove to you. By the measures we have, this society has never been more miserable.

[PAUSE]

Franklin Roosevelt, in that first inaugural, made a point that sounds radical even now. He said that happiness does not lie in the mere possession of money. It lies, he said, in the joy of achievement, in the thrill of creative effort. He warned his listeners against chasing what he called evanescent profits, and he said the country had to stop treating material wealth as the standard of success.

He said that in nineteen thirty three, in the middle of a Depression, to a country that had very little money to possess. It was a brave thing to say to hungry people.

It is a much easier thing to say to us. We have the money. We have tried the experiment that the hungry people of nineteen thirty three could only dream about.

We got rich. We got rich beyond the wildest projections of the economists of that era.

And the experiment has returned its result.

[SLOW]

The result is that wealth, on its own, did not make us well.

There is an old idea in economics, named for a man called Richard Easterlin, who in nineteen seventy four published a paper with a wonderfully blunt title: Does Economic Growth Improve the Human Lot? Easterlin noticed something strange. At any single moment, richer people tend to report being happier than poorer people.

Richer countries tend to report being happier than poorer countries. But over time, as a country grows richer, its average happiness does not seem to climb with it.

Other economists have argued with Easterlin for fifty years. Betsey Stevenson and Justin Wolfers, in two thousand eight, published evidence that across many countries, growth and happiness do move together. And others have pointed out that the American exception might have something to do with how unequally our growth has been shared, since median incomes rose far more slowly than incomes at the top.

I am not here to settle that academic fight. For the United States, both sides of it lead to the same uncomfortable place.

Either growth does not buy happiness at the level of a nation. Or the way we have grown has left so many people behind that the average gains never reach the average heart.

Either way, the arithmetic of output has failed to deliver the thing it was supposed to deliver.

So let us call our witnesses, and let them tell us how badly.

## III. The First Witness: The Gallup Ledger

Our first witness is the Gallup organization, which most of you know for its presidential approval numbers. Since twenty fifteen, Gallup has been asking a large, carefully drawn panel of American adults two simple questions as part of what it calls its National Health and Well-Being Index.

The first question is whether a doctor or nurse has ever told you that you have depression. The second is whether you currently have depression or are currently being treated for it.

These are not perfect questions. Nobody says they are. But they are asked the same way, of the same kind of sample, year after year, and that consistency is what makes them powerful. You can watch the line move.

Here is where the line started. In twenty fifteen, about one in five American adults, nineteen point six percent, said they had been diagnosed with depression at some point in their lives. And the share who said they were depressed or being treated for depression right now was roughly one in ten.

Here is where the line is today.

In April of this year, twenty twenty six, Gallup reported that the share of American adults who say they currently have or are being treated for depression stood at nineteen point one percent in the first quarter. That projects to about fifty one million Americans.

And the quarter before that, the last three months of twenty twenty five, Gallup recorded the highest reading in the history of the series: twenty point zero percent. One in five American adults.

[LET IT LAND]

In a decade, the share of Americans who say they are depressed right now has risen by nearly nine percentage points. It has almost doubled.

The lifetime number has climbed too. Twenty nine and a half percent of American adults now say a doctor or nurse has told them, at some point, that they have depression. That is up from nineteen point six percent in twenty fifteen. Nearly three in ten.

Think about what that means in a room like this one. Look to your left and to your right. Count off ten people.

On average, three of them have been told by a medical professional that they have depression. Two of them are living with it right now.

[PAUSE]

Now, when I first saw these numbers, I assumed what you might be assuming. I assumed it was the pandemic. And it is true that the steepest part of the climb came after the spring of twenty twenty. Gallup itself said, in its September twenty twenty five report, that most of the increase had occurred since the onset of COVID nineteen.

But here is what should trouble us. The pandemic ended. The schools reopened.

The offices filled back up, at least partly. The restaurants and stadiums and churches came back. And the depression rate did not come down.

It kept rising. The record was set not in twenty twenty, not in twenty twenty one, but in the final quarter of twenty twenty five, years after the last lockdown.

Whatever the pandemic did to us, it did not do it alone, and it did not undo it when it left.

[SLOW]

Now look closer, because the averages hide the people who are hurting most.

Look at the young. In twenty seventeen, thirteen percent of American adults under the age of thirty told Gallup they were currently depressed. In twenty twenty six, that number was twenty eight percent. It more than doubled in under a decade.

More than one in four young American adults. These are people in what was supposed to be the most hopeful time of life. The years of first apartments and first loves and first real jobs. The years when, in every previous generation we have data on, people were among the happiest in the country.

And Gallup did something clever. It followed that same generation as it aged. The people who were eighteen to twenty nine in twenty seventeen are now twenty seven to thirty eight.

Back then, thirteen percent of them were depressed. Today, twenty four point six percent are. They did not grow out of it.

They carried it with them into their thirties.

[PAUSE]

Look at the poor. Take the households that earn less than twenty four thousand dollars a year. In twenty seventeen, twenty two point one percent of the adults in them said they were currently depressed. That was already high.

In twenty twenty six, it was thirty seven point four percent.

More than a third of our poorest neighbors. A fifteen point increase in nine years.

I will come back to that number, because I think it is the key that unlocks much of this evening. For now I just want you to notice that the burden of this crisis is not spread evenly. It falls hardest on the people with the least.

And look at the lonely. Gallup asks people whether they felt loneliness a lot of the day yesterday. Not in general, not over their lives, but yesterday.

In twenty twenty five, twenty one percent of adults said yes. Among adults eighteen to twenty nine, it was twenty nine percent, the highest of any age group.

And here is the link that matters. Among Americans who were lonely the day before the survey, thirty three percent reported depression. Among those who were not lonely, it was thirteen percent.

One in three, against one in eight. Remember that pairing. Loneliness and depression travel together, and before the night is over I will argue that the first is driving much of the second.

[PAUSE]

So that is our first witness. A large, consistent, carefully weighted survey that has watched the inner life of this country for a decade, and has seen the share of Americans living with depression rise to the highest level it has ever recorded.

Highest among the young. Highest among the poor. Highest among the lonely.

A clever critic might say: that is just one pollster. And it is self-reported. People decide for themselves what counts.

Fair enough. Let us call a witness who does not rely on anyone's self-diagnosis.

## IV. The Second Witness: The Government's Own Instruments

Our second witness is the National Center for Health Statistics, a quiet corner of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention that most Americans have never heard of. It is staffed by people whose job is to count things carefully and say exactly what the counts show, no more and no less.

Every couple of years, the Center runs something called the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. It is one of the most remarkable instruments in American public life. Researchers select households at random from across the country.

They send out mobile examination centers, trucks full of medical equipment. They take blood pressure and blood samples and measurements. And they ask a standard set of nine questions about mood.

Those nine questions are called the Patient Health Questionnaire. Doctors use them in clinics all over the world. They ask about the last two weeks.

I am going to read them to you now. Do not raise your hand. Just answer them, silently, for yourself.

[SLOW]

Have you had little interest or pleasure in doing things? Have you felt down, depressed or hopeless? Have you had trouble sleeping, or slept too much?

Have you felt tired, or had little energy? Have you had a poor appetite, or overeaten? Have you felt bad about yourself?

Have you had trouble concentrating? Have you moved or spoken so slowly that others noticed, or been so restless you could not sit still? And the hardest one: have you had thoughts that you would be better off dead?

[PAUSE]

Some of you answered yes to more of those than you expected. If that last one was among them, I will come back to you before we finish tonight, and I mean that.

Here is why those questions matter for our argument. This is not asking people whether a doctor ever gave them a label. It is asking a random sample of Americans, the same way every time, about their actual symptoms over the last fourteen days.

It does not depend on whether you have health insurance. It does not depend on whether you ever went to a therapist. It does not depend on whether depression has become less stigmatized.

It just asks how you have been.

In April of twenty twenty five, the Center published the results for the period from August twenty twenty one to August twenty twenty three. Among Americans aged twelve and older, thirteen point one percent had depression in the past two weeks.

Ten years earlier, in twenty thirteen and twenty fourteen, using the same questionnaire, the figure was eight point two percent.

That is a rise of roughly sixty percent in a decade.

[LET IT LAND]

Look at who carries it. Among adolescents aged twelve to nineteen, the figure was nineteen point two percent, nearly one in five. Among teenage girls, it was twenty six and a half percent. More than one in four teenage girls in America, screened at random, met the threshold for depression in the previous two weeks.

And at the other end of life, among Americans sixty and older, the figure was eight point seven percent. Depression in this survey falls with age, which is the reverse of what many people assume and the reverse of what you might expect if this were simply about bodies wearing out.

Look at income. Among Americans living below the federal poverty line, twenty two point one percent had depression. Among those with family incomes at four times the poverty line or more, it was seven point four percent. Three times the rate.

And look at what depression does to people's lives. Of the Americans who screened positive, eighty seven point nine percent said it made it at least somewhat difficult to do their work, take care of things at home, or get along with other people. About three in ten said it made those things very difficult or extremely difficult.

This is not the ordinary sadness of a bad week. This is a condition that stops people from functioning.

[PAUSE]

And here is a number that should shame us. Of all those Americans with depression, only thirty nine point three percent had received counseling or therapy from a mental health professional in the previous year. Six in ten had not.

So when someone tells you that our depression numbers are high only because everyone is in therapy now, remember this. In a random sample, most of the people with measurable depression had not seen a therapist at all.

The same agency tells the same story in a second language. The language of prescriptions.

In the late nineteen eighties, antidepressants were relatively uncommon. Over the next two decades, the Center reported, their use among Americans twelve and older rose by nearly four hundred percent.

By the years two thousand five to two thousand eight, about eleven percent of Americans twelve and older were taking them. And more than sixty percent of those people had been taking them for two years or longer.

By twenty fifteen to twenty eighteen, thirteen point two percent of American adults had used an antidepressant in the past thirty days. Among women aged sixty and over, it was twenty four point three percent. Nearly one in four.

And in twenty twenty three, according to the National Health Interview Survey, eleven point four percent of American adults were taking prescription medication specifically for depression. Fifteen point three percent of women. Seven point four percent of men.

Higher among the poor than the rich. Higher in rural counties than in big cities.

[PAUSE]

I want to be careful here, because I know some of you in this room take these medicines, and some of you are alive because of them. I am not making an argument against antidepressants. For many people they are a lifeline, and anyone who shames a person for taking medicine their doctor prescribed should be ashamed of themselves.

I am making a different argument. I am saying that a nation where tens of millions of adults take medicine every day for their mood is a nation telling you something about itself. A pill can lift a person out of the pit. It cannot tell you why so many people are falling into it.

One more voice from the federal government, and then we move on. The National Survey on Drug Use and Health, run each year by the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, and summarized by the National Institute of Mental Health. In twenty twenty one, it estimated that twenty one million American adults, eight point three percent, had experienced a major depressive episode in the past year.

Among young adults aged eighteen to twenty five, it was eighteen point six percent. Among adolescents aged twelve to seventeen, it was twenty point one percent, five million young people. Among adolescent girls, twenty nine point two percent.

The government itself cautions that the twenty twenty one numbers are not directly comparable to earlier years because the survey changed its methods during the pandemic. I respect that caution, and I will not draw a trend line through it. But I do not need a trend line to know that five million American adolescents in a major depressive episode in a single year is an emergency.

[PAUSE]

So that is our second witness. Not a private pollster. Not a self-reported diagnosis.

The federal government's own random samples, its own symptom screens, its own prescription records. And they say the same thing as the first witness. More depression than a decade ago.

Most of it among the young. Most of it among the poor. Most of it untreated.

Two witnesses, two methods, one story.

## V. The Third Witness: The Mirror of the World

Our third witness is not American at all. It is the whole world, holding up a mirror.

Almost every year since twenty twelve, a group of economists and researchers has published something called the World Happiness Report. It draws on the Gallup World Poll, which asks people in well over a hundred countries a deceptively simple question. Imagine a ladder, with steps numbered from zero at the bottom to ten at the top.

The top of the ladder represents the best possible life for you. The bottom represents the worst possible life. On which step do you personally feel you stand at this time?

It is called the Cantril ladder, after the researcher who designed it. And what I love about it is that it does not ask you about happiness as a feeling. It asks you to judge your life as a whole. It asks you to step back and evaluate.

In that very first report, in twenty twelve, the United States ranked eleventh in the world. Not first; we have never been first. But eleventh, in the company of the Nordic countries, the Netherlands, Switzerland, Canada, Australia. A rich democracy, roughly where you would expect a rich democracy to be.

That eleventh place turned out to be our high water mark. We have never reached it again.

[SLOW]

By twenty twenty three, we had slipped to fifteenth. In twenty twenty four, we fell to twenty third, and for the first time in the history of the report, the United States was not among the twenty happiest countries in the world. In twenty twenty five, we fell again, to twenty fourth, the lowest ranking we have ever recorded.

In March of this year, the twenty twenty six report put us at twenty third, up one place. Here is the honest part. It is a small improvement, and I will take any improvement. But it means that three years running, the richest major country on earth has stood outside the top twenty, behind countries with a fraction of our income per person.

[LET IT LAND]

And here is the part that should stop us cold. In twenty twenty four, the report broke the rankings down by age. Older Americans ranked tenth in the world.

Still near the top. Still roughly where America used to be.

Americans under thirty ranked sixty second.

[PAUSE]

Sixty second. Our young people, the inheritors of the richest economy in history, rated their lives below the young people of dozens of countries far poorer than ours.

When reporters asked Gallup why, one of its researchers pointed first to social support. Younger Americans, it seems, are less likely to feel they have people they can count on when they need help.

People they can count on. That is not a luxury good. It is the floor a life stands on.

[PAUSE]

The twenty twenty five report found something I have not been able to stop thinking about since I read it. In twenty twenty three, roughly one in four Americans said they had eaten all of their meals alone the previous day. Every meal, alone.

That share was up fifty three percent from two thousand three. And it rose in every age group, most of all among the young.

The report's authors found that sharing meals with others is one of the strongest predictors of how people evaluate their lives, comparable in its effect to income and to unemployment, and that this held across ages, genders, and cultures.

One of the oldest human acts, as old as the campfire, turns out to matter about as much as a paycheck. Breaking bread together. And a quarter of us did not do it once in an entire day.

The same report noted that deaths of despair, the deaths from suicide, from drugs, from alcohol, have fallen by about three quarters across dozens of countries since the year two thousand. In the United States, they rose.

The world got better at keeping its people alive in the face of despair. We got worse.

And this year's report, twenty twenty six, turned its attention to young people and social media. Its headline finding was that in North America and Western Europe, young people are much less happy than they were fifteen years ago, a decline that coincides with a sharp rise in social media use. I will come back to the question of whether the one caused the other, because that is a genuine scientific fight and I will not pretend it is settled.

For now, notice only this. The decline was not everywhere. It was sharpest in a particular set of rich, highly connected countries: the United States, Canada, Australia, New Zealand.

Something is happening to the young people of the richest, most connected societies on earth. And we are leading the way.

[PAUSE]

So that is our third witness. The mirror of the world. And in that mirror we see a country that stood eleventh when the report began and has slid to the edge of the top twenty and below it.

Not because other countries leapt ahead of us in wealth. They did not. Because our own people, especially our young, have been rating their lives lower and lower.

## VI. The Fourth Witness: The Oldest Question

Our fourth witness is the oldest of them all.

In nineteen seventy two, a research center at the University of Chicago began something called the General Social Survey. Every year or two since then, its interviewers have sat down with a random sample of American adults and asked them hundreds of questions about their lives, their beliefs, their families, their work. And one question has been asked, in nearly the same words, for more than half a century.

You know it already. It is the question I opened with.

Taken all together, how would you say things are these days? Would you say that you are very happy, pretty happy, or not too happy?

It is a plain question. Some would say a crude one. But its crudeness is its strength.

The same question, asked of Americans through Watergate and the oil shocks, through the Reagan years and the end of the Cold War, through the dot com boom and the September eleventh attacks and the financial crisis. It is a thread running through fifty years of American life.

And for most of those fifty years, the answer barely moved. Across the whole history of the survey, about three in ten Americans have said they were very happy. Through recessions and wars and scandals, it stayed in a fairly narrow band. Americans were remarkably steady in their answer.

In twenty eighteen, thirty one percent said very happy. Thirteen percent said not too happy.

[SLOW]

Then came twenty twenty one. The share saying very happy fell to nineteen percent. The share saying not too happy rose to twenty four percent.

For the first time in the history of the General Social Survey, more Americans said they were not too happy than said they were very happy.

[LET IT LAND]

Now, here is the honest part, and it is an important one. In twenty twenty one, because of the pandemic, the survey could not send interviewers into people's homes. It moved to a format where people filled out the questions themselves, mostly online.

And researchers know that people tend to give gloomier answers when they are filling out a form alone than when they are speaking to a friendly stranger across a kitchen table. So part of that historic drop may be an artifact of how the question was asked, not only of how people felt.

I do not want to hide that. A speaker who hides the weak points of his own evidence deserves to lose the argument.

But here is the answer. When the survey brought interviewers back into people's homes, alongside other methods, in twenty twenty two and again in twenty twenty four, happiness did not bounce back to where it had been. The twenty twenty four data show the share of very happy Americans still well below the pre-pandemic level. And among Americans aged eighteen to twenty nine, average happiness in twenty twenty four sat below every reading recorded before twenty eighteen, below even the dip that followed the great financial crisis, which recovered within a couple of surveys.

[PAUSE]

So the oldest question in American social science, asked of ordinary people since nineteen seventy two, gives the same answer as the newest polls and the government's symptom screens and the world's ladder. Something broke, and it has not healed.

And here is what makes this fourth witness so important to our case. Remember Richard Easterlin. His paradox was built on exactly this kind of data, the long, flat line of American happiness through decades of growth. For most of the twentieth century, the puzzle was that we got richer and stayed the same.

The puzzle of our century is worse. We got richer and got sadder.

Real output per person more than doubled between the early nineteen seventies and the end of the twenty tens. And happiness stayed flat. Then output kept growing, through the twenty twenties, to roughly two and a half times its nineteen seventy two level. And happiness fell.

If the Easterlin paradox was a warning, our moment is the alarm. Money did not merely fail to buy happiness. While the money kept arriving, something else was quietly leaving.

[PAUSE]

Four witnesses. A private pollster, asking about diagnoses. The federal government, screening for symptoms in a random sample and counting prescriptions.

The world, asking us to rate our lives on a ladder. And the oldest survey we have, asking whether we are happy.

They use different questions. They use different methods. They are run by different people with different interests.

Not one of them was designed to prove the point I am making. And every one of them points in the same direction.

When four witnesses who have never met each other tell the same story, a jury is entitled to believe them.

But there is one more witness. And this one does not answer questions at all.

## VII. The Children

Before we come to that last witness, I need to stop and talk about our children. Because if there is one place where every line of evidence converges, it is there.

Every two years, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention runs the Youth Risk Behavior Survey. It goes into public and private high schools across the country and asks students, anonymously, about their lives. About drugs and drinking, about violence, about sex, about sleep. And about how they feel.

One question asks whether, during the past twelve months, a student felt so sad or hopeless almost every day for two weeks or more in a row that they stopped doing some of their usual activities.

In twenty twenty three, thirty nine point seven percent of American high school students said yes.

Four in ten.

Among high school girls, it was fifty two point six percent. More than half of the teenage girls in America said that, at some point in the past year, they had felt so sad or hopeless, for weeks on end, that they stopped doing the things they normally do.

[LET IT LAND]

Twenty point four percent of high school students said they had seriously considered attempting suicide in the past year. One in five. Nine point five percent said they had actually attempted it. Roughly one in ten.

Picture a high school classroom of thirty students. On average, about twelve of them have been through a stretch of persistent hopelessness this year. Six have seriously thought about ending their lives. Nearly three have tried.

That is not a classroom. That is a triage ward.

[PAUSE]

The CDC's own summary of ten years of this survey, from twenty thirteen to twenty twenty three, put it plainly. Over that decade, substance use among high school students went down. That is good news, and it is worth saying. But poor mental health, and suicidal thoughts and behaviors, went up.

Think about how strange that is. The things we spent decades warning teenagers about, drinking, smoking, drugs, early sex, all declined. Our kids are, by many traditional measures, more careful and more cautious than any generation in memory. And they are sadder.

[SLOW]

Now here is the honest part. Between twenty twenty one and twenty twenty three, the CDC saw what it called early signs of improvement in student mental health. The share of students reporting persistent sadness or hopelessness edged down to just under forty percent in twenty twenty three. That is movement in the right direction, and I thank every teacher, counselor, parent and young person who helped make it happen.

But do not mistake a slight easing of a fever for health. We are talking about four in ten children, down from a little more. And by the CDC's own account, the decade as a whole moved the wrong way. If your child's temperature came down from one hundred and four to one hundred and three, you would not cancel the appointment with the doctor.

Remember what the other witnesses said about the young. The government's symptom screen found nearly one in five adolescents with depression in the previous two weeks, and more than one in four teenage girls. The national drug use survey found five million adolescents in a major depressive episode in a single year.

Gallup found that the share of adults under thirty who are currently depressed more than doubled since twenty seventeen. The World Happiness Report found Americans under thirty ranked sixty second in the world.

Four instruments. Four methods. One generation.

So what happened to them?

Here I have to tread carefully, because there is a fierce argument underway, and the people on both sides are serious scholars who care deeply about children.

In March of twenty twenty four, the social psychologist Jonathan Haidt published a book called The Anxious Generation. His argument, in brief, is that between roughly twenty ten and twenty fifteen, childhood in the rich world was rewired. Smartphones with front facing cameras and always on social media arrived in the pockets of teenagers.

At the same time, he argues, parents and schools had grown so protective that children had lost much of the unsupervised, physical, risky play that used to build resilience. Less play in the real world. More life on a screen.

He points to a sharp rise in teen anxiety, depression and self harm that begins around that period, and he proposes reforms that include phone free schools, later smartphones, later social media accounts, and more independence and free play.

The book became a phenomenon, and it put the question at the center of a national conversation.

But other researchers push back hard. The psychologist Candice Odgers, reviewing the book in the journal Nature, argued that most of the research does not show large or consistent harms from social media, that the arrows of cause and effect could run the other way, with struggling teenagers turning to their phones rather than phones causing the struggle, and that a single villain could distract us from other causes that deserve our attention. She concluded, in effect, that there are no simple answers.

And the World Happiness Report this year, which devoted itself to exactly this question, landed somewhere in between. One chapter argued that social media is harming adolescents at a scale large enough to change things at the level of whole populations. Other chapters stressed that turning the research into policy requires care, and that the global picture is complex.

[SLOW]

Here is where I come down, and I want you to hear it as my judgment, not as settled science.

I do not believe there is one cause. I do not believe the phone is the only villain. But I believe the timing, the concentration among the young, and the particular set of highly connected rich countries where the decline is sharpest, all point to something about how we have reorganized childhood and social life in the last fifteen years. And I believe that when we are unsure whether something is harming our children, and the cost of caution is small, we ought to lean toward caution.

Making a school day phone free costs us very little. Waiting a few more years to hand a child an unlimited window into the judgment of strangers costs us very little. If Haidt is wrong, we lose almost nothing. If he is right, and we do nothing, we lose a generation.

[PAUSE]

But I want to widen the lens, because if we make this only about phones, we will miss the larger point. The children are not a separate story. They are the leading edge of the same story.

They are living, more intensely and with fewer defenses, the life that the rest of us are living too. Less time together in person. Fewer meals at a shared table.

More hours alone with a glowing rectangle. More comparison and less belonging.

They are the canary. And the canary is telling us something about the air in the whole mine.

## VIII. The Ledger of the Dead

Now we come to our last witness. The one that does not answer surveys. The one that cannot be accused of exaggerating, or of being influenced by a change in survey mode, or of reflecting a new willingness to talk about feelings.

The death certificate.

[PAUSE]

In twenty twenty four, according to the National Center for Health Statistics, forty eight thousand eight hundred and twenty four Americans died by suicide. That is a rate of fourteen point four per hundred thousand people. Suicide was the tenth leading cause of death in this country.

Of those deaths, twenty seven thousand five hundred and ninety three involved a firearm. More than half.

Now the longer view, one step at a time.

In two thousand two, the age adjusted suicide rate in the United States was ten point nine per hundred thousand.

By twenty eighteen, it had climbed to fourteen point two.

It dipped in the first year of the pandemic, to thirteen point five. And by twenty twenty two it was back at fourteen point two, matching the old peak.

A rise of roughly thirty percent in twenty years. Thirty percent more of our neighbors, our brothers, our daughters, our friends, lost to suicide, in a country that was getting richer every year.

[LET IT LAND]

Behind every one of those numbers is a family that will never be the same. A mother who checks her phone for a text that will never come. A father who replays a conversation a thousand times.

A child who grows up with a hole where a parent used to be. When we talk about statistics in a speech like this, it is easy to forget that each digit is a person who was loved and is gone.

[PAUSE]

And suicide is only one of the three ways that despair kills.

In twenty fifteen, two Princeton economists, Anne Case and Angus Deaton, published a paper that changed how many of us think about this country. They found that death rates among middle aged white Americans, especially those without a college degree, had stopped falling and begun to rise, at a time when death rates were falling in nearly every other rich country and among other groups in this one. And the causes were not the usual ones. They were drug overdoses, alcohol related liver disease, and suicide.

They gave those causes a name that has stuck: deaths of despair. In their later book, Deaths of Despair and the Future of Capitalism, they argued that the deepest cause was not simply poverty but a loss of the structures that give life meaning. Good jobs that carried dignity.

Stable families. Unions, churches, communities. When those structures crumble, they argued, people do not only lose income.

They lose a reason to get up in the morning.

Now the overdoses. In two thousand three, according to the National Center for Health Statistics, twenty five thousand seven hundred and eighty five Americans died of drug overdoses.

By twenty twenty two, the number was one hundred and seven thousand nine hundred and forty one. The rate nearly quadrupled.

One hundred and seven thousand people in a single year. That is more than the population of many American cities, gone in twelve months, from drugs.

[PAUSE]

And now, because I will not hide good news to make my case stronger, here is something that should give every one of us hope.

In twenty twenty three, overdose deaths fell slightly. In twenty twenty four, they fell to seventy nine thousand three hundred and eighty four. A drop of roughly a quarter in a single year.

That is one of the most important pieces of good news in American public health in a generation. Tens of thousands of people who would have died are alive. Researchers are still debating exactly why it happened.

The candidates include wider access to the overdose reversal drug naloxone, more treatment, and changes in the illicit drug supply, alongside the work of countless people in emergency rooms, recovery programs and outreach vans. Whatever the precise mix, it shows that these deaths are not fate. They can be prevented.

But notice what it also shows. Seventy nine thousand deaths is still roughly three times the number from two thousand three.

And suicide has not fallen in the same way. The suicides of twenty twenty four were only slightly fewer than those of twenty twenty two, when roughly forty nine thousand four hundred Americans died. And in twenty twenty four, suicide climbed into the ten leading causes of death in this country.

So the ledger of the dead agrees with our other witnesses. The surveys say we are sadder. The prescriptions say we are medicating more.

The world says we are slipping. And the death certificates say that despair is killing Americans at rates that would have been unthinkable a generation ago.

[PAUSE]

That is the case for the prosecution. Five witnesses.

A decade of Gallup. Decades of federal screens and prescription records. The world's happiness ladder. Half a century of the oldest question. And the cold arithmetic of the morgue.

Now let me give the defense its turn.

## IX. The Strongest Objection

Every honest argument has to face its strongest opponent. Not a straw man. The real thing, at full strength. So let me make the case against everything I have said tonight, as well as I can make it.

The objection goes like this.

Americans are not actually more miserable. They are more willing to talk about it. Thirty years ago, depression was a shameful secret.

A man who admitted to it might lose his job. A woman who admitted to it might be told she was hysterical. People suffered in silence, drank in silence, and sometimes died in silence, and nobody counted them as depressed because nobody used the word.

Today, depression is discussed openly. Celebrities talk about therapy. Athletes talk about anxiety.

Doctors screen for depression at routine checkups. Teenagers have a vocabulary for their inner lives that their grandparents never had.

So of course the numbers went up, the objection says. We turned on the lights, and now we can see what was always in the room.

[SLOW]

And the objection has a second part. Things are already getting better. Overdose deaths fell by roughly a quarter in a single year.

Life expectancy rose to seventy nine. The high school sadness numbers eased a little between twenty twenty one and twenty twenty three. Gallup's depression rate in the first quarter of this year was slightly below the record of the quarter before.

America even climbed one place in the world happiness rankings. Why call it the worst time ever, when the curve may already be bending?

And there is a third part, the historian's part. Never more miserable? Really?

More miserable than during the Great Depression, when, by the best later estimates, roughly a quarter of the workforce could not find a job? More miserable than during the Civil War, when an estimated seven hundred thousand soldiers died and much of the South lay in ruins? More miserable than a slave in a cotton field, or a child in a coal mine?

[PAUSE]

That is the objection, and I want to say that every piece of it contains truth. Let me answer each part in turn.

Start with the historian, because the historian is right, and I concede the point without reservation. When I say Americans have never been more miserable, I mean never since we started measuring. We do not have a General Social Survey from eighteen sixty three.

We do not have a symptom screen from the breadlines of nineteen thirty three. I would never claim that a teenager scrolling in a comfortable suburban bedroom suffers as an enslaved person suffered. There is no comparison, and to make one would be obscene.

My claim is narrower and, I think, more disturbing. In the half century during which we have actually been asking Americans how they feel, during which we have built the most careful instruments any society has ever built to measure its own inner life, those instruments are now reading the lowest happiness and the highest depression they have ever recorded. And that is happening at the very moment of our greatest material abundance.

The Great Depression was miserable because people were poor. We are miserable while rich. That is the paradox, and it demands an explanation.

Now the second part, the good news. I have given you all of it tonight, honestly, and I welcome every bit of it. But look at what it is.

A fall in overdose deaths that leaves them still about three times their level of two thousand three. A one place rise in a world ranking that leaves us outside the top twenty for the third year in a row. A slight easing in teen hopelessness that still leaves four in ten high school students in its grip.

A quarter of Gallup data that comes in just below the all time record.

These are not signs that the fever has broken. They are signs that the patient is still alive and can recover, which is exactly what I believe. Good news at the margins is a reason to act, not a reason to relax.

[PAUSE]

Now the heart of the objection, the stigma argument. This is the strongest part, and I want to take it most seriously.

It is true that less stigma and more screening would raise the number of people with a depression diagnosis, even if the true amount of suffering stayed the same. That is a real effect, and I suspect it explains part of the rise in Gallup's lifetime diagnosis numbers. If you want to discount those numbers somewhat, I will not argue.

But the stigma argument has a weakness. It explains why diagnoses would go up. It does not explain why everything else would go up with them.

[SLOW]

Ask yourself. Does the end of stigma explain why a random sample of Americans, screened with the same nine questions in their homes, shows depressive symptoms rising by roughly sixty percent in a decade? The questionnaire does not ask whether you have a diagnosis.

It asks whether you have been sleeping, eating, concentrating, enjoying things. Perhaps people are a little more willing to admit to those symptoms. But sixty percent more willing, in ten years?

Does the end of stigma explain why Americans under thirty rate their whole lives, on a ladder from zero to ten, lower than young people in sixty one other countries? The ladder question does not mention depression at all.

Does the end of stigma explain why the share of Americans saying they are very happy, a question that has nothing to do with mental illness, fell to levels never seen in fifty years of asking? Why would less shame about depression make people less likely to say they are happy?

Does the end of stigma explain why a quarter of Americans eat every meal of the day alone?

And most of all, does the end of stigma explain the death certificates? A suicide is not a survey response. It is not affected by whether people feel comfortable talking to an interviewer.

If anything, less stigma and more treatment should push the suicide rate down. Instead it rose by about thirty percent over twenty years.

When the soft measures and the hard measures, the questions about feelings and the counts of the dead, all move in the same direction at the same time, the simplest explanation is not that everyone has suddenly become more honest. The simplest explanation is that more people are suffering.

[PAUSE]

So here is where I land. Some of the rise in diagnoses is real awareness, and that is good. Some of the drop in twenty twenty one happiness is survey mode, and I have told you so.

Some of the improvement of the last year or two is real, and thank God for it. But when you have subtracted every honest discount, you are left with a country whose people are sadder, lonelier, and more likely to die of despair than they were a generation ago, despite being far richer.

The defense has made its best case. I respect it. And the verdict stands.

## X. The Timbers

Now we turn from diagnosis toward understanding.

In that Springfield speech of eighteen fifty eight, Lincoln did something memorable. He asked his audience to imagine a collection of framed timbers, cut at different times and places by different workmen, and then, when they are brought together, they fit exactly. Every tenon in its mortise.

Every length exactly right. And he said that when you see a frame like that, you cannot help believing that the workmen had been working from a common plan.

Lincoln used that image to argue that the political events of his day were not accidents. I want to borrow his image, but I want to change it in one crucial way.

[SLOW]

The timbers I am about to show you also fit together. They fit together astonishingly well. But there was no common plan.

There was no architect. There is no secret group of men in a back room who decided to make Americans miserable. I want to be absolutely clear about that, because the temptation in times like these is to look for a villain, and the villain-hunting itself makes us sicker.

Our timbers were cut by millions of people, each making choices that seemed reasonable at the time. A family moving for a better job. A company designing an app people would want to open.

A town closing a library to balance its budget. A parent deciding the park was not safe. A worker deciding there was no time for the bowling league.

No one intended the frame. And yet, here it is, standing over us.

[PAUSE]

The first timber is loneliness.

We have already heard the numbers. One in five adults lonely a lot of the day yesterday. Nearly three in ten young adults. A depression rate of one in three among the lonely, against one in eight among those who are not.

In May of twenty twenty three, the Surgeon General of the United States, Vivek Murthy, took the unusual step of issuing a formal advisory on what he called our epidemic of loneliness and isolation. He compared the threat to public health to smoking and obesity. That sounds like exaggeration, until you look at the research.

In twenty ten, a team led by the psychologist Julianne Holt-Lunstad pooled one hundred and forty eight studies, covering more than three hundred thousand people. They found that people with stronger social relationships had a fifty percent higher likelihood of survival over the periods studied. An effect, the authors wrote, comparable with quitting smoking.

Loneliness is not merely a feeling. It is a risk factor for death.

The second timber is the empty table.

I told you about the World Happiness Report finding that about one in four Americans ate every meal alone the day before they were surveyed, a figure that rose by more than half in twenty years. Picture it. A country of separate plates.

Breakfast in the car. Lunch at the desk. Dinner in front of a screen.

Not because anyone decided that shared meals were bad. Because schedules diverged, households shrank, commutes lengthened, and the screen was always there, offering company that is not company.

The report found that sharing meals rivals income as a predictor of how people rate their lives. We have spent fifty years chasing the income. We let the table go.

The third timber is the screen.

I have told you that the science on social media is contested, and it is. But even if you are a skeptic about the specific harms, you cannot deny the sheer displacement. Every hour spent alone with a device is an hour not spent with a person in the same room.

The scholars may argue about whether the screen is poison. No one argues that it is a substitute for a friend's face across a table, or a game of pickup basketball, or a long, aimless conversation on a porch on a summer night.

And for the young, the screen arrived at the exact moment in human development when we are most sensitive to the judgment of our peers. It took the ancient adolescent fear of being left out, and gave it a live scoreboard.

The fourth timber is the scoreboard of money.

Franklin Roosevelt, in that first inaugural, aimed his fire at what he called the money changers, the bankers and speculators whose recklessness had helped bring on the crash. He said they had fled from their high seats in the temple of our civilization. He said they knew only the rules of a generation of self seekers.

I am not going to aim my fire at bankers tonight. Our problem is broader and more personal than that. Our money changers are not only on Wall Street.

They are in us. We have become a society that measures almost everything by money and output. We measure our nation's health by gross domestic product.

We measure our schools by test scores that are sold as tickets to higher earnings. We measure our own worth by our salaries and our follower counts and the square footage of our homes.

And when a society measures everything by money, it starves everything that money cannot measure. Friendship. Neighborliness.

Worship. Play. Rest.

The slow, unprofitable work of raising children and caring for the old.

The fifth timber is the loss of what Case and Deaton might call the structures of meaning. The good job with dignity, which for millions of Americans without a college degree has become harder to find. The union hall and the lodge and the church basement.

The bowling league that the political scientist Robert Putnam made famous when he wrote about Americans bowling alone. These were never perfect institutions. Many of them excluded people unjustly.

But they gave people a place to belong, a role to play, someone who would notice if they did not show up.

As they thinned out, nothing equally sturdy replaced them. We were promised that online communities would take their place. For some people, they have. For many more, they have not.

Put the timbers together and look at the frame.

Fewer friends and less time with them. Fewer shared meals. More hours alone with screens designed to keep us scrolling. A culture that measures worth in money while the institutions that once gave meaning to ordinary lives wither away.

Each timber was cut separately, by different hands, for different reasons. And they fit together into a house in which a great many Americans now live alone, at a table set for one, staring at a small bright screen, and wondering why, in the richest country on earth, they feel so poor.

[LET IT LAND]

Let me put a face on that frame. She is not a real person. She is a composite, an illustration built from the patterns in the data I have shown you, so that we can see the numbers with a human face.

Call her Alicia. She is twenty six. She grew up in a mid sized town that used to have a plant, and a bowling alley, and a church her grandmother never missed.

The plant closed when she was in grade school. The bowling alley is a storage facility now. The church merged with another one two towns over, and nobody in her family goes anymore.

Alicia got her first smartphone at eleven, the same year as most of her friends. In middle school, the social life of her grade moved onto apps that ranked and measured everything. Who was invited.

Who was left out. How many people liked a photo, and how quickly. She learned to check, and check again, and check again.

She went to college, took on debt, and graduated into a job that pays a little more than her parents ever earned, though rent takes nearly half of it. She works from a laptop in a one bedroom apartment in a city where she knows about four people. Two of them are coworkers she has met in person twice.

On a typical weekday, Alicia eats breakfast standing at the counter, lunch at her laptop, and dinner on the couch with a show playing and her phone in her hand. She does not eat a single meal with another human being. She would be in the quarter of Americans the World Happiness Report counted.

She is not poor, by the standards of the world or of history. She has a car, a phone, a streaming subscription, a gym membership she rarely uses. Her grandparents, at her age, had less of almost everything you can buy.

[PAUSE]

But her grandmother, at twenty six, had a church on Sunday and a bowling league on Wednesday and a kitchen table where three generations argued over pot roast. She knew the names of everyone on her street. When she was sick, people brought casseroles. When she was sad, someone noticed.

Alicia has a thousand followers and no one who would notice if she did not leave her apartment for a week.

Last year, after months of not sleeping well, of losing interest in things she used to love, of a heaviness she could not explain, Alicia went to a clinic. The doctor gave her the nine question screen. She scored in the moderate range. The doctor prescribed an antidepressant and gave her the number of a therapist who had a three month waiting list.

The medicine helps a little. She is grateful for it. But in the quiet moments she wonders something she has never said out loud.

She wonders whether the problem is really inside her head at all. Or whether it is the shape of her life. The empty table.

The absent neighbors. The glowing screen that offers everything except a hand on the shoulder.

I think Alicia is asking exactly the right question. And I think the honest answer is: both. Her suffering is real, and her treatment is real, and she deserves every bit of it.

But she is also one person living inside a frame that tens of millions of Americans live inside. No pill can rebuild that frame for her. Only people can.

[LET IT LAND]

There are millions of Alicias in this country. Some of them are seventy, not twenty six. Some of them are men in rural counties who would never use the word depression and are dying of it anyway.

Some of them are teenagers who have never known a world without the scoreboard. Some of them are in this room.

They are not failures. They are what the frame produces.

No one built that house on purpose. But all of us live in it. And that means all of us can begin to take it apart, timber by timber, and build something better in its place.

## XI. The Pursuit of Happiness

Before I come to the wall that runs through our house, I want to step back and remind us that we are not the first Americans to wonder about any of this. The question of happiness is written into the founding of this country.

In seventeen seventy six, a committee of the Continental Congress, with Thomas Jefferson holding the pen, set down the reasons for a revolution. Among the rights they called unalienable, alongside life and liberty, they named the pursuit of happiness.

It is a strange and beautiful phrase. Not happiness itself; no government can hand that out. The pursuit of it. The founders believed that people had a right to seek a good life on their own terms, and that a government which made that pursuit impossible had lost its claim to rule.

I think about that phrase a great deal when I look at the numbers I have shown you tonight. Because the founders did not imagine happiness as something you buy. The word, in the eighteenth century, carried older meanings, drawn from the classical philosophers the founders had read.

It meant something closer to flourishing. A life lived well, in a community, with virtue and purpose. Not a feeling of pleasure, but a way of being.

[SLOW]

Some fifty five years after the Declaration, a young French aristocrat named Alexis de Tocqueville traveled across the United States and wrote one of the most perceptive books ever written about this country, Democracy in America. Two things about Americans struck him most.

The first was that they were joiners. Everywhere he went, he found Americans forming associations. Not only political parties and businesses, but societies for every purpose under the sun, religious and moral, serious and trivial.

Where a Frenchman would wait for the government to act, and an Englishman for a great lord, Americans simply got together with their neighbors and did it themselves. Tocqueville believed this habit of association was one of the great safeguards of American freedom, because it pulled people out of their private lives and into a common one.

The second thing he noticed was darker. He devoted a chapter of his book to the question of why Americans were so restless in the midst of their prosperity. He saw a people with more opportunity than any on earth, and yet anxious, hurried, never satisfied, always chasing the next thing, as though afraid that they would die before they had tasted every good that life could offer.

He thought this restlessness was the shadow side of equality and abundance. When anything seems possible, nothing ever feels like enough.

[PAUSE]

Hold those two observations side by side, because I think they explain our moment better than almost anything written since.

For most of our history, the restlessness Tocqueville saw was balanced by the associations he admired. Americans chased wealth, yes, often frantically. But they also went to church on Sunday and to the lodge on Tuesday and to the town meeting once a month.

They coached the Little League team and sat on the school board and volunteered at the fire department. The private hunger was held in check by public belonging.

What has happened in our time, I believe, is that the balance has tipped. The restlessness is still with us, sharper than ever, fed now by a device that shows us every hour exactly how much better everyone else seems to be doing. But the associations, the counterweight, have thinned. We kept the hunger and lost the table.

[BUILD]

About sixty years after Tocqueville published his book, at the end of the nineteenth century, the French sociologist Emile Durkheim published a book simply called Suicide. It was one of the founding works of modern social science. Durkheim's insight was that something as intensely private as the decision to end one's life also follows social patterns.

Suicide rates, he argued, rise when people are poorly integrated into their communities and when the norms that give shape to life break down. He had a word for that breakdown, anomie, a kind of normlessness, a condition in which people no longer know what they are supposed to want or how much is enough.

Durkheim was writing about Europe in the eighteen nineties. But read him today and it feels like he was writing about us. A society that has grown richer while its bonds have grown weaker. A society in which the old answers to the question of how to live have faded, and the new answer, keep acquiring, keep achieving, keep scrolling, offers no resting place.

And at the end of the twentieth century, the political scientist Robert Putnam gathered decades of evidence that Americans were withdrawing from the associations Tocqueville had praised. Fewer people in civic groups. Fewer at dinner parties.

Fewer in leagues. He called his book Bowling Alone, after the image of Americans still bowling but no longer in leagues with their neighbors. People argued with parts of it, as people argue with every important book.

But the image stuck, because people recognized themselves in it.

I tell you this history for a reason. When people hear the numbers I have shown you tonight, they often assume that the explanation must be something brand new, some recent invention, some recent villain. And recent changes matter, especially for the young.

But the deeper pattern is old. Thinkers who studied this country and others like it saw, a long time ago, that abundance without belonging breeds restlessness, and restlessness without belonging breeds despair.

[SLOW]

What is new in our time is the speed and the scale. The forces that Tocqueville and Durkheim and Putnam described have all accelerated at once. The wealth has grown faster.

The associations have thinned faster. The comparison has become constant. And our instruments, for the first time, are good enough to show us the result in real time.

The founders gave us the right to pursue happiness. They did not promise we would find it. And they certainly never imagined that we would pursue it so hard, in so many lonely directions, that we would run right past it.

## XII. A House Divided

Now I come to the image that gives this speech its spine.

Lincoln, quoting scripture, said that a house divided against itself cannot stand. He was talking about a nation half slave and half free, and he believed it could not endure that way permanently. It would become all one thing or all the other.

[PAUSE]

I want to be careful with Lincoln's words, because they were spoken about the gravest injustice in our history, and nothing in our moment is its equal. But the logic of his image applies to us in a narrower way. There is a wall running through the American house, and on either side of it, people are living in different emotional countries.

Look at the numbers again, and this time look at them as a map of that wall.

Among Americans living below the poverty line, the government's random symptom screen found depression in twenty two point one percent. Among those with incomes at four times the poverty line or more, seven point four percent. Three times the rate.

Among Americans in households earning under twenty four thousand dollars a year, Gallup found current depression of thirty seven point four percent in twenty twenty six, up from twenty two point one percent in twenty seventeen. The national average is about nineteen percent.

Among adults living below the poverty line, the rate of taking prescription medication for depression was nearly fifteen percent, against about ten percent for the well off.

[SLOW]

So yes, the whole country has grown sadder. But it has not grown sadder evenly. On one side of the wall, life is hard but bearable. On the other side, more than a third of adults are living with depression right now.

And the wall runs not only through income. It runs through age, with the young on the far side of it. It runs through geography, with prescription rates for depression higher in rural counties than in big cities.

It runs through sex, with women and teenage girls reporting depression at far higher rates than men and boys, even as men die by suicide at three to four times the rate of women. It runs through our politics, where the despair of one region becomes the grievance of one party and the contempt of the other.

[PAUSE]

Here is why this matters, and here is where I think Lincoln's logic truly applies.

A country cannot stand forever with a large part of its people in despair while another part prospers and wonders what all the fuss is about. Despair does not stay put. It turns into addiction, and addiction fills our emergency rooms and our jails.

It turns into isolation, and isolation turns into suspicion. It turns into the sense that the system is rigged and nobody is coming to help, and that sense turns into a politics of rage.

Last year's World Happiness Report found that falling life satisfaction goes along with more votes against the political establishment, here and in Europe. Unhappy voters who distrust others drift to one extreme; unhappy voters who still trust drift to the other. You do not have to share any particular political view to see the danger. Unhappy people are easier to divide.

Divided people are easier to rule. And a people who have lost faith that life can get better will eventually stop believing in the institutions that are supposed to make it better.

[BUILD]

I do not believe this house will fall. I believe what Lincoln believed about his: that it will not stay divided forever. It will become one thing or the other.

Either the despair on the far side of the wall will spread, as it has been spreading for a decade, until the misery of the poorest becomes the ordinary condition of the young and the middle, and a whole nation learns to live in a kind of low grey fog.

Or we will decide, together, that this is not acceptable, and we will begin to tear the wall down.

[LET IT LAND]

There is no stable middle. There is no permanent arrangement in which a rich country simply shrugs and accepts that a third of its poorest adults and a quarter of its young adults are depressed, and moves on. Either we fix it, or it grows.

## XIII. Fear Itself

I have spent a long time tonight on hard numbers. Now I want to talk about what we do with them.

[PAUSE]

When Franklin Roosevelt took the oath of office in March of nineteen thirty three, the country was not just poor. It was frightened. People were pulling their savings out of banks, which made the banks fail, which made people more frightened, which made them pull out more.

Fear was feeding on itself. It had become a machine that turned anxiety into catastrophe.

And so Roosevelt said the sentence that every schoolchild learns. He said that the only thing we have to fear is fear itself. He described it as a nameless, unreasoning, unjustified terror that paralyzes the efforts needed to turn retreat into advance.

That line is often quoted as a kind of pep talk. It was not a pep talk. It was a diagnosis.

Roosevelt understood that the greatest danger in a crisis is not the crisis itself. It is the paralysis that sets in when people come to believe nothing can be done.

[SLOW]

I want to tell you what I think the paralysis looks like in our time. It does not look like a run on the banks. It looks like a shrug.

It looks like the person who hears that one in five Americans is depressed and says, well, that is just modern life. It looks like the official who reads that four in ten high school students are persistently hopeless and says, well, kids today are fragile. It looks like the commentator who sees the suicide rate and says, well, people have always despaired. It looks like all of us, scrolling past another headline about loneliness on our way to the next headline about something else.

The shrug is our version of fear itself. It is resignation dressed up as sophistication. It is the belief that the numbers I have shown you tonight are simply the weather, something that happens to us, rather than the result of choices we have made and can make differently.

[PAUSE]

And here is the cruel irony. The shrug is itself a symptom. One of the defining features of depression is hopelessness, the belief that things cannot get better and that nothing you do will matter. A depressed person does not reach for help, because a depressed person does not believe help is possible.

A depressed nation behaves the same way. It stops believing in its own capacity to change. It treats every problem as unsolvable. It turns on itself.

So when I say we must reject the shrug, I am not asking you to cheer up. I am asking you to refuse to let the illness write the prescription.

[BUILD]

Roosevelt went on, in that address, to say something that I think is even more relevant to us than his line about fear. He said that the nation's distress came from no failure of substance. There was no plague of locusts.

Nature still offered her bounty. Plenty was at the doorstep. The problem was that the rulers of the exchange of goods had failed, and so had the values they lived by.

Now say it of us. Our distress comes from no failure of substance. We have more money than any society ever.

We have medical knowledge and effective treatments that did not exist a few generations ago. We have tools for connection that would have stunned Roosevelt's generation.

What we lack is not resources. What we lack is the decision to use them for the things that actually make human beings well.

And there is reason for hope in the numbers themselves, if you know where to look.

Look at the overdose deaths, falling by roughly a quarter in a single year, alongside years of hard work on naloxone and treatment and outreach. Whatever the full explanation turns out to be, it shows that despair's deadliest consequences can be bent downward.

Look at the high school numbers, edging in the right direction after twenty twenty one. That is a sign that the young can begin to recover.

Look at the World Happiness Report itself. The countries at the top are not simply the richest. The Gallup chief executive, introducing last year's report, put it in a single line: happiness, he said, is about trust, connection, and knowing people have your back.

[SLOW]

Trust, connection, and knowing people have your back. Not one of those things requires a breakthrough in technology. Not one requires another doubling of our economy. Every one of them is something human beings have known how to build for as long as there have been human beings.

That is the hopeful message hidden inside the bleak numbers. Our crisis is not a mystery beyond our understanding. We are not short of goods. We are short of each other. And that is the one shortage a free people can end on its own.

The only thing we have to fear is the shrug.

## XIV. What Recovery Would Ask of Us

Roosevelt did not stop at diagnosis. The second half of his address was a list of things to do. Put people to work.

Help the farmers. Stop the foreclosures. Reform the banks.

He was specific, because he knew that a nation in crisis does not need poetry alone. It needs a plan.

So let me be specific too. What follows is my argument, not a finding of any study. You may disagree with any part of it. But I would rather give you something concrete to argue with than leave you with a fog of good intentions.

[PAUSE]

First, we must measure what matters.

For almost a century, we have judged the health of this country primarily by one number, the size of its economy. When that number goes up, presidents take credit and markets cheer. When it goes down, heads roll. And for most of that time, almost nobody in power has had to answer for the numbers I have shown you tonight.

I believe that should change. The federal government already collects much of this data, scattered across agencies. It should bring it together into a single, regular, prominent national report on the wellbeing of the American people, depression and anxiety and loneliness and life satisfaction, released with the same fanfare as the jobs report.

What we measure, we manage. What we ignore, we lose.

Second, we must protect childhood.

Whatever the final verdict of the scientists on social media, I believe the precautionary steps are cheap and the potential benefits are large. Make the school day phone free, so that children can spend seven hours a day looking at each other instead of at screens. Support parents who want to delay smartphones and social media accounts, by making it normal rather than strange. Insist that the companies that design these products bear real responsibility for how they affect children, the way we insist that car makers build safe cars and toy makers build safe toys.

And give children back the unstructured, unsupervised time to play, to roam, to fail and get up again, that every previous generation took for granted. Open the school yards after hours. Build the parks.

Let them walk to school. Let them be bored, and discover what boredom teaches.

Third, we must make care reachable.

Remember the federal finding that six in ten Americans with measurable depression had not seen a mental health professional in the past year. Remember that the rates are highest among the poor, who are least able to pay for care, and in rural counties, where therapists are hardest to find.

I believe a rich nation should be ashamed of that gap. Put counselors in every school, in numbers that let them actually know the students. Make it easy to get mental health care through the same doctor you see for a sore throat.

Fund the crisis lines, so that anyone, in any county, at any hour, can reach a trained human voice. And keep investing in what has worked against overdose deaths, because the drop of twenty twenty four tells us that this work saves lives.

Fourth, we must take on the wall.

If the poorest Americans are depressed at three times the rate of the well off, then mental health policy is also economic policy. I am not going to pretend there is a simple formula here, and honest people of every party disagree about the best route. But I believe we should judge economic proposals not only by whether they grow the total, but by whether they reduce the grinding insecurity that the evidence links to despair.

Can a family afford a place to live? Can a worker count on enough hours to plan a week? Can a parent take a sick child to the doctor without choosing between the visit and the rent?

Insecurity is a mental health hazard. We should treat it like one.

Fifth, and this is the one that no government can do for us, we must rebuild the table.

The research on social connection is as close to a free lunch as public health ever gets. Strong relationships are associated with a fifty percent higher likelihood of survival. Sharing meals predicts how people rate their lives about as well as income. In the Gallup data, the lonely are depressed at more than two and a half times the rate of everyone else.

You cannot legislate friendship. But you can choose it.

[BUILD]

So here is what I am asking of each of you.

Eat with someone this week. Not in front of a screen. Across a table.

Invite the neighbor you have nodded at for three years and never spoken to. Call the friend you have been meaning to call. Join something.

A choir, a team, a congregation, a volunteer crew, a book club, a union, a garden. It does not much matter which. What matters is that it meets in person, regularly, and that someone would notice if you stopped coming.

If you lead an organization, ask how you could make it a place where people belong rather than a place where people merely transact. If you run a business, ask whether your product brings people together or leaves them more alone. If you hold public office, ask whether your town has places where people can gather without paying for the privilege: the library, the park, the community center, the public square.

[PAUSE]

Earlier tonight, when I read you those nine questions, I said I would come back to some of you. This is that moment. To those of you in this room who are struggling right now, and statistically there are many of you, I want to speak directly.

You are not weak. You are not broken. You are not alone, even though everything in you may be telling you that you are.

You are living through something that tens of millions of your fellow citizens are living through, in a country that has built a way of life that makes it harder than it should be to be well. That is not your fault.

But you do not have to wait for the country to fix itself before you reach for help. Talk to someone. A friend, a doctor, a counselor.

If you are in crisis, call or text nine eight eight, the national suicide and crisis lifeline, any hour of any day. Reaching out is not a sign that you have given up. It is the bravest thing a person can do.

[LET IT LAND]

Sixth, we must give people back the places and the time to belong.

Connection does not happen in the abstract. It happens somewhere. In a park, a library, a church hall, a ball field, a diner, a front porch.

Over the decades, many American towns have let those places close, or put them behind a ticket price, or built neighborhoods where you cannot walk to anything at all. I believe we should treat these shared places as essential infrastructure, as important to the health of a community as its water pipes. When a town is deciding whether to keep its library open late, or to build a park within walking distance of every child, it is making a mental health decision, whether it knows it or not.

And connection takes time. Friendship is built in hours, not in good intentions. A parent working two jobs, a worker on a schedule that changes every week, a commuter who spends two hours a day in traffic, cannot easily join a choir or coach a team or linger over dinner.

I am not going to pretend I have a single answer to that. But I believe that employers, unions and lawmakers of every persuasion should start asking a question they rarely ask: does this policy give people more time with the people they love, or less? Predictable schedules, reasonable commutes, the ability to take a day off to care for a sick parent, these are not luxuries.

In a lonely country, they are medicine.

[SLOW]

And to those of you who manage other people, I would add something smaller. Notice who eats lunch alone. Notice who has stopped talking in meetings.

Notice who used to joke and now does not. You do not have to be a therapist to ask a colleague how they are really doing, and to wait for the honest answer. In a country where so many people would go unnoticed for a week, being the person who notices is no small thing.

Seventh, and finally, we must change what we honor.

Roosevelt told his listeners that the standards of success had to change, that wealth alone could not be the measure, that the joy of achievement and the thrill of creative work and the honesty of public service had to count for more. That was the deepest part of his speech, and it is the deepest part of ours.

We will not fix any of this as long as we honor only the rich, the famous, and the viral. We need to honor again the people who hold communities together. The teacher who stays late.

The coach who drives the kid home. The neighbor who checks on the widow next door. The nurse on the night shift.

The volunteer who answers the crisis line at three in the morning. Those are the people who build trust, connection, and the knowledge that someone has your back.

Those are the true builders of the house.

## XV. The House We Could Build

[SLOW]

We began tonight with Lincoln's first instruction. Before we decide what to do, we must know where we are and where we are heading.

So let me say, one last time, where we are.

We are the richest large society in the history of the world. Our economy produces roughly two and a half times as much per person as it did when the oldest question was first asked. We live longer than our grandparents dreamed. We carry libraries in our pockets.

And one in five of our adults says they are depressed right now.

Four in ten of our high school students have lived through weeks of hopelessness in a single year.

Our young adults rank sixty second in the world in how they rate their lives.

A quarter of us eat every meal alone.

And the suicide rate stands about thirty percent above where it stood at the start of this century.

That is where we are.

[LET IT LAND]

And where are we heading? That part is not yet written. That part is up to us.

I have told you the honest truth tonight, including the parts that complicate my case. I have told you that diagnoses rose partly because stigma fell. I have told you that a survey changed its methods in the middle of a pandemic. I have told you that overdose deaths are falling, that teenage hopelessness eased a little, that the latest Gallup reading came in just below the record, that we climbed one place in the world.

I told you those things because a nation in trouble does not need to be frightened. It needs to be told the truth, all of it, and then trusted to act on it. That is what Roosevelt did on the Capitol steps.

He did not pretend the banks were sound. He did not pretend the farms were prospering. He told his people the situation was grave, and then he told them it was not hopeless, and then he told them what to do.

[BUILD]

So let me tell you what I believe we can do.

We can be the generation that looked at these numbers and refused to shrug.

We can be the generation that decided the health of a nation is not measured only by the size of its economy, but by the number of its people who wake up glad to be alive.

We can be the generation that gave childhood back to children. That let them play outside, look each other in the eye, and grow up without a scoreboard in their pocket measuring their worth every hour of the day.

We can be the generation that made help reachable. That made sure no one in the richest country on earth had to choose between therapy and rent, and that a voice would answer at any hour for anyone standing at the edge.

We can be the generation that tore down the wall. That stopped accepting a country where the poorest are depressed at three times the rate of the comfortable, as though that were simply the natural order of things.

And we can be the generation that set the table again.

I keep coming back to that table. It seems so small, after all these numbers. A meal shared with another human being.

And yet the research says it matters about as much as income in how people judge their lives. The oldest technology for human happiness is not a pill or an app or a policy. It is people sitting down together, passing the bread, and listening.

Picture the reports of ten years from now.

Picture a World Happiness Report that finds the share of Americans eating every meal alone cut in half.

Picture a Gallup release that finds depression among young adults back toward where it was before twenty seventeen.

Picture a Youth Risk Behavior Survey that shows hopelessness falling, year after year after year.

And picture an interviewer in an American living room, asking the oldest question, and hearing more young people say very happy than not too happy.

None of that is impossible. Every one of those numbers was better within living memory. Every one of them moved the wrong way because of choices that human beings made, and every one of them can move the right way because of choices that human beings make.

Lincoln, in Springfield, ended by telling his audience that the result was not doubtful. Wise counsels might accelerate it, he said, or mistakes delay it, but sooner or later the victory was sure to come. He said that to a young party that had lost the last presidential election, in a country sliding toward war. He said it because he believed that a people who understood where they stood could not be stopped from moving forward.

I believe the same of us.

I believe this nation is not condemned to be the richest and saddest society that ever lived. I believe that a people who built the town meeting and the barn raising and the volunteer fire company and the public library know how to build belonging. We have simply forgotten, for a while, that it needs building.

That it does not happen by itself. That a society can grow richer every year and poorer in everything that matters, unless its people decide otherwise.

So decide otherwise.

Decide it at your own table tonight. Decide it in the phone call you make tomorrow to the friend you have been neglecting. Decide it in the meeting you show up to, the team you join, the neighbor you invite in.

Decide it in how you vote, and in what you ask of the people who represent you. Decide it in how you raise your children and how you treat the lonely person at the end of the street.

Decide that the measure of this country will be not only what it produces but how its people feel when they wake up in the morning.

Decide that the only thing we have to fear is the shrug, and refuse to shrug.

I promised at the beginning that I would ask you the oldest question one more time. Here it is.

Taken all together, how would you say things are these days?

[PAUSE]

Do not answer me. Carry it out of this room. Ask it of the person beside you on the way out, of the neighbor at the mailbox, of the friend you have not called in a year. Ask it across a table.

And this time, wait for the answer.

Because a country that is short of each other will not be healed by a program alone, or a pill alone. It will be healed one question at a time, asked by someone who stays to hear the reply.

A house divided against itself cannot stand. But a house can be rebuilt. Timber by timber, table by table, friend by friend, we can build a house in which the richest people in history are also, at last, among the happiest. Not because we got even richer, but because we finally remembered what the riches were for.

We know where we are. We know where we are heading if we do nothing.

[SLOW]

Now let us choose, together, to head somewhere else.

[LET IT LAND]

Thank you.
