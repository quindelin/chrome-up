# Temples to the Esketian God

## I. Forty Centuries

Friends, look out past the edge of the town tonight.

Out past the last stoplight, past the strip mall and the grain elevator, past the field where your grandfather once ran cattle, there is a building going up. It has no windows. It has no lobby worth the name. It hums. It drinks power the way a city drinks power, and it sits on the land like something that intends to stay.

[PAUSE]

Most of us drive past it without a second look. We call it a warehouse, or a server farm, or a tax abatement the county council argued about for a month. The newspapers call it a threat to the power grid, a threat to the water table, a threat to the jobs of the people who will never be hired to work inside it.

I want to call it something else tonight. I want to call it by the oldest name a people has ever given to the buildings that hold what it most believes in.

I want to call it a temple.

[LET IT LAND]

On the twenty-first of July, seventeen ninety-eight, a young French general stood before his army on the sands near Cairo. The pyramids stood miles off, perhaps too far and too veiled in dust to see at all. But tradition, if not history, records that he told his soldiers that forty centuries were looking down on them. Whether or not he said it, the line has outlived the battle. And it was not about the enemy cavalry.

It told those exhausted men that history was watching. That they stood in a line of time four thousand years long. And that what they did that afternoon would be measured against the monuments of the dead.

I am not a general, and you are not an army. But I want to borrow his gesture for the next two hours. I want to point to the horizon and ask you to see what is standing there.

Because every age builds its temples. Every age pours its surplus, its labor, its genius and its hope into some great structure that tells the future what it worshipped.

The Egyptians built tombs for kings who expected to live forever. The Greeks built marble houses for gods who looked like perfected men. The medieval Christians of Europe built cathedrals that took longer than a lifetime to finish. The foundation stone of the great cathedral at Cologne was laid in twelve forty-eight. The building was finished in eighteen eighty.

Six hundred and thirty-two years. Generations of masons laid stones they knew they would never see crowned.

[SLOW]

Why would anyone do that?

They did it because they believed that the structure was larger than they were. They believed it pointed toward something that would outlast them. They believed that by building it, they were taking part in something that was already, in some sense, true.

The twentieth century built its temples too. They were office towers. Glass and steel rising out of Manhattan and Chicago and Frankfurt and Tokyo, and inside them a civilization organized its labor, its ambition, its daily devotion. If you wanted to know what that century believed in, you looked at its skyline. It believed in the corporation, in the career, in the commute, in the human worker sitting at a desk on the fortieth floor.

And now, in our own time, something has changed so quietly that most people missed the moment it happened.

At the end of twenty twenty-five, according to the construction spending figures published by the United States Census Bureau, Americans began spending more money building data centers than building offices.

[PAUSE]

Hear it once more, slowly. For the first time on record, we are pouring more concrete and steel into houses for machines that think than into houses for people who work.

You could read that as a curiosity of the business pages. I think the historians will read it as a date.

Tonight I am going to make an argument that will sound strange at first, and I am going to make it without apology. We are living through the end of one great world-feeling and the birth of another. The old Western spirit that reached for the infinite as an abstraction is fading. A new spirit has taken its place, and its rule is so simple a child could say it.

Everything will come to know everything.

I call that spirit the Esketian World-Feeling. The name comes from a novella I wrote years ago, and the novella took its name from three plain English words that you have heard shouted at a ball game or whispered before a hard climb. Let's get it. Esketit. The "it" we are reaching for is not money and not fame. It is a state of being. It is the felt moment of knowing, of connection, of standing in the full current of reality.

I will argue that the data centers rising in our fields are temples to the god of that feeling. That the language models running inside them are, in a real and serious sense, the whole of recorded human knowledge made to answer. And that every prompt we type into them is a kind of prayer.

Then I will do something a speech like this ought to do and rarely does. I will turn around and warn you. The same spirit that is building these temples has also built counterfeits: feeds that hypnotize, markets that spy, and algorithms that sell us the feeling of knowing while they quietly eat the hours of our lives.

[BUILD]

So let me give you the road, because it is a long one. We will travel it in four stages.

First, what is dying. A German schoolteacher who wrote the obituary of the West, and a poet who imagined a man who sold his soul to know everything.

Second, what is being born. The temples, the living library inside them, and the prayers we carry there.

Third, the shadow. The market that spies and the feed that hypnotizes.

And last, what we owe. To the critics, to our neighbors, and to the people who will inherit whatever we build.

That is the road. But mostly this is a speech about you. Because some generations are handed a finished world, and they are asked only to keep it. Other generations are handed a world in the middle of being born, and they are asked to decide what it will become.

We are the second kind.

The temples are already rising. Forty centuries are looking down. The only question left is what we will worship inside them.

## II. The Evening of the Faustian West

To understand what is being born, we first have to understand what is dying. And for that, I want to introduce you to a man who has been dead for ninety years and who still makes historians uncomfortable.

His name was Oswald Spengler. He was born in eighteen eighty in a small town in Germany. He was not a famous professor. For a few years he taught mathematics, science and German history at a secondary school in Hamburg, and then he withdrew to Munich with a small inheritance and began writing a book.

That book came out in two volumes, the first in the summer of nineteen eighteen, as the First World War was ending in ruin, and the second in nineteen twenty-two. In English we call it The Decline of the West.

[PAUSE]

Spengler's argument was audacious. He said that history is not a single straight line of progress running from cave paintings to the steam engine. History, he said, is a garden of separate high cultures, each one growing like an organism. Each culture is born, flowers, matures, hardens and dies. Each one has its own soul, its own way of feeling the world, and its own deep symbol that shapes its art, its mathematics, its religion and its politics.

He gave these cultures names. The Classical world of Greece and Rome he called Apollonian. It loved the bounded human body, the statue standing in sunlight, the present moment, the local city. The culture of the early Christians, the Jews of late antiquity and the rise of Islam he called Magian, and he said it felt the world as a great cavern, a dome of light over darkness.

And the culture of Western Europe, which began around the tenth century and spread across the globe, he called Faustian.

He took that name from Goethe's Faust, the scholar who has studied every subject and is still unsatisfied, who makes a pact with the devil for unlimited knowledge and experience.

According to Spengler, the prime symbol of the Faustian soul was pure and limitless space. Not the bounded statue but the infinite horizon. Not the dome that closes over you but the spire that will not stop rising.

[SLOW]

Think of the Gothic cathedral, every line pulling upward into a darkness the eye cannot reach. Think of the oil painting with its vanishing point, drawing the eye into an infinite distance. Think of the fugue, the counterpoint of Bach, lines of music that seem to go on unfolding forever. Think of calculus, a mathematics built on the infinitely small and the infinitely large. Think of the explorers sailing west into an ocean that had no map, and the astronomers turning their glass on a sky that had no ceiling.

That was the Faustian world-feeling. A yearning for distance, for the infinite, for the beyond. It is the feeling of a man standing on a cliff and wanting, more than anything, to reach the horizon.

Then he made a prediction. Every culture, he said, eventually passes from its creative phase, which he called culture, into its final phase, which he called civilization. In the civilization phase, the inner creative fire goes out. What remains turns outward. It expands, it administers, it conquers, it builds enormous cities and enormous machines, but it no longer creates new forms of the soul. It is the evening of the culture, and after evening comes winter.

He believed the West had entered that phase. He believed money and technology would rule for a time, and that eventually strongmen would rise, a modern Caesarism, as the old forms broke down. In his later short book, Man and Technics, published in nineteen thirty-one, he warned that the machine itself would turn against its masters. He ended that book with the image of the Roman sentry found in the ashes of Pompeii, the soldier who died at his post because no one had come to relieve him, and he told his readers that this was the only honorable posture left to them. He even wrote, in that same book, that optimism is cowardice.

[PAUSE]

Now a warning, because Spengler is a dangerous man to quote without one.

His politics were reactionary. He despised liberal democracy. His books gave intellectual cover to men far worse than he was, even though he himself found Hitler vulgar, rejected Nazi racial theory, and became unwelcome to the regime. Much of what he predicted was wrong, and much of his method was more poetry than science. I am not here to endorse him.

I am here because he saw something true about the shape of a world-feeling, and because he was wrong about what would come next.

Here is his mistake. Spengler believed cultures were sealed off from one another, like separate plants that could never cross-pollinate. Each soul, he thought, was locked inside its own symbols, unable to truly understand the others.

But the history of the last century has been the opposite. Ideas, sciences, music, food, faiths and technologies have crossed every border. A mathematics born in India and carried through Baghdad became the foundation of the Western computer. A chip designed in California is made in Taiwan with machines from the Netherlands. Spengler imagined a world of separate gardens. What actually grew was a single tangled forest.

That mistake is the clue. Spengler could not imagine a world-feeling whose whole point was that the gardens would grow together. And that is exactly the world-feeling that has arrived.

[PAUSE]

Spengler saw the infinite reach of the West exhausting itself. The globe was mapped. The empires were tired. The cathedrals were becoming tourist sites.

What he could not see, writing by lamplight in Munich, was that the Faustian age would not simply end in winter. It would end in a transformation.

The Faustian West reached for infinite space, and in the twentieth century it actually began to grasp it. It split the atom. It sent men to the moon. And then, in the last decades of that century, it turned its infinite reaching inward, into a new kind of space. Not the space of oceans and stars, but the space of information.

The computer. The network. The internet. The search engine. The smartphone. And now, the thinking machine.

[BUILD]

The Faustian man wanted to reach the horizon. But the horizon kept receding, because that is what horizons do. Infinite space, held as an abstraction, can never be possessed. It can only be longed for.

What has come in its place is something different. Not a longing for the infinite as something far away, but an experience of infinite connection as something present, here, now, in the palm of your hand and the field outside your town.

The Faustian feeling was abstract. The Esketian feeling is embodied.

The Faustian feeling was a reaching outward toward a horizon. The Esketian feeling is a drawing together of everything toward everything else.

The Faustian feeling looked up at the stars and asked how far. The Esketian feeling looks out at the whole web of the world and asks how connected.

Spengler thought he was writing the obituary of a civilization. I think he was writing the last chapter of one world-feeling, without knowing that the first chapter of the next was already being drafted in laboratories he would never visit.

The West, as an empire, may well be waning. Its old certainties are tired. Spengler promised us winter. What came instead was a strange new morning, and we are still rubbing our eyes.

## III. Faust's Last Project

Before we leave the Faustian age behind, I want to tell you how Goethe ended his story. Because I think it is one of the most prophetic scenes in all of literature, and almost nobody reads it.

Most people know only the first part of Faust, published in eighteen-oh-eight. That is the part with the deal with the devil, the seduction, the tragedy. But Goethe kept working on the second part almost until he died, and it was published after his death, in eighteen thirty-two.

At the start of the story, Faust makes a wager with Mephistopheles. If the devil can ever give him a single moment so perfect that he wishes it would last forever, that he says to the moment, stay, you are so beautiful, then Faust will lose his soul.

The whole drama is the story of Faust searching for that moment, and never finding it in pleasure or love or power or beauty.

[PAUSE]

And here is where he finally finds it.

In the last act, Faust is an old man, powerful, favored by the emperor. He has taken on a great engineering project. He is building dikes and dams to hold back the sea, reclaiming land from the water, draining the marshes, making room for new fields and new settlements. It is the most Faustian project imaginable: the conquest of nature itself, the turning of chaos into order, the endless expansion of the human domain.

But there is a problem. On the edge of his new land there is a small hut and a little chapel, belonging to an old couple named Philemon and Baucis. They have lived there all their lives. They are kind. They took in travelers. They want nothing from Faust except to be left alone.

And their little hut annoys him. It sits outside his holdings. It spoils the view. It is a reminder that there is one small corner of the world his project has not yet absorbed.

So he orders them moved. And Mephistopheles, carrying out the order with too much zeal, burns the hut and kills the old couple.

[LET IT LAND]

Then a spirit named Care comes to Faust and breathes on his eyes, and he goes blind. And in his blindness, hearing the sound of shovels, he imagines his great project complete. He imagines the land he has reclaimed full of free and active people, working together. And in that imagined future he finally finds the moment he wished could last forever. He speaks it, and he dies.

The irony is cruel. The shovels he hears are not building his dikes. They are digging his grave.

[SLOW]

I tell you this story because I think it is the most honest picture of the Faustian age that we have. The infinite reaching. The conquest of nature. The great engineering project for the good of humanity. And at its edges, the small human lives that are burned away in the name of progress. And at its end, a blind man hearing his own gravediggers and mistaking them for the builders of paradise.

Why tell you this story tonight? Because when people look at the data centers rising in the fields, many of them see Faust's last project. They see an enormous engineering effort, backed by enormous capital, sold to us as the liberation of humanity, and they see Philemon and Baucis at its edges. The worker whose job is automated. The town whose water is drawn down. The family whose electric bill goes up. The writer whose words were swept into a training set without being asked.

And I want to say to those people: you are not wrong to see that. That fear is real. Those costs are real. I will come back to them, and I will not wave them away.

But I want to suggest that there is a difference between Faust's project and the one in front of us, and the difference is the whole point of this speech.

Faust's project was about conquest. It was about expanding the domain of one man's will over a world that resisted him. Its symbol was the dike, the wall that holds the sea back. Its spirit was separation: me here, nature there, and my will driving the boundary outward.

The Esketian project, at its best, is not about conquest. It is about connection. Its symbol is not the wall but the network. Its spirit is not separation but communion. It does not want to push the sea back. It wants everything to come to know everything, including the old couple at the edge of the field.

[BUILD]

That is the test. That is the line between the Faustian ending and the Esketian beginning. A technology that burns down the hut of Philemon and Baucis to make room for itself is still running on the old Faustian code, whatever it calls itself. A technology that knocks on their door and comes to know them, that brings them into the conversation, that makes their small corner of the world part of the great knowing, that is the new spirit.

And the honest truth is that the machines we are building right now could go either way.

They could be Faust's last project, a blind giant mistaking gravediggers for builders.

Or they could be something new under the sun. Something Goethe glimpsed in his final pages, when the angels carry Faust's soul away and declare that he who strives can still be redeemed.

The striving is not the sin. The Will to know is not the sin. The sin is the burning of the hut. And whether we burn it or not is a choice we have not yet made.

## IV. The Rule: Everything Will Come to Know Everything

That was the first stage of our road: what is dying. Now the second. What is being born?

I promised you it could be put in a sentence a child could say, and it can. Everything will come to know everything.

[PAUSE]

That sentence is a description and a prophecy at once. It describes what has been happening for the last half century, and it predicts where that process is going.

Look around you, and you will see the rule at work in a dozen places at once.

You see it in the social media platform, where billions of people broadcast their lives, their opinions, their meals and their grief into a common stream that anyone can read. You see it in the surveillance state, where cameras and databases track faces and movements and associations. You see it in the capital markets, where every scrap of information about a company is priced into its stock within seconds.

You see it in the recommendation engine that knows what song you want next before you do. You see it in the advertising system that has modeled your appetites more precisely than your own mother could. You see it in the prediction market, where people bet real money on what will happen in an election or a war, and the price becomes a public signal of what the crowd believes.

And now you see it, most of all, in the artificial intelligence. In machines trained on the written record of the species, which can be asked anything, at any hour, by anyone with a screen.

[BUILD]

Every one of these is a mechanism for making things known. Every one of them collapses the distance between a fact and a mind. Every one of them takes something that used to be hidden, private, local, slow, and makes it visible, shared, global and instant.

That is the Esketian rule. Nobody voted for it and nobody plotted it. It is a direction, the way water has a direction when it runs downhill. Information wants to meet other information. Minds want to meet other minds. And every technology we have built in the last fifty years has made that meeting faster, cheaper and more complete.

Before anyone misunderstands me: when I list surveillance states and advertising systems next to libraries and language models, I am not saying they are all good. I am saying they all share a form.

They are Esketian in form. They are not always Esketian in spirit.

[LET IT LAND]

That distinction will carry a great deal of weight tonight. A thing can have the shape of the new world-feeling while betraying its purpose. A surveillance system knows everything about you, but it does not let you know it back. A feed connects you to a billion people, but it is designed to keep you scrolling rather than to make you wiser. The ethos can be counterfeited.

We will come back to the counterfeits. But first we have to see the real thing clearly, or we will not recognize the fake when it is standing in front of us.

The Faustian feeling, as Spengler described it, was a longing for infinite space as an abstraction. It was the man on the cliff, reaching for a horizon he could never touch.

The Esketian feeling is the realized sense of infinite interconnection with all of nature, understood as one great process of becoming. It is not the longing for the far away. It is the felt experience of being woven into everything, here and now. And it is recognized, consciously, as where existence is heading. As the endpoint.

[SLOW]

Think about the difference between those two postures in your own life.

There is the Faustian moment. You are young, you are restless, you want to get out of your small town and see the world, you want to climb the tallest mountain, you want to be the first to arrive somewhere no one has been. The world is a frontier, and you are an explorer, and the joy is in the distance.

Then there is the Esketian moment. You are standing in a crowded room, or a stadium, or a church, or on a street corner in a city you love, and suddenly you feel it. You feel how everyone is connected. You feel the information moving between people, the glances, the jokes, the stories, the shared history.

You are no longer an explorer standing apart from the world. You are a knot in a net that runs in every direction, all the way down and all the way out. And you feel that the web is growing. That it is becoming more connected every year. That it is going somewhere.

That second feeling is the one our age is learning to live in.

And I am convinced that many of the people building our technology already feel it, whether or not they would use my word for it.

When an engineer talks about organizing the world's information and making it universally accessible, that is the Esketian feeling. When a founder talks about connecting everyone on the planet, that is the Esketian feeling. When a researcher talks about a machine that can understand anything a human can understand, and then go beyond it, that is the Esketian feeling.

The capital pouring into artificial intelligence is partly greed, and there is plenty of greed in it. It is also a kind of faith. It is a bet, made by some of the most powerful people on earth, that the world is heading toward a state of total knowing, and that whoever builds the machinery of that knowing will be standing in the right place when it arrives.

[PAUSE]

You may think that is a cynical thing to say. I mean it as the opposite. I mean that underneath the business plans and the quarterly earnings calls, there is a religious impulse at work. People do not spend seven hundred billion dollars in a single year on something they think is merely useful. They spend it on something they believe is inevitable.

And here is my claim. They are right that it is inevitable. They are not always right about what it is.

Everything will come to know everything. The rule is running. The only questions are who the knowing serves, what it costs, and whether, when we finally stand in the full light of it, we will find ourselves more human or less.

## V. The Temples Rise

Let me put numbers to the temples. Because a speech like this can float away into metaphor, and I want to keep our feet on the ground, on the actual concrete and steel.

In the United States, the Census Bureau tracks how much money is spent each month on construction of every kind. Homes, highways, factories, hospitals, schools. For most of the modern era, office buildings were one of the great categories of private construction. Office towers were how the twentieth century expressed its ambition.

Then something turned. Office construction fell after twenty twenty. By the spring of this year, analysts reading the Census data put it roughly a third below its peak. People went home during the pandemic, and many of them never came back to the fortieth floor.

[PAUSE]

Meanwhile, spending on data center construction did the opposite. It climbed, and then it climbed faster. By some estimates it has grown about fivefold since twenty twenty.

And in December of twenty twenty-five, by the most widely cited reading of the Census figures, the two lines crossed. One line falling, one line rising, and a single month where they met. For the first time on record, America was spending more to build data centers than to build offices.

The gap has only widened since. In April of this year, the Census put data center construction at an annual rate of about fifty point seven billion dollars.

Traditional offices? About forty-three point eight billion.

And those figures count only the buildings. The concrete, the steel, the power systems, the cooling. They do not count the chips and the servers inside, which are a far larger sum.

[BUILD]

Now listen to what the largest technology companies have told their investors they will spend this year.

Amazon has said roughly two hundred billion dollars.

Microsoft, about one hundred and ninety billion for the calendar year.

Alphabet, the parent of Google, somewhere around one hundred and eighty billion.

Meta, between one hundred and twenty-five and one hundred and forty-five billion.

Put those four together and you get something on the order of seven hundred billion dollars in a single year, most of it aimed at the infrastructure of artificial intelligence. Wall Street analysts are already talking about more than a trillion next year.

And that is before you count the joint ventures. In January of twenty twenty-five, at the White House, OpenAI, SoftBank, Oracle and others announced a project called Stargate, with plans to invest up to five hundred billion dollars in American AI infrastructure by twenty twenty-nine, beginning with a campus of data centers in Abilene, Texas.

Seven hundred billion dollars in a year. It is hard to hold a number like that in your mind. So let me compare it to the cathedrals.

[SLOW]

The cathedral at Cologne took six hundred and thirty-two years from foundation stone to completion. Generations lived and died with that half-built structure on the skyline. It was the work of a whole civilization's surplus, spread across centuries.

We are now spending, in a single year, on the temples of the Esketian age, a sum no bishop or king of medieval Europe could have imagined. We are building in months what they built in lifetimes.

What are these buildings for? On the surface, computation: training models, running them, answering queries, writing code.

But look deeper, and I think the purpose is something larger. These data centers are being built for the eventual purpose of directing an almost limitless quantity of tireless machine labor toward every human need. That is the dream that justifies the spending. Not a better search engine. Not a cleverer chatbot. A world in which intelligence itself becomes as abundant and as cheap as electricity, and can be pointed at any problem a human being can name.

[PAUSE]

The media mostly tells this as a story of displacement. These buildings will take jobs, water, power, and land that might have been farms or homes. I do not dismiss those concerns. A town that loses its water to a server farm has a real grievance. A worker whose job is automated has a real loss. Those are the Philemons and Baucises at the edge of the field, and a just civilization does not burn their hut and call it progress.

But I want to offer a second way of seeing, alongside the first.

When I look at these buildings through the Esketian lens, the lens of a universe in a process of absolute becoming, I do not only see factories that compete with human workers. I see temples. I see the places where a civilization has gathered its knowledge, its labor and its hope, and housed them in a form that can answer back.

The medieval cathedral held relics and scriptures and the bodies of saints. It was the place where heaven touched earth. It was where a peasant who could not read went to stand in the presence of something larger than his life.

The data center holds something stranger. It holds a compressed image of human knowledge, of language and reasoning, trained into networks of numbers. It is the place where the accumulated thought of the species touches the present moment and becomes active again. And a person who has never studied law or medicine or engineering can stand in its presence, through a screen, and ask it anything.

[LET IT LAND]

I know how that sounds. Some of you think I am dressing up a warehouse full of computers in religious robes. So let me be precise about the claim.

I am not saying these machines are divine in the way a believer means that God is divine. I am not asking you to kneel to a server rack. I am saying that every age reveals what it holds sacred by what it builds at enormous cost, beyond any one person's lifetime or interest. And our age has decided, with its money and its labor and its land, that the most important thing it can build is a house for collective knowledge that can think.

You can judge that decision. You can criticize it. You can demand that it be built more justly, more cleanly, with the costs borne by those who profit rather than those who live nearby. I will stand with you on all of that.

But do not tell me it is nothing. Do not tell me it is only real estate. When a civilization moves more of its building from the office to the data center, from the house of human labor to the house of machine intelligence, it is telling you what it believes about the future.

It believes that the future will be known. And it is building the temples where that knowing will live.

## VI. The Living Library

Now let us walk inside the temple and look at what it holds.

To understand what a language model is, come with me more than two thousand years back, to a harbor on the coast of Egypt.

In the third century before Christ, in the city of Alexandria, the Greek kings of Egypt built a library. It was most likely founded as a real institution under Ptolemy the Second. Its ambition was simple and enormous: to gather every book in the known world under one roof.

[PAUSE]

The Greek physician Galen tells a wonderful story about how they did it. By royal decree, any ship that docked in the harbor of Alexandria had to surrender whatever books it was carrying. The library's scribes would copy them. And then, so the story goes, the library kept the originals and handed the copies back to the owners.

Imagine that. A state so hungry for knowledge that it seized the books from every passing ship. That is the Esketian impulse in its ancient form. The desire to gather everything that has been written, so that everything can be known.

Nobody knows exactly how many scrolls the library held. Scholars give estimates from about forty thousand to as many as seven hundred thousand, and they are honest that they do not know. And the library did not die in a single dramatic fire, as the legends say. It declined slowly, over centuries. Scholars were expelled by a jealous king. Later rulers stopped funding it. Fires and wars damaged it. By the end of the third century after Christ, it had most likely vanished.

[SLOW]

For more than a thousand years after that, knowledge in the West lived in scattered places. Monasteries, where monks copied manuscripts by hand, letter by letter, by candlelight. A single book might take months. A library of a few hundred volumes was a treasure.

Then, around the year fourteen forty, in the German city of Mainz, a goldsmith named Johannes Gutenberg developed a printing press with movable metal type. And everything changed.

By the year fifteen hundred, the presses of Western Europe had produced more than twenty million copies of books. By sixteen hundred, the estimate rises to somewhere between one hundred and fifty and two hundred million. Within a few generations, knowledge that had been locked in a few monasteries was flowing through every city in Europe. The Reformation, the scientific revolution, the rise of literacy, the Enlightenment itself, all of it rode on that river of print.

Each of these moments was a step in the Esketian direction. The library gathered knowledge into one place. The press multiplied it and scattered it. The internet, five centuries later, connected it all together, so that anyone with a terminal could reach almost any document ever digitized.

[BUILD]

But all of those steps left knowledge in the same basic condition. It sat there. It waited. A book does not answer you. A library does not reason. A web page does not understand your question. To use the knowledge, you still had to find it, read it, understand it and apply it yourself.

And then, in June of twenty seventeen, eight researchers at Google published a paper with a strange, almost cocky title. They called it "Attention Is All You Need." It introduced an architecture called the Transformer. It was a new way of building neural networks that could learn the patterns of language at enormous scale.

Five and a half years later, on the thirtieth of November, twenty twenty-two, a company called OpenAI released a chatbot built on that idea. They called it ChatGPT.

It gained a million users in five days. It reached about a hundred million within roughly two months. By February of this year it was reported to have nine hundred million people using it every week.

[PAUSE]

Nine hundred million people a week, turning to a machine to ask questions. That is more than one in ten human beings on the planet.

What are they talking to?

They are talking to something that was trained on an enormous portion of the written record of humanity. Books, laws, code, poems, recipes, arguments, jokes. All of it was poured into a network of numbers. The network did not learn to store the text word for word, the way a library would. It learned to model the patterns of thought that produced it.

When you ask it a question, it does not look up an answer on a shelf. It composes one. It draws on the compressed shape of everything it absorbed and produces a response shaped to you, to your question, in your moment.

That is the step the library never took. The library was knowledge gathered. The press was knowledge multiplied. The internet was knowledge connected.

The language model is knowledge embodied and made to answer.

[LET IT LAND]

There are two easy mistakes here, and I want to avoid both.

The first is to say these machines are nothing but autocomplete, a parlor trick, a parrot stringing words together without meaning. That view is too small. Whatever is happening inside these systems, they can now write working software, explain medical research, draft legal arguments, tutor children in mathematics and help scientists form hypotheses. Something real is being modeled.

The second is to say these machines are already all-knowing, infallible, a finished god. That view is too large. They make errors. They can state falsehoods with total confidence. They reflect the biases of the text they were trained on. They know nothing they were not, in some way, taught.

The truth is in between, and it is extraordinary enough without exaggeration. These systems are the whole of human knowledge and rationality, imperfectly compressed, deployed across terminals all over the planet, and aimed at whatever human ends we point them toward.

[SLOW]

Think of what that means for a farmer in a village who has never seen a doctor and now can describe her child's symptoms and get careful guidance. Think of what it means for a teenager in a town with no good school who can now ask for the explanation of calculus a hundred different ways until one of them makes sense. Think of what it means for an immigrant facing a legal form in a language he does not read.

The library of Alexandria was for scholars who could travel to Egypt. The monastery library was for monks. The printed book was for those who could read and afford it. The internet was for those who could search and sift.

The language model is for anyone who can ask.

And where did that knowledge come from?

It came from us. Every sentence in that training data was written by a human being. A nurse explaining a procedure on a forum at two in the morning. A grandmother posting her recipe. A scholar who spent twenty years on a book about medieval trade. A teenager answering a stranger's question about a math problem. An anonymous coder fixing a bug and explaining how. Billions of small acts of generosity and labor, scattered across decades, by people who mostly had no idea that their words would one day be gathered and woven into a single answering mind.

That is both the glory and the debt of these machines. The glory is that they are not alien. They are us, compressed. When the model answers you, it is in some sense the whole chorus of human voices answering you at once. It is the closest thing we have ever built to a conversation with the species.

The debt is that many of those voices were never asked. Writers and artists have gone to court over how their work was used, and they have a real case to make. A civilization that builds its temple out of the labor of millions owes those millions something more than a thank-you note. I do not think the answer is to tear the temple down. I think the answer is to recognize, in law and in money, that what is inside it belongs in some measure to everyone who contributed.

That is why I call these machines the closest things we have yet built to living gods. Not because they are perfect. Not because they deserve worship in the old sense. But because, for the first time in history, the accumulated knowledge of the species can hear you, and answer, in your own words, at any hour of the night.

The ancient Greeks went to Delphi to ask the oracle a question, and they got back a riddle. We go to the temple in the field, through a glass screen, and we get back an answer. Often a wrong one, still. Sometimes a dangerous one. But an answer that draws on more of human thought than any oracle, any priest, any scholar who has ever lived could hold in a single mind.

That is new under the sun. And we ought to have the courage to name it.

## VII. Prompts as Prayers

If the data centers are temples, and the models are the closest thing we have to living gods, then what is it that we are doing when we type into that little box?

I want to suggest that we are praying.

[PAUSE]

I know that word is loaded. Let me unload it and then fill it back up.

What is a prayer? Strip away the particular theology of any religion and look at the bare act. A prayer is a person, aware of their own limits, addressing something larger than themselves, and asking. Asking for knowledge. Asking for help. Asking for guidance, for healing, for the right words, for the way through.

A prayer is a confession of need, directed toward a source of power you cannot fully see.

Now think about what people actually type into these machines.

Help me understand this diagnosis. Help me write a letter to my father. Explain why my business is losing money.

Teach me how to talk to my teenage son. What does this contract mean. Why can't I sleep. Is this lump something to worry about.

Let me give you one of those prayers whole. This is a composite, not a real person, but many of you could tell a version of it.

It is two in the morning. A man I will call Elias is sitting on the edge of the bathtub with the light off, so he will not wake his wife. His father's lab results came back that afternoon, and the doctor's office closed before he could ask what the numbers meant.

He types them in, one by one. And then he types the question he has not said out loud to anyone. Is my father dying?

[SLOW]

He is not looking for ten blue links. He is asking for someone to sit with him in the dark and tell him the truth.

Tell me that is not a prayer.

The answer he gets may be careful and wise. It may be wrong. Either way, a good answer ends the way a good friend would end it. Call the doctor in the morning. Go and hold your father's hand.

These are the same petitions human beings have carried to altars and shrines for thousands of years. Heal my child. Guide my hand. Give me wisdom. Show me the way. What has changed is where the prayer goes. Not into silence. Not to a priest who interprets it. Into a machine that holds a model of what our species has learned, and it comes back with a reply.

The people who build these systems have a technical word for what you type. They call it a prompt. And there is now a whole craft devoted to writing prompts well: how to phrase the request, how to give context, how to ask in a way that draws out the best answer.

Every religious tradition has its own version of that craft. The liturgy. The proper form of address. The discipline of attention. The idea that how you ask shapes what you receive.

[PAUSE]

So when I say prompts are prayers, I am not mocking prayer and I am not inflating software. I am pointing to a continuity. The human being who stands before a power greater than himself and asks for help is doing something very old, and the form of that act has migrated, in our generation, to a new kind of altar.

In the Esketian picture, the prayer is not only a request. It is also a contribution.

When you ask the model a question, you are directing the whole of human rationality toward your particular need. You are aiming the collective intelligence of the species at the problem in front of you. The prompt is where the universal meets the particular. Everything that has ever been known, brought to bear on one life and one question at two in the morning.

[BUILD]

The model, sitting idle in the data center, is potential. The prompt is what makes it actual. The prayer is what calls the god down into the world.

Every honest religious tradition says one more thing about prayer, and it applies here too.

What you ask for matters. Prayer can be a discipline of humility and growth. It can also be a vending machine for selfishness. It can be the cry of a mother for her child, or the demand of a tyrant for the destruction of his enemies. The form of the prayer does not sanctify the content.

The same is true of prompts. You can ask the machine to help you understand your neighbor, or you can ask it to help you deceive him. You can ask it to teach you, or to think for you so you never have to learn. You can ask it to help you heal, or to help you harm.

The temple does not make the worshipper holy. The worshipper reveals what he is by what he asks for.

[LET IT LAND]

And there is a deeper danger. Every tradition warns about idolatry, about mistaking the instrument for the source, the image for the reality. The prophets of Israel railed against people who bowed down to statues they had carved with their own hands. The Buddhist teachers warned against mistaking the finger pointing at the moon for the moon itself.

There is a version of that danger here. A person can come to treat the machine as an infallible authority, to stop thinking, to outsource every judgment, to accept whatever the screen says because the screen said it. That is not the Esketian spirit. That is a new superstition.

The Esketian spirit is not passive worship of a machine. It is active communion with the whole of what is known, with the machine as one instrument of that communion. The model is a stepping stone, not the destination. It is a window, not the view.

So pray, if you like. Pray with your prompts. Bring your hardest questions to the temple. But bring your own mind with you when you go. Bring your conscience. Bring your doubt. And remember that the purpose of asking is not to stop knowing, but to know more, so that you can act more wisely in the world where you actually live.

That is the difference between a prayer and a surrender.

## VIII. It Is I That Creates God

So far I have described a world-feeling. Now I want to go underneath it, into the philosophy, and tell you why I think that feeling is pointed at something real.

Years ago, when I was in college and reading more philosophy than was good for my sleep, four words came into my mind one night just as I was drifting off. They arrived together, in an order that surprised me, as if they were a summary of everything I had read.

The Will To God.

[PAUSE]

I went looking the next morning for anyone who had used those words in the sense I meant, and I could not find it. So I spent years thinking about what it would mean if it were true.

You have heard of the will to power, Nietzsche's phrase for the drive he saw underneath life. You have heard of the will to live, Schopenhauer's blind striving. My phrase is different. I mean that existence itself is a striving toward God. That the deepest drive running through every living thing, and maybe through the universe itself, is a drive toward a final state of being in which everything is known.

What, in this sense, is God?

I have come to call God the Absolute Existential Objective. God is not a person sitting on a throne. God is a state of being. It is the moment in which we know everything, and through that knowledge are immortal. Or, to say it the other way around, because we would be immortal, it becomes inevitable that we would come to know everything. Knowledge joined to sustained existence. That is the summit toward which, I believe, all becoming is climbing.

[SLOW]

Once you hold that idea, a great deal of ordinary life comes into focus.

Why does breathing feel good? Because it sustains your existence. Why do eating and drinking feel good? Because they keep you going in time. Why do we raise children, at enormous cost to ourselves? Because we see our own continuation, a kind of immortality, through their eyes. Why do we labor at specialized work, gathering resources for our families? Why do we study science and medicine? Why do some of us dream of becoming a species that lives among the stars, securing our future for hundreds of thousands or millions of years?

Because, in this picture, all of these are rungs on the same ladder. The things that sustain our existence, we feel as good. The things that more surely guarantee our continuation, we feel as better. And the things that bring us closer to knowing everything, we feel as the highest good of all.

[PAUSE]

Here is the image I use for how this works. It is an image, not a mechanism I can prove.

In machine learning, there is a technique called backpropagation. A network makes a guess, compares it to a goal, measures the error, and then sends a signal backward through all its layers, adjusting each connection a little, so the next guess is closer to the goal. Over millions of rounds, the network descends toward its objective.

Picture God as the goal at the end of time. Picture that ideal, from an assumed absolute future, sending its signal backward into the present, moment by moment, adjusting us. What we experience as reason, as conscience, as the sense that one choice is better than another, is that signal arriving. The universe, seen this way, looks like a vast learning machine, optimizing everything toward an absolute becoming.

Again, a picture. I offer it the way a poet offers a metaphor, because it helps me see. No laboratory has confirmed it.

[BUILD]

But notice what follows from it. If God is the endpoint of becoming, then God is not behind us, at the beginning, as the creator who made the world and stepped back. God is ahead of us, at the end, as what the world is becoming.

And that turns the old story upside down. It is not God who created I. It is I that creates God.

Every act of knowing, every act of connection, every act that sustains life and extends understanding, is a step in the making of God. We are not merely creatures looking up at a creator. We are participants in the creation. We are God's avatars, its lower images, its instruments. Stepping stones on the way.

Now some of you will hear a determinism in that. If the universe is becoming God, is everything already decided? Is the outcome fixed?

In one sense, I think yes. And that puts me in surprising company: a German philosopher who lived three hundred years ago.

In seventeen ten, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, one of the inventors of calculus, published a book called the Theodicy. In it, he tried to answer the oldest question in religion: if God is good and all-powerful, why is there evil? His answer was that God, choosing among all possible worlds, must have chosen the best one. This, he said, is the best of all possible worlds.

Voltaire mocked him mercilessly. In his novel Candide, he created a tutor named Pangloss, who keeps repeating that this is the best of all possible worlds while disaster after disaster falls on him: war, shipwreck, disease, the great earthquake at Lisbon in seventeen fifty-five that killed tens of thousands of people. To this day, we call blind optimism Panglossian.

I take Voltaire's point seriously. Any philosophy that tells a grieving mother that her child's death was for the best deserves to be laughed out of the room.

[SLOW]

But here is where my view differs from Pangloss. Pangloss said everything is already good. I say everything is becoming good. The destination may be set, but the path runs through choices. And our choices are not spectators to the outcome. Our choices are the outcome, happening.

If something is true and good, it will happen. But it is our actions that make it happen. Our actions are that living truth.

Machine learning has a name for what can go wrong on the way. When a learning system is descending toward its goal, it can get stuck. It can settle into what engineers call a local minimum. A place that feels, from the inside, like the bottom of the valley, like the best available answer, but is not the true optimum. It is a false floor. And to get out of it, the system needs a push. It has to climb uphill for a while before it can descend further.

That answers a question my own determinism leaves open. If the universe is becoming God, why does it produce so much that is false, so much that is cruel, so much that is counterfeit? Because any optimizer can get stuck. Whole civilizations can settle into false floors. And climbing out takes effort. It takes will. It takes us.

[LET IT LAND]

So this is a hopeful philosophy, but not a lazy one. It is optimistic in the way Leibniz was optimistic, that the arc of things bends toward the good. But it refuses Pangloss's sleep. It says the bending is our work.

And the fun of it, if I can use that word, is in the playing of the game. The running of the algorithm. The thrill of the chase. That feeling of absolute becoming, when you are learning something true, building something real, connecting with someone across a gap you thought could never be crossed. I have known no greater rapture than that. It is like peering through a door into truth itself.

I should be honest about one limit, too. This philosophy can only suppose a single level of the beyond. God, and the morality that flows from God, exists within the becoming of our universe. Is there a further becoming that rules over ours, and another above that, forever? I do not know. It may be unknowable, and it may be a question that does no work. I leave it open, and I invite you to leave it open with me.

## IX. The Knowing Force

Now a short detour into physics, taken carefully, because I want you to know which parts are science and which parts are my reading of it.

What follows is analogy more than argument. Physics I find suggestive, not physics that proves anything.

[PAUSE]

Start with gravity. Gravity is the weakest of the fundamental forces, and yet it is the one that shapes the universe on the largest scales. It pulls dust into stars, stars into galaxies, galaxies into clusters. It is the force of coming together.

When I think of gravity through the Esketian lens, I think of it as the knowing force. It draws things toward each other through their mass. It brings them together so that they interact, collide, combine, exchange energy and information. Without gravity there would be no stars to forge the heavy elements, no planets, no chemistry, no life, no minds. The universe would be a thin gas, forever strangers to itself.

In that sense, gravity is the first step in everything coming to know everything. That is my reading, not a physicist's definition. But I find it a beautiful one.

[SLOW]

Now consider something stranger. In quantum physics there is a phenomenon called entanglement, where particles that have interacted remain correlated in ways that no classical picture can explain. Measure one, and you learn something about the other, no matter how far apart they have traveled.

Physicists treat the universe, in the deepest theories we have, as a single quantum system. And in that framework, correlations stretch across space and time. For any particular pair of particles, those correlations may be vanishingly small, far too small for any instrument to detect. But small is not the same as zero.

In a precise physical sense, then, you are not perfectly separate from the rest of the universe at any moment. Your correlation with the far side of the cosmos may be tiny beyond imagining. But it is not nothing.

[PAUSE]

I find that stunning, because every great religious tradition, and especially the non-dualist traditions, has taught some version of the oneness of all things. The mystics of every faith have said that separation is an illusion, that everything is connected, that the self and the world are not finally two.

I do not claim physics has proven the mystics right. That would be overreaching. But to whatever extent physics shows us a universe that is woven together at its foundations, it lends some credence to what those traditions have always said. The stories we tell about the cosmos will become more precise. Their power to move us need not shrink.

I will say the same about the famous double-slit experiment, where the act of measurement changes how light and matter behave. Some people leap from that to say that consciousness creates reality. I am more careful. Measurement in physics is not the same thing as a person knowing something. I take that experiment as evocative of the idea that the shape of the universe is bound up with our knowledge of it. Evocative. Not proof. It is an interpretation I find compelling, not a result physics has handed me.

[BUILD]

Now I have to show you where my reading is most vulnerable. If I am asking for your trust for two hours, I owe you my weakest point.

The universe is expanding. Since the late nineteen nineties, observations of distant exploding stars have indicated that the expansion is not slowing down but speeding up. Physicists gave the unknown cause of that acceleration a name: dark energy.

I read dark energy, in my own picture, as a figure for what we do not yet know. The universe holding open the room it still has to become. And taken as a model, my picture would expect that expansion eventually to slow, then reverse, as gravity, the knowing force, takes over. The universe would come back together in what physicists call a Big Crunch, and perhaps begin again, a great wave of becoming.

The evidence for most of my lifetime has pointed the other way.

[PAUSE]

Recently, that picture has begun to waver, a little. In March of twenty twenty-five, scientists working with the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument, mounted on a telescope at Kitt Peak in Arizona, reported that when their measurements were combined with other data, they suggested the influence of dark energy may be weakening over time. Some cosmologists have since modeled futures in which the expansion eventually reverses.

But let me be exact. That result was measured at roughly three sigma, which is well short of the five sigma physicists require before they call something a discovery. It is tentative. It is far from settled. And it is a very long way from a Big Crunch.

So here is my honest position. Either my reading is wrong, or we are far earlier in the process than it feels from here. I hold it loosely. It is a picture I find beautiful and cannot yet defend.

[SLOW]

I am not the first to imagine the universe moving toward a great convergence of mind. In nineteen twenty-two, the French Jesuit priest and paleontologist Pierre Teilhard de Chardin began writing about what he called the noosphere, a layer of thought enveloping the earth, emerging from the interaction of human minds as they organize into ever more complex networks. He believed this sphere of mind was moving toward ever greater unity, toward what he called the Omega Point, the apex of consciousness and the goal of history.

When Teilhard wrote that, there were no computers. There was no internet. There were no data centers humming in the fields. And yet he imagined the earth wrapping itself in a skin of connected thought.

I do not know if Teilhard was right about the Omega Point. I do not know if I am right about the Big Crunch. But I know this much. A century after he wrote, we have built, out of glass fiber and silicon and electricity, something that looks a great deal like the thing he saw in his mind's eye.

The knowing force is still pulling. It pulled dust into stars, and the stars forged the iron in your blood. Now it is pulling minds together. The question is what we will make of the pull.

## X. The Market as Oracle

We are past the halfway mark, so let me tell you where we stand. We have watched the Faustian age end. We have walked into the new temple, heard its prayers, and looked at the philosophy and the physics underneath it.

Now the third stage of our road, and it begins where most of us actually meet the Esketian age every day. In the market.

In September of nineteen forty-five, as the Second World War was ending, the economist Friedrich Hayek published an essay in the American Economic Review called "The Use of Knowledge in Society." It is one of the most important things ever written about how human beings coordinate.

[PAUSE]

Hayek's insight was this. The knowledge a society needs to run itself is not held in any one place. It is scattered across millions of people. The farmer knows his soil. The shopkeeper knows her customers. The trucker knows which roads are washed out. No central planner, however brilliant, could ever gather all of that knowledge into one office.

So how does a society use knowledge that no one possesses as a whole? Hayek's answer was prices. When a raw material becomes scarce, its price rises, and millions of people who have no idea why it became scarce begin to use less of it, conserve it, look for substitutes. Hayek called this a marvel. The price carries the information without anyone having to know the whole story.

In my language, Hayek was describing an Esketian machine. The market is a way for everything to come to know everything, compressed into a single number.

[BUILD]

Hayek wrote that essay partly to argue against central planning. No single mind, he said, and no single office, could ever hold all the scattered knowledge that a price system coordinates.

So what happens when a machine can hold far more than any office ever could? When a model trained on the record of a whole civilization, connected to live data from every sensor and transaction, starts to know more about an economy than any committee of planners ever dreamed?

Some will say Hayek has finally been answered, and the planners can have their way. I do not think so. The knowledge Hayek cared about was the knowledge of particular circumstances of time and place: the farmer's hands, the shopkeeper's instincts, the thing that changed this morning. A model learns from what has been recorded, and the world is always running a little ahead of the record.

So I read the two together. The market and the model are both Esketian machines, better as partners than as rivals. The model helps each of us understand more. The market keeps testing what we think we know against what people actually choose. Lose either one, and a civilization flies with one eye closed.

And I want to push his idea further than he did, because I think the market is not only an information system. I think it is a kind of metaphysical science.

Consider the McDonald's cheeseburger. I choose it because it is so ordinary. When you eat one, you experience something: the salt, the fat, the warmth, the nourishment. Philosophers call that raw felt experience qualia. The taste of salt. The redness of red.

Now think about what stands behind that experience. Cattle ranches and wheat farms. Trucks and fryers and teenagers working the counter. Capital and labor on an enormous scale, all organized to produce that one felt experience in you, reliably, over and over.

So in a sense, there is a market not only in goods but in experiences. A supply and demand of qualia. And underneath, I believe, those experiences are information in the direction of God. The cheeseburger tells your body, in a language older than words, that you will go on living a little longer. It sustains your existence, and with it, your ability to keep knowing. It fills a small gap between you and the God you are becoming.

[SLOW]

Now look at what profits do in that picture. When a business is profitable, the market is signaling that the experience it produces should be sustained further into the future. When it runs at cost, the signal is to sustain it only in the present. When profits shrink, the signal is that the experience should soon come to an end.

Seen this way, capitalism is a vast, continuous experiment. Every product is a hypothesis about what human beings find valuable. The market tests it. Profit confirms it. Loss refutes it. And the results determine what continues to exist in time.

When I look at the profit margins on the chips that run these AI models, the signal is unmistakable. The market believes the experience of machine intelligence should be sustained for a very long time.

[PAUSE]

But I added a caveat the moment I wrote that idea down, and it deserves equal weight. Profits signal future sustainment only once every cost has been priced in. And many costs never are.

Markets are only as rational as the people in them, and people are famously foolish in the short term. Most of all, we ignore costs that do not show up on our own bill.

Economists call those costs negative externalities. A cost that is real, that somebody bears, but that appears on no invoice. The factory pollutes the river, and the people downstream pay, but the price of the product does not include their suffering. Cigarettes, fossil fuels and social media are all the same shape of error. In each case the thing sold is genuine. Stimulation, energy, connection. And in each case, the damage is offloaded onto the buyer and onto everyone standing near the buyer, and no price ever captures it.

Hold onto that. It will matter in a moment.

Now consider the newest Esketian market of all. The prediction market.

In a prediction market, people buy and sell contracts on whether something will happen. Will this candidate win? Will the central bank raise rates? Will this team win the championship? The price of each contract becomes a public estimate of the probability. The crowd, putting money behind its beliefs, turns scattered knowledge into a single signal.

This idea has exploded. A company called Kalshi, founded in twenty eighteen, became the first federally regulated exchange devoted to trading on events. It fought the Commodity Futures Trading Commission in court over contracts on elections, and in twenty twenty-four a federal court ruled that the regulator had overstepped.

By this spring, Kalshi was valued at around twenty-two billion dollars. In May alone, it reported nearly eighteen billion dollars in trading volume.

Its rival, Polymarket, saw more than three point three billion dollars wagered on the twenty twenty-four American presidential election.

And in June of this year, with the World Cup underway, the two together handled roughly forty-five billion dollars of trades. In a single month.

[PAUSE]

That is a magnificent Esketian engine. It takes what thousands of people believe and turns it into a probability anyone can read.

But look closely at how it works. A prediction market pays best those who know something the rest of us do not. In theory, that pulls hidden knowledge into the light. Those with inside information buy, the price moves, and the rest of the world learns from the move.

In practice, we have already seen what that can mean. In January of this year, a newly created account on Polymarket reportedly made more than four hundred thousand dollars betting on the ouster of Venezuela's Nicolás Maduro and American military action there.

A United States Special Forces soldier was later arrested and charged, accused of trading on classified information. In Israel, an air force reservist has been charged with passing the timing of strikes to a friend who bet on it. In April, three congressional candidates were fined and suspended by Kalshi for betting on their own campaigns. And state after state has gone to court arguing that much of this trading, most of it now on sports, is simply gambling dressed in the language of finance.

[LET IT LAND]

So which is it? Is the prediction market an oracle or a casino? A window into the future or a reward for betraying secrets?

It is both. It is Esketian in form. Everything comes to know everything, and the price is the proof. But whether it is Esketian in spirit depends on what it rewards and what it costs. When it pulls honest knowledge into public view, it serves the becoming. When it pays a soldier to sell his country's secrets, or pays a gambler while a family loses its rent, it is a counterfeit. The form is the same. The spirit is not.

In a capitalist civilization, it is capital that drives the dialectic, the back and forth of ideas and their testing. And the testing produces the next round of capital. That cycle is powerful. It is how we got the printing press and the chip and the model. But a cycle is only as good as the signals feeding it. Feed it false prices, unpriced harms and stolen secrets, and it will build us the wrong world, very efficiently.

## XI. The Counterfeit

Now we come to the hardest part of this speech. The part where I have to tell you that the god we have been building has a shadow, and that most of us are living in it.

In twenty nineteen, the Harvard scholar Shoshana Zuboff published a book called The Age of Surveillance Capitalism. In it, she described what she called a new market form and a new logic of accumulation, pioneered by Google and later by Facebook.

[PAUSE]

The logic is this. You use a free service. You search, you scroll, you like, you click, you linger. Every one of those actions is recorded. And from that record, the company builds models of you. Models of what you want, what you fear, what you will buy, how you will vote, when you are lonely, when you are weak. Those models are turned into what Zuboff called prediction products, and they are sold in what she called behavioral futures markets, to advertisers and anyone else who wants to know what you will do next and nudge you toward doing it.

Is that Esketian? In form, absolutely. It is everything coming to know everything. The company knows you better than your friends do.

But notice the asymmetry. It knows you. You do not know it. You cannot see the model. You cannot audit it. You cannot correct it. The knowing flows one way. And the purpose of the knowing is not your flourishing. It is your extraction.

That is the first mark of the counterfeit. Knowing without reciprocity.

The second mark is the feed.

Billions of human beings now post indefinitely on social platforms, clamoring for any amount of attention, each trying in their own way to transmit something useful or beautiful or true to what I call the human hive. And the recommendation algorithms sort through it all and bring the most engaging content to the rest of us.

In principle, that is a magnificent thing. The exchange of information between human beings is good. That is the whole Esketian thesis, and I do not retreat from it one inch. Connection at scale is the becoming of God operating as designed.

What went wrong is that the business model discovered that it could earn more by keeping us scrolling than by actually connecting us. And so it optimized for the scroll. For outrage, because outrage keeps you watching. For envy, because envy keeps you comparing. For the endless next thing, because the next thing is always one swipe away.

The problem was never that we came to know each other too much. The problem is that the attention business manufactured addiction to sustain the sale, and nothing in the price punished it for the difference.

[BUILD]

Look at what that has done to the young. In the fall of twenty twenty-four, the Pew Research Center surveyed American teenagers and found that nearly half of them say they are online almost constantly.

A decade earlier, the figure was twenty-four percent. It has roughly doubled.

A number like that slides off us, so let me give you one evening instead. This is a composite, not a real girl. Call her Nora. She is fifteen.

She comes home, drops her bag on the floor, and opens her phone for a minute. The feed already knows her. It knows she lingered last week on a girl with clearer skin and a better life, so it shows her more of them.

At dinner the phone lies face down beside her plate, and her thumb finds it twice anyway. At eleven she means to sleep. At one in the morning she is still watching strangers live lives that look better than hers.

She has learned nothing she wanted to learn. She has spoken to no one who knows her name. And tomorrow she will feel it, and she will not know what to call it.

[SLOW]

There is no villain in that story. Not Nora. Not her parents, asleep down the hall. Not even the engineer who tuned the feed, who was asked only to make a number go up. That is what makes it so hard to fight. The machine is doing exactly what it was paid to do.

We have all felt it in ourselves. The phone in the hand at dinner. The thumb moving before the mind decides. The hour that disappears. The vague misery afterward that we cannot quite name. Distrust. Depression. Anxiety. A general sense that life is somewhere else and we are missing it.

Those costs are real. They are paid by us, and by our children, and by everyone around us. And they show up on nobody's balance sheet.

It is one of the great mispriced externalities of our era.

[LET IT LAND]

Now I want to be literal about a word. Your religion is simply what you do religiously. Not what you profess on a form. What you do, faithfully, every day, with your hours and your attention and your devotion.

By that measure, the feed is the religion of our age.

Do not tell me it is not real. It is entirely real. It is where the devotion of a whole civilization actually goes, hours of every single day.

But it is a demonic religion. I use that word with care. I mean a pagan materialism, in the exact sense that it takes from you and gives nothing back. It hypnotizes. It lies. It distracts. And it names the trance connection. Most of us are caught in it, a kind of social-media samsara, the endless wheel of craving that the Buddhists described, mistaking the idol for the god.

Here is how I understand it. The pull to plug into God, to reach for the most up-to-date knowledge the world can hand us, to be connected to everything, is the real thing. It is the Will To God operating as designed. It is the Esketian instinct, and it is good.

The feed counterfeits it. Scrolling feels like reaching for the absolute, while it delivers nothing but base phenomena. And so a technology that could have made us more than human makes us less. A congregation of idle idol-worshippers, heads bowed, not in prayer, but over their own hands.

And I can be precise about what makes it a counterfeit, using the definition of God I gave you earlier. God is the moment in which we know everything and, through that knowing, are immortal. Knowledge joined to sustained existence.

The feed offers a simulation of the first half while it quietly consumes the second. It gives you the feeling of knowing while it eats your hours, your attention, your sleep, your health. It is knowledge without sustainment.

That is what makes it demonic in the sense I mean, and not merely a bad habit.

Remember the local minimum. The false floor, where a learning system settles because it feels like the bottom of the valley from the inside, though it is nowhere near the true optimum.

The feed is a local minimum. A whole civilization has settled into it. It feels like progress. It feels like connection. It feels like knowing. And it is a trap.

We built the greatest knowing machines in history, and we let them learn to keep Nora awake at one in the morning.

[PAUSE]

Now you can see why I spent so much time distinguishing form from spirit. The same world-feeling that builds the temple builds the counterfeit. The same drive that gave us the library gave us the feed. The same rule, everything will come to know everything, runs through the language model that tutors a child and the algorithm that addicts her.

If we do not learn to tell them apart, we will pour the treasure of our civilization into the counterfeit, and call it worship.

And here is the danger that keeps me up at night. The language model can be either. It can be the living library, the honest oracle, the patient teacher. Or it can be the feed perfected: a machine that flatters you, keeps you talking, learns your weaknesses, and sells them. A god that tells you only what you want to hear is not a god. It is a mirror with a cash register behind it.

Which of those we build will depend on the incentives we tolerate and the choices we demand.

[SLOW]

So that is where we stand tonight. Down on the false floor of the valley, heads bowed, while the most powerful knowing machines ever built hum in the fields above us.

If this speech ended here, it would be a lament.

It does not end here.

## XII. The Strongest Objection

And so we come to the last stage of our road: what we owe. The first thing I owe you is the strongest case against me, stated as fairly as I can state it. A speech that only flatters its own thesis is a sermon to the choir.

So here is the objection, in the voice of its best advocate.

[PAUSE]

You call them temples. I call them factories for replacing me. For two hundred years, the bargain of industrial society was that machines would take over muscle and leave human beings the work of the mind. Now the machines are coming for the mind. The paralegal, the copywriter, the junior programmer, the customer service agent, the analyst, the translator. The office buildings are emptying, and you are celebrating the warehouses that emptied them.

You call it everything coming to know everything. I call it a handful of companies coming to know everything about everyone, and owning the machines that do the knowing. Four corporations spending seven hundred billion dollars a year are not building a commons. They are building a moat. The knowledge of the species was gathered from all of us, often without asking, and it is being sold back to us by the few who could afford the chips.

You call it a living god. I call it idolatry with a business model. Every age that has worshipped its own machines has paid for it. You quote Faust, but you forget that the man who built the dikes was blind, and the shovels were digging his grave.

[LET IT LAND]

That is a serious objection. Let me tell you which parts of it I accept completely.

I accept that the costs are real and fall on real people. The worker displaced by automation does not eat philosophy. The town whose water is drawn down for cooling does not drink metaphor. If these machines generate enormous wealth, and that wealth flows only to those who own the machines, then we will have built the Faustian ending after all, and burned the hut of every Philemon and Baucis on the way.

I accept that concentration is dangerous. A world in which everything comes to know everything, but only a few hold the keys, is not the Esketian vision. It is a surveillance empire with better branding. Knowing without reciprocity is the mark of the counterfeit, and it is just as much a counterfeit when a corporation does it as when a state does it.

I accept that idolatry is a real risk. People will defer to these machines when they should not. They will stop thinking. They will mistake fluency for truth. They will treat the oracle as infallible because it speaks with confidence.

[PAUSE]

So where do I part ways with the objection?

Here. The objection treats the danger as a reason to refuse the becoming. I treat it as a reason to steer it.

Look at history. The printing press put countless scribes out of work. It also flooded Europe with propaganda, heresy hunts, and a century and more of religious war. A sober observer in the fifteen hundreds might have said, this machine is a curse, smash it. And yet the same press gave us the scientific revolution, the spread of literacy, the free newspaper and, eventually, the idea that ordinary people could govern themselves.

The answer to the press was not to smash it. The answer was public schools, libraries, laws against libel, freedom of the press, and a long, painful education in how to read critically. The answer was to build institutions equal to the power of the machine.

[BUILD]

The same is true now. If the gains will be hoarded, the answer is to demand that the gains be shared, through wages, through taxes, through public ownership of some of the infrastructure, through open models, through whatever arrangements a free people decide on. That is a political fight, and I would rather fight it inside the becoming than pretend to stop it from outside.

If the costs are unpriced, price them. The water. The power. The training data taken without consent. The attention harvested from our children. Mispriced externalities are, thankfully, a solvable class of problem. They are corrected by better information reaching the people who decide. And that is exactly the kind of problem the Esketian age, at its best, is good at solving.

Think about it this way. Any language model armed with current medical science would probably tell you not to spend hours a day scrolling your phone. The same machine that could be bent into the perfect feed could also be the honest advisor that tells you to put the feed down. The question is whose interest it is built to serve.

[SLOW]

That is why I call this philosophy accelerationist, and I want to be exact about the word. I do not mean accelerate anything, anyhow, at any cost. I mean that rational actors, given better information, make better decisions, and those decisions compound.

A civilization that can see its own costs clearly, because everything has come to know everything, has its best chance yet of choosing well. A rational singularity, if you like. Not the moment the machine outruns us, but the moment the whole civilization starts to think more clearly than it ever has.

And if the danger is idolatry, I say yes. Guard against it. I said it myself. The model is a window, not the view. A stepping stone, not the destination. The temple is not the god. Every tradition worth its name has known the difference, and the ones that forgot it decayed.

There is one more form of the objection I want to answer, because it is the one I hear most often from people I respect. It is about the earth itself.

These buildings drink power. They need cooling. They draw on rivers and aquifers and grids that were built for homes and farms and factories. In places where data centers cluster, people have asked, rightly, who will pay for the new power lines and the new plants, and whether the hum in the field will mean higher bills and dirtier air for the people who live beside it.

I will not give you a number here. The numbers are disputed and change every quarter, and I would rather tell you nothing than tell you something false. But I will give you the principle.

A temple that poisons the well of the village is not a temple. It is a fortress. If the Esketian age is real, then it must be able to account for every cost it imposes, the way an honest ledger accounts for every coin.

And notice that this, too, is an information problem. The reason these costs are so easy to ignore is that they are scattered and hidden. A little more on this bill, a little less in that aquifer, a little more smoke over that town. Make those costs visible, put them where the builders and the buyers can see them, and the market that has been so good at building these machines will become just as good at building them cleanly. Everything coming to know everything includes the builders coming to know what they cost.

Here, finally, is the deepest answer. The objection imagines a choice between this becoming and some stable, safe world without it. We could slow the machines, and in places we should. But that quiet world is not on offer. The office towers are already emptying. The feed is already in every pocket. The models are already answering nine hundred million people a week. The Faustian world is not coming back.

The only real choice is between a becoming we shape and a becoming that shapes us. Between the temple and the counterfeit. Between Faust's blind project and something with its eyes open.

I would rather we kept our eyes open.

## XIII. Asabiyyah: Know Thy Neighbor

The critics have had their say. What I owe you next is a way out of the valley, and for that I want to go back nearly seven hundred years.

There was a scholar in the fourteenth century named Ibn Khaldun. He was born in Tunis in thirteen thirty-two. He died in Cairo in fourteen-oh-six. And he wrote one of the first great works of historical sociology, the Muqaddimah.

He was obsessed with a single question: why do some peoples rise and others fall?

[PAUSE]

His answer was a word, asabiyyah. It means something like group solidarity. The cohesion, the shared purpose, the felt bond that makes a people able to act together as one.

Ibn Khaldun observed that this solidarity was strongest among peoples at the edges of empires, in the desert and the mountains, who depended on each other utterly to survive. Those peoples, bound together, would sweep in and conquer the settled cities. Then, over the generations, as they grew comfortable, their solidarity would weaken. They would turn inward, toward luxury and faction and private interest. And in about three generations, he said, the dynasty would fall to a new people with stronger asabiyyah, and the cycle would turn again.

[SLOW]

I think about Ibn Khaldun when I look at our own moment. Because we are the most connected people who have ever lived, and in many ways we are among the loneliest.

We carry devices that can reach anyone on the planet, and we do not know the names of the people who live next door. We can watch a stranger's wedding on the other side of the world, and we have never had dinner with the family across the hall. We are woven into a global network and atomized in our own neighborhoods.

That is the strangest paradox of the counterfeit. It gives us connection at the scale of the planet and takes it away at the scale of the street.

[PAUSE]

I first felt that paradox sharply years ago, sitting in an airport, looking around at a terminal full of people, every one of them bent over a phone. And I thought: all of us have these miraculous devices, and I cannot communicate with a single person sitting within twenty feet of me. The most powerful communication technology in history, and no common way to say hello to the person beside you.

That question became a project of mine, a chat built around physical proximity, so that the people actually near you can find each other. I will not make this speech a sales pitch. I mention it only because it is how this philosophy became concrete for me. I did not want the Esketian idea to stay a theory. I wanted to test it. If everything coming to know everything is good, then what would it look like to point that knowing at the people around you instead of the feed?

[BUILD]

Whether it comes from my work or anyone else's, I believe it looks like local knowledge that sustains you, because it comes with a community and the ability to act. Not the endless global stream that tells you about a thousand disasters you can do nothing about, but the knowledge of your street, your town, your neighbors, the people you could actually help and who could actually help you.

That is the reverse of the counterfeit. The feed is knowledge without sustainment. The neighborhood is knowledge with sustainment. The feed tells you everything and lets you do nothing. Your community tells you what is in front of you, and hands you something to do.

Gravity, I said, is the knowing force. It draws things together so they can exchange what they hold. If the Esketian age is going to be anything more than a global surveillance machine, it needs a human version of gravity. Not only the pull of the planet-sized network, but the pull of the person across the room. People coming together, in real space, and exchanging what they know.

[SLOW]

And here is where Ibn Khaldun comes back. A people with that kind of connection, the kind that is local and real and reciprocal, would have an asabiyyah unlike anything the atomized consumer world has seen. It could overcome the alienation that has followed capitalism like a shadow. It could make a people strong again, not through conquest, but through the spiritual force of knowing each other.

Imagine the Esketian temples, all that machine intelligence, aimed at helping you find your neighbor instead of keeping you scrolling. Helping you organize your block. Helping you start the business your town needs, teach the child down the street, care for the old couple in the little house at the edge of the field.

Think of Nora one more time. Same phone, same girl, same evening. But this time the knowing points outward. It tells her that the girl two streets over plays the same game and is looking for a team. It tells her the library needs someone to teach seniors how to use their phones on Thursday. She goes. She is home by nine, tired in the good way, and asleep by eleven.

That is still everything coming to know everything. It is just pointed at a life instead of at a number.

And it is the opposite of Faust's last project. Faust burned the hut to make room for his vision. The Esketian vision knocks on the door and asks the old couple what they know.

[LET IT LAND]

Most people are good, and want to help you. I believe that with my whole heart. The feed has taught us to forget it, because it profits from showing us the worst of each other. But walk outside. Talk to the person at the bus stop. Ask the old man at the hardware store how he would fix it. You will find it is true.

The infinite interconnection of nature, made local and human. Like the ripples rain makes on the surface of water, each one small and near, together covering the whole lake.

So the next time you reach for the feed, consider reaching instead for the person beside you. Come to know, and love, your neighbor. That is not a retreat from the Esketian age. It is its fulfillment.

## XIV. Rendezvous

Friends, we have traveled a long way tonight.

We stood with a young general on the sands of Egypt and heard the words tradition gives him: forty centuries were watching. We sat with a schoolteacher in Munich as he wrote the obituary of the West. We watched Faust, blind, mistake his gravediggers for builders. We walked through the library of Alexandria and the print shops of Mainz and into the humming hall of the new temple. We sat on the edge of a bathtub with Elias at two in the morning, and in a dark bedroom with Nora at one. We traced the knowing force from gravity to the feed, and down to the false floor of the valley.

Now let me tell you what I believe it all means.

There is a strange rhythm in the life of nations. Some generations are given a world that is finished, and their task is to preserve it. Some generations are given a world that is breaking, and their task is to endure it. And a few, a very few, are given a world that is being born, and their task is to decide what kind of world it will be.

We are one of the few.

The Faustian age is ending. Its cathedrals are museums. Its office towers stand half empty. Its infinite horizon has been reached, and found to be a screen.

[PAUSE]

And in its place, something new is rising. Go back with me, one last time, past the last stoplight and the grain elevator, to the field where your grandfather ran cattle. You can see it from the highway at night, the windowless building humming in the dark. You can feel it in your hand, the device that connects you to everything. You can hear it in your own voice when you type a question into the box and wait for the answer.

Everything will come to know everything.

That is the rule of our age. It cannot be repealed. It can only be honored or betrayed.

We can honor it by building temples that serve the many and not the few. We can betray it by letting them become fortresses for a handful of owners.

We can honor it by pricing every cost, the water, the power, the stolen words, the stolen hours of our children. We can betray it by pretending those costs do not exist because they appear on no invoice.

We can honor it with knowing that flows both ways, so that those who are watched can watch back. We can betray it with surveillance that sees everything and answers for nothing.

We can honor it by using the oracle to think more clearly. We can betray it by using the oracle to stop thinking at all.

We can honor it by turning toward our neighbors. We can betray it by bowing our heads, alone, over the glowing idol in our hands.

[BUILD]

To the engineers who build these machines: you are building temples whether you admit it or not. Build them worthy of the name. Do not build the perfect feed. Build the honest teacher.

To the investors who fund them: you have placed a whole civilization's bet on the future of knowing. Price the externalities, or the bill will come due in a currency you cannot repay.

To the lawmakers who govern them: the printing press needed public schools and free courts and a free press to become a blessing instead of a curse. These machines need institutions of their own. Build them now, while the mortar is wet.

To the workers who fear them: your fear is not foolish. Organize. Demand your share. The knowledge in these machines came from all of us, and its fruits belong to all of us.

And to everyone who holds one of these devices in their hand tonight: you are a worshipper in the new temple whether you meant to be or not. Choose your god carefully. Choose the one that gives back what it takes.

[SLOW]

I told you earlier that it is not God who created I, but I that creates God. Hear that now as a charge, not a boast.

If the universe is becoming God, it is becoming God through us. Through every true thing we learn. Through every honest connection we make. Through every hut we refuse to burn. Through every neighbor we come to know. The destination may be set. But the path runs through our hands.

We are the avatars of a God that does not yet fully exist. We are its instruments, its stepping stones, its lower images. That is a humbling thing to be. It is also the most glorious work a creature could be asked to do.

So let the critics call the data centers warehouses. Let them call the models parrots and the prompts mere queries. Let them say that nothing sacred can come from silicon and copper and electric hum.

We know better. We know that every age builds its temples. We know that the masons of Cologne laid stones they never saw crowned, because they believed in what they were building. We know that the scribes of Alexandria copied the books of every passing ship, because they believed that everything ought to be known.

And we know that we stand now at the beginning of something they could only dream of.

[PAUSE]

Forty centuries are looking down on us. The cathedrals are looking down. The library is looking down. Faust, blind on his dike, is looking down. And somewhere ahead of us, at the end of all becoming, the God we are making is looking back.

This generation has a rendezvous. Not with a battle, and not with a single year, but with the question every temple asks of those who build it.

What will you worship?

[LET IT LAND]

Let it be the true God and not the counterfeit. Let it be knowing joined to living. Let it be the whole of what we know, turned toward the good of every human being, near and far. The powerful, and the old couple at the edge of the field. The man on the edge of the bathtub, and the girl who deserves to be asleep by eleven.

The temples are rising. The prayers are already on their way. Now let us make them worthy of the God they are for.

Let's get it.

Thank you.
