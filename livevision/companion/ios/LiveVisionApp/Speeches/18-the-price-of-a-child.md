# The Price of a Child

## I. The Kitchen Table

It is eleven o'clock on a Tuesday night, and two people are sitting at a kitchen table with a laptop open between them.

On the laptop there is a spreadsheet. The spreadsheet has a name. The name is "Baby?" With a question mark.

I will tell you plainly that these two are a composite. I have sat at a hundred versions of that table, and so have you. Call them Dana and Marcus. They are thirty-one. They have been married three years. They both work. By every measure we use to grade a life in this country, they are doing fine.

[PAUSE]

In the first column are the things they know. Rent. Car payment. The student loans, his and hers. The grocery bill that went up and never came back down. In the second column are the things they have looked up. What the daycare down the street charges for an infant. What a two-bedroom costs in a school district they would not be afraid of. What happens to their income if one of them cuts back. What it costs to deliver a baby with the insurance they actually have.

And in the third column, at the bottom, there is a number. It is red.

They look at it for a while. Then Marcus says what people say at that table. He says, "Maybe next year." And Dana says what people say back. She says, "We said that last year."

[LET IT LAND]

Hold on to that spreadsheet for the next two hours. I am going to argue that it is one of the most important documents in America. More important than most bills in Congress. A spreadsheet named "Baby?" on a laptop at a kitchen table, multiplied by millions.

Here is my argument, in one sentence, so that you can hold me to it. Americans want more children than they are having, and a large part of the distance between the family they want and the family they have was built, brick by brick and fee by fee, by the prices we let rise and the policies we chose.

Notice what that thesis does not say. It does not say every person must have children. It does not say the government should set a target and herd people toward it. It does not say money is the only thing that matters. I will be honest with you tonight about the limits of my own case, and I will give the other side its strongest form before I answer it.

But I will say plainly what I believe. We have built a country in which the most ordinary hope there is, the hope to raise a family, has become a luxury good. We have put a price on the cradle. And we did it without ever voting on it.

[SLOW]

No legislature ever passed a law called the Act to Discourage Children. No president ever stood at a podium and said, "I believe young couples should have fewer babies than they want." Nobody decided this. It happened in the zoning board meeting that nobody attended, and in the tuition increase that everybody accepted, and in the daycare that closed because it could not pay its workers, and in the tax credit that lapsed at midnight on a New Year's Eve while the country was looking somewhere else.

It happened in a thousand small rooms. Tonight we will walk through those rooms together, one at a time, the way Abraham Lincoln did it at Cooper Union in the winter of 1860. He did not ask his audience to trust his feelings. He counted. He took the men who signed the Constitution and went through their votes, one by one, until the evidence was so heavy that no one in the hall could lift it off.

So here is the road we will travel. First, what Americans say they want, and what they actually have. Then the bill, line by line: the roof, the daycare, the debt, and the whole total. Then who wrote that bill, which is us, through our laws. Then I will count it all up. Then I will hand the floor to the strongest case against me, and answer it. And at the end, I will tell you what a serious country would do, and ask you to help do it.

[PAUSE]

We start with a question almost nobody asks first.

## II. What They Say They Want

What do people actually want?

Because if Americans simply do not want children anymore, then this speech is over. If the cradle is empty because people prefer it empty, then that is their choice, and it is not my business or yours. A free people may decide to have small families. I would not stand here and scold them.

But that is not what they tell us.

In the summer of 2023, Gallup asked a nationally representative sample of American adults a question it has been asking, on and off, since the 1930s. What do you think is the ideal number of children for a family to have?

Forty-five percent said three or more.

[PAUSE]

Forty-five percent. That was the highest share Gallup had recorded since 1971. Twenty-nine percent said three. Twelve percent said four. Another few percent said five or more. The average ideal, across everyone, came out to two point seven children.

And how many said the ideal number of children was zero? Two percent. Two.

A word of caution, because honesty requires it. An ideal is not a plan. When you ask someone what the ideal family looks like, you are asking about a picture in their head, not about their own calendar. Some people who say three is ideal will tell you, in the same breath, that it is not realistic for them. That is exactly the point. The picture in the head and the life on the ground have come apart.

And here is something else in that same Gallup report that should stop us. The group most likely to say that three or more children is ideal was not the elderly, remembering the big families of their youth. It was adults aged eighteen to twenty-nine. The youngest adults in the country. The very people who are supposedly choosing the childless life.

[LET IT LAND]

Gallup is not alone. The Institute for Family Studies, in work by Wendy Wang and Brad Wilcox published in 2024, put it this way. American women's ideal family size has stayed at slightly more than two children for decades. Their expected lifetime births have fallen to about one point six. The ideal has held still. The reality has walked away from it.

In the summer of 2026, just a few months ago, the same institute released its State of Fertility report, with the demographer Lyman Stone among its authors. As reported, the report puts what Americans say they want at roughly two point four children, against actual fertility below one point six. It called that one of the widest gaps between desired and actual family size in modern history.

Think about what that sentence means. It means that if Americans simply had the families they say they want, this country would not have a birth rate problem at all. We would be above replacement. The so-called demographic crisis, which fills the opinion pages and the podcasts, is not a crisis of desire. It is a crisis of distance.

So let us ask the people in the gap. Why the distance?

The Institute for Family Studies and the Wheatley Institute surveyed women aged eighteen to fifty-five whose desired number of children was larger than the number they had. They asked what was standing in the way. Thirty-six percent said they could not afford more children. And thirty-six percent said they did not have a suitable partner.

[PAUSE]

Hold both of those numbers, because I will come back to the second one. A speech about money that pretends money is the only thing would be a dishonest speech. Love is not a line item. Some of the gap is about marriage and partnership, and I will not pretend otherwise.

But notice that the first number, the money number, ties for the top. More than a third of the women who want more children than they have say, in so many words, that they cannot afford them.

And the survey asked young women under thirty-five about their plans. Thirty percent already had children. Forty-one percent said they wanted to. Fifteen percent were not sure. And fourteen percent said they did not want children.

Fourteen percent. Not a majority. Not even close. Most young women in this country either have children or want them. The childless-by-choice future we keep hearing about is, so far, a story told mostly about people who are telling us something different.

I promised you honesty, so here is a number that cuts the other way. I will deal with it fully later. The Pew Research Center found in 2024 that forty-seven percent of childless adults under fifty said they were unlikely ever to have children. That share had risen ten points since 2018. And of those, a majority said, simply, that they just do not want to.

I take that seriously. I will come back to it. But hold it next to everything else we have heard. The same country where a growing share of the childless say they will stay that way is a country where the young are more likely than anyone to call a big family ideal, and where most women who want more children say money or a partner is the obstacle.

These are not contradictory facts. They are the same fact seen from two sides.

Consider what it is like to be twenty-five in America right now and to want a family. You look at your parents, who may have married in their twenties and bought a house soon after. You look at your own bank account and your own lease. You look at the friends who have had children and you watch them struggle with the bills, and you hear them talk about the waiting lists and the costs. You do not stop wanting a family. But you begin, quietly, to make your peace with having a smaller one, or a later one, or perhaps none at all. A people can want something and, over time, stop expecting it. And when you stop expecting something long enough, you may begin to tell a pollster that you never wanted it at all.

[SLOW]

That is what a price does, when it stays high long enough. It does not only stop you from buying. It teaches you to stop wanting.

## III. What They Have

Now let us look at the other side of the ledger. Not what Americans want, but what they have.

In 1957, at the height of the postwar baby boom, the total fertility rate in the United States was about three point seven seven. That number, the total fertility rate, is the one demographers use. It means, roughly, how many children the average woman would have over her lifetime if the birth rates of that year held steady.

Three point seven seven. Nearly four children per woman.

By 1976, it had fallen to about one point seven four. That was the bottom of the first great decline. And then something happened that we have mostly forgotten. It came back. By 1990 it was about two point oh eight. In 2007, it stood at two point one two, almost exactly what demographers call replacement, the level at which a population holds itself steady from one generation to the next without immigration.

[PAUSE]

Remember that year. Two thousand seven. For a brief moment, the United States was the great exception among rich countries. Europe was aging. Japan was shrinking. And America was replacing itself, the young country, the confident country, the country where people still started families.

Then came the financial crisis. And the number started falling. And it has not stopped.

By 2020 it was about one point six four. In 2023, the final number was one point six two one. In 2024, the National Center for Health Statistics put it at one point five nine nine, the lowest ever recorded in this country. And the provisional figures for 2025, released this spring, show it lower still, at about one point five eight.

[LET IT LAND]

Now put it in births, because a rate is an abstraction and a baby is not.

In 2024, the United States recorded three million six hundred twenty-eight thousand nine hundred thirty-four births.

In 2025, by the provisional count, three million six hundred and six thousand four hundred.

About twenty-two thousand fewer babies. Down about one percent in a single year.

The general fertility rate, which counts births for every thousand women of childbearing age, fell to fifty-three point one in 2025. Reuters reported that this rate has fallen nearly twenty-three percent since 2007.

Twenty-three percent, in less than twenty years.

There is a piece of good news buried in these numbers, and I want to name it, because a speaker who sees only darkness is not seeing clearly. The teen birth rate fell to eleven point seven per thousand, another record low. That is a genuine achievement. Fewer teenagers are having babies before they are ready. No one in this room should want that trend reversed.

But look at what else the data show. Birth rates for women under thirty kept falling. Birth rates for women in their thirties rose a little, not enough to make up the difference. What we are watching is not only a decline. It is a delay. Americans are pushing family life later and later, and the later you start, the fewer children you tend to end up with.

That is not a moral judgment. It is biology, and it is arithmetic. Fertility declines with age. A couple who starts at thirty-four has less time for a second child, let alone a third, than a couple who started at twenty-six. Every year of delay is a year subtracted from the family people say they want.

And delay carries a cost that falls hardest on the people who did everything right. Some couples who wait until their mid-thirties find that the child they postponed does not come easily. Then begins a different kind of bill: the specialists, the tests, the treatments that many insurance plans cover only in part or not at all.

There is a cruelty in that sequence. We price young people out of having children when it is easiest, and then charge them again when it is hardest. The delay is not free. Sometimes it costs the child itself. I will come back to that before the night is over.

And why are people delaying? One more number, from the Census Bureau. In 2024, the median age at first marriage in this country was thirty point two for men and twenty-eight point six for women. Fifty years earlier, in 1974, it was twenty-three point one and twenty-one point one.

[PAUSE]

Seven years for men. Seven and a half for women. In half a century, the start of married life in America has moved back by about the time it takes a child to go from kindergarten to the end of sixth grade.

Where did those years go? Fifty-seven percent of Americans aged eighteen to twenty-four live in their parents' home. Sixteen percent of those aged twenty-five to thirty-four still do. These are not failures of character. These are young adults doing the arithmetic on rent.

So here is where we stand. On one side of the ledger, a people who say they want about two and a half children, and whose youngest adults are more enthusiastic about large families than anyone. On the other side, a fertility rate at its lowest point in the history of the republic, falling fastest among the young, with family life pushed later and later.

[BUILD]

Between those two numbers there is a gap. In that gap, there are children who are wanted and not born. Not children forbidden by any law. Not children rejected by their would-be parents. Children postponed, and postponed, and postponed, until one year the spreadsheet on the kitchen table is closed for good.

So what fills that gap? My answer is that it is filled, in large part, by a bill. Now we read the bill, line by line. And we start where every family has to start. With a roof.

## IV. The House

Every family needs a place to put the crib.

That sounds like a joke. It is the oldest truth in the history of the household. Across nearly every culture and nearly every century, people have married and had children when they could establish a home of their own. The farm passed down. The apartment above the shop. The house with the extra bedroom. When the young can find a place, they start families. When they cannot, they wait.

So let us look at whether the young can find a place.

Every year the National Association of Realtors publishes a profile of the people who bought homes. The latest came out last November, and its numbers should be read aloud in every statehouse in America.

The share of homes bought by first-time buyers fell to twenty-one percent. That is the lowest share since the association began tracking it in 1981. According to the association's own economist, that share has fallen by about half since 2007.

[PAUSE]

There is that year again. Two thousand seven. The year America last replaced itself. The year the first-time buyer share was roughly twice what it is today.

And the age. The median first-time home buyer in America is now forty years old.

Forty.

[LET IT LAND]

I do not think we have absorbed that. The typical American who buys a first home today is forty years old. Not twenty-eight. Not thirty-two. Forty. The age at which, for most women, the biological window for children is closing or closed.

The median repeat buyer is sixty-two. The median buyer overall, in that report, was fifty-nine. The housing market of the United States has become, in large part, a market in which older owners trade homes with one another, while their children and grandchildren rent and wait.

And here is the number from that same report that ought to haunt us. The share of home buyers with children under eighteen living at home fell to twenty-four percent. An all-time low. Fewer than one in four people buying homes in this country are buying them for a family with kids in it.

Now, someone will say: correlation is not causation. Maybe people are buying later because they are having kids later, not the other way around. That is a fair objection, and it deserves a real answer. So let me give you one.

In 2014, two economists, Lisa Dettling of the Federal Reserve and Melissa Kearney of the University of Maryland, published a study in the Journal of Public Economics. It was called "House Prices and Birth Rates." They asked a clean question. When house prices go up in a metropolitan area, what happens to births?

The design is what makes it powerful. A rise in house prices means different things to different people. If you already own a home, rising prices make you richer. Your equity grows. If you rent, rising prices make the home you want further away. So if housing really affects the decision to have children, you would expect rising prices to push births up among owners and down among renters.

That is exactly what they found. In the published version of their study, a ten thousand dollar increase in house prices was associated with about a five percent increase in births among homeowners, and about a two point four percent decrease among non-owners.

[PAUSE]

The same rising price, in the same city, in the same year, raises births among people who own and lowers births among people who rent. Owners and renters in the same city share the same culture, the same churches, the same television. What they do not share is a balance sheet.

In fairness, at the average homeownership rate of the time, Dettling and Kearney found the net effect of rising prices was slightly positive, because owners outnumbered renters. I will not hide that from you. But think about what has happened since. Homeownership among the young has fallen. The first-time share has been cut in half. The group on the losing side of that equation, the renters, the would-be first-time buyers, has become exactly the group that is supposed to be having the babies.

When the young owned homes, rising prices were a windfall for families. Now that the young mostly rent, rising prices are a tax on the families that have not yet been formed.

[BUILD]

And why are prices so high? Housing economics is a field where serious people disagree about a great deal. But there is a point on which a remarkable range of economists, left and right, have come to agree. We do not build enough homes where people want to live, and a big reason we do not is that it is illegal to do so.

Across vast stretches of American cities and suburbs, local rules make it unlawful to build anything but a detached single-family house on a large lot. Minimum lot sizes. Parking quotas. Height caps. Bans on the duplex, the apartment over the garage, the small house on the small lot that built the American middle class in the first place.

Let me take you to where those rules are kept. This is a composite too, but you have read about it in your local paper.

It is seven o'clock on a Thursday night in a suburban council chamber. Folding chairs, fluorescent lights, a microphone on a stand. On the agenda is a proposal to put a row of modest townhouses on a lot that has been empty for years. Two and three bedrooms. Small yards. Priced, the builder says, for young families.

One by one, residents come to the microphone. They worry about traffic. They worry about parking. They worry about the character of the neighborhood. Some of them say, sincerely, that they have nothing against young families, just not here, and not this many.

[PAUSE]

Now look at who is not in the room. The nurse working the night shift. The couple putting a toddler to bed in a one-bedroom across town. The young teacher who would have bought one of those townhouses, and who has never heard of this meeting. The people whose families are being decided are the people who could not come.

The motion fails. The lot stays empty. Nobody in that room voted against a baby. And yet, in a small way, that is what they did.

I will not give you a single national number for how many homes these rules have prevented. The estimates vary widely, and I will not pretend to a precision I do not have. But I will make an argument, and you can judge it.

Every one of those rules has a purpose its defenders can name. Preserve neighborhood character. Prevent congestion. Protect property values. And there it is. Protect property values. When a rule is designed to keep the price of a home high, it is working exactly as intended when the young cannot afford one.

We have built a housing system that treats a high price as a success. And then we wonder why the people who need a lower price are not starting families.

Think of the starter home. The word itself tells you what it was for. It was where you started. Two bedrooms, maybe one bath, a small yard. You brought the first baby home to it, and when the second came you sold it to the next young couple and moved up. That ladder was the engine of family formation in this country for generations.

Picture the postwar suburb, the one in the old photographs. Rows of small, nearly identical houses, built fast on modest lots, sold to young couples who had very little money and a great deal of hope. Architects sneered at them. Critics called them ticky-tacky. And yet those small houses held one of the largest generations in American history. The children who grew up in them became the teachers and engineers and nurses of the decades that followed.

I am not nostalgic for everything about that era, and I will come to its injustice shortly. But notice what it got right. It made the first home small, cheap, and plentiful. It let young couples start with the starter house and trusted them to grow.

Today we ask the opposite. We ask young couples to arrive at their first home already fully formed: the savings built, the debts paid, the careers established. In much of America the bottom rung of the ladder has been sawed off. Starter homes did not become unpopular. In many places, building one became uneconomic or simply unlawful, and the ones that remain have been bid up beyond the reach of the people they were built for.

Now bring it back to the kitchen table. Dana and Marcus, our composite couple, live in a one-bedroom apartment. Where does the baby sleep? In their room, at first. Fine. But they have both read that the second bedroom costs several hundred dollars a month more, and that the house with the yard near a decent school costs more than they can borrow. And so they look at the red number at the bottom of the spreadsheet and they say, maybe next year.

[PAUSE]

That is how a zoning code becomes a birth rate. Not in one dramatic stroke. In the quiet accumulation of "maybe next year," across millions of kitchen tables, while the median first-time buyer creeps past forty.

## V. The Daycare Bill

Suppose Dana and Marcus find the place. Suppose they stretch and borrow and move into the two-bedroom. Now comes the second line on the bill, and for many young families it is the one that breaks the spreadsheet.

Who watches the baby?

[PAUSE]

In most American families with young children, both parents work. That is not a cultural fad. It is the economic floor. For a great many households, a second income is not a luxury; it is the rent. And so the question of who watches the baby while both parents earn is, for most families, a question with a price tag.

Here is the price tag.

Child Care Aware of America, which tracks childcare prices across the country, released its report on 2024 in the spring of 2025. The national average annual price of child care was thirteen thousand one hundred twenty-eight dollars. A year earlier, it was eleven thousand five hundred eighty-two. In a single year, the average price rose by more than fifteen hundred dollars, outpacing inflation.

[PAUSE]

Thirteen thousand dollars a year is the average. For one child. And averages hide the infants, who cost the most, and the cities, where young families cluster.

That same report estimated that the cost of care would consume about ten percent of a married couple's median income, and about thirty-five percent of the median income of a single parent. Thirty-five percent. More than a third of everything a single mother earns, before rent, before food, before anything.

For reference, the federal government's own benchmark, the line our Department of Health and Human Services drew for what a family receiving childcare help should pay, and now the cap on those families' co-payments, is seven percent of family income. We are not near it. For the average married couple we are above it. For the single parent we are five times over it.

[LET IT LAND]

And then this. In forty-five states plus the District of Columbia, the report found that the average annual price of center-based care for two children was higher than the annual mortgage payment. In most of this country, it costs more to have someone watch your two children than it costs to pay for your house.

So when we ask why young couples stop at one, there it is. The second child does not merely add a second set of diapers. In many places it adds a second mortgage.

The financial firm LendingTree publishes an estimate of the cost of raising a child, and its 2026 edition, released this April, puts the first five years of a child's life at about twenty-nine thousand three hundred dollars a year. And the single largest item in that figure, by far, is day care. Infant day care, in their estimate, runs about seventeen thousand two hundred sixty-four dollars a year, more than half the total.

[BUILD]

Now, someone in the back is already thinking: if childcare is so expensive, the workers must be getting rich. They are not. This is one of the cruelest features of the whole system. Childcare is labor-intensive by nature. You cannot safely put one adult in charge of twenty infants. The ratios are low because babies need people. And so the price is high and the wages are low at the same time, and the centers run on margins so thin that a single bad year closes them.

Parents cannot afford to pay more. Workers cannot afford to be paid less. The industry sits squeezed between them. And the families in the middle make the only adjustment left to them. They have fewer children, or they have them later, or one parent leaves the workforce and the household absorbs the loss.

I want to tell you a story about how close this country once came to a different answer.

In December of 1971, Congress passed a bill called the Comprehensive Child Development Act. It was bipartisan in the way bills could be then. It passed the Senate sixty-three to seventeen. It passed the House. It would have created a national network of child development programs, with fees on a sliding scale according to income.

On December tenth, 1971, President Nixon vetoed it. In his veto message he warned against what he called a "communal approach to child-rearing" and spoke of its "family-weakening implications." The Senate tried to override him and fell short.

[PAUSE]

I tell that story not to relitigate Richard Nixon. Reasonable people at the time had real concerns about federal control of how children are raised, and some of those concerns deserve respect today. Any childcare policy worth supporting should let parents choose: a center, a neighbor, a church program, a grandmother, or a parent at home.

I tell the story because of the irony. The bill was vetoed in the name of protecting the family. And in the half century since, the absence of any serious national answer to the question of who watches the baby has become one of the heaviest burdens on the very families the veto was meant to protect.

We said we would not let the government raise our children. Fine. But we did not replace that with anything. We left young parents to face the market alone, and the market charged them more than their mortgage.

[SLOW]

And here is what that does at the kitchen table. Dana and Marcus look up the center down the street. They see the waiting list, which is long, and the monthly fee, which is longer. They calculate what would be left of her paycheck after daycare. In some months, in some cities, for some families, the answer is close to nothing. She would be working to pay someone else to raise her child, and bringing home almost nothing for it.

So they face a choice no generation should have to face as a matter of routine. Give up an income they need, or give up the second child they want. And in a country where most families need both incomes to keep the roof, the choice too often makes itself.

And let me say a word for the family that makes the other choice. The family where one parent stays home.

That family gets almost nothing from our current system. It does not use paid childcare, so most childcare help never reaches it. It gives up an entire income, often during the years when the household's costs are highest. It is doing, unpaid, the same work we charge thirteen thousand dollars a year for when someone else does it. And when that parent tries to return to work years later, the market often treats the gap on the resume as a defect rather than a season of real labor.

I raise this because a serious family policy cannot be only a childcare policy. It has to respect every way that families raise children. The goal is not to push every parent into the workforce, nor to push any parent out of it. The goal is to lower the cost of raising a child, however a family chooses to do it.

## VI. The Diploma and the Debt

The third line on the bill is the one young people carry before they ever sit down at the kitchen table. They carry it out of school.

For two generations, we told every young person in America the same thing. Go to college. It is the path to the middle class. Borrow if you have to; it will pay for itself. And many of them did exactly what we told them.

Here is where it left them.

According to the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, Americans owed one point six four trillion dollars in student loans in the second quarter of 2025. One point six four trillion. About forty-five million Americans carry student debt. In 2023, the typical federal borrower owed nearly forty thousand dollars.

And in that same report from the New York Fed, ten point two percent of all student debt was ninety days or more delinquent. One in ten dollars, seriously behind.

[PAUSE]

I hold myself to one rule tonight: no number I cannot stand behind. I will not tell you that each thousand dollars of debt subtracts some precise fraction of a child. I have seen estimates, and they vary, and I do not trust any single one enough to put it in your ear.

But I do not need a regression to make this argument. I need only the arithmetic of a monthly budget. A loan payment is money that leaves a household every month, for years, often for decades. It is money that does not go into a down payment. It is money that does not go into a savings account for the hospital bill. It is money that does not pay the daycare.

Debt does not only take money. It takes time. A young person with forty thousand dollars of debt does not feel ready to marry until they feel they have a handle on it. They do not feel ready to buy a home until they can save a down payment around it. They do not feel ready for a baby until both of those things are done. Each of those delays is reasonable. Stacked together, they eat a decade.

[BUILD]

And here is the deeper problem, the one that does not show up in any single loan balance. We have made the road to adulthood longer for everyone.

Think about what a credential costs. Not just in money. In years. For a growing number of good jobs, a bachelor's degree is the entry ticket, and for a growing number of others, a master's degree is the ticket that the bachelor's used to be. Each new requirement adds years at the front of a working life, years of tuition and low pay and living in someone else's house.

We call this credential inflation, and like any inflation it is a hidden tax. Employers require degrees for jobs that did not use to require them, because they can. The young comply, because they must. And every year spent acquiring a credential that the job did not truly need is a year not spent building a household.

Here is the human version. A nurse, a teacher, a social worker, a physical therapist. These are the careers we hold up as the backbone of community life. In many of these fields, the expected training has grown over the years. Some now expect graduate degrees for work that once began after a bachelor's or less. And the people who enter them are, very often, women in their twenties: exactly the people whose plans for a family are pushed back by every additional year of schooling and every additional dollar of debt.

[PAUSE]

Let me be fair to the other side of this. Education is good. A more educated country is a richer and freer country. I am not standing here against college, and I am not standing against women's careers. A woman who wants a career and a family should be able to have both, and that is precisely my point. We have arranged the sequence so that the career must be fully built, and paid off, before the family can begin. And by then, for many, the window has narrowed.

Other countries, and our own country in other eras, arranged things differently. They made it possible to start a family while young and build a career alongside it, or to take time out and come back without being punished. We have chosen a sequence that runs: school, debt, more school, more debt, career, savings, house, marriage, and only then, at the very end, child. And we act surprised that the last item on a very long list so often never gets checked.

Let me make this concrete with another composite, built from the kinds of lives many of you will recognize. Call her Anjali. She finished college at twenty-two with debt. She worked for two years and found that the job she wanted required a master's degree, so she went back and borrowed more. She finished at twenty-six. She spent the next four years paying down loans, moving between rentals, and building the career the degrees were supposed to buy. She met someone at twenty-nine. They married at thirty-two, once they felt steady. They began trying for a child at thirty-four.

Nothing in that story is foolish. Every step was the responsible one. Every step was the step we told her to take. And at the end of it, she has, if things go well, perhaps time for two children. The third, the one she told a pollster was ideal when she was nineteen, was lost somewhere in the sequence, not by a decision she made but by a schedule she was handed.

[PAUSE]

Multiply Anjali by millions, and you have a birth rate. Not a rejection of family. A postponement of it, structured into the very path we call success.

## VII. The Whole Bill

Let us add it up.

In 2017, the Department of Agriculture published what turned out to be its last report on what families spend on children. It estimated that a middle-income married couple would spend two hundred thirty-three thousand six hundred ten dollars to raise a child born in 2015 to the age of seventeen. That assumed inflation of about two percent a year.

Inflation did not stay at two percent. In 2022, Isabel Sawhill at the Brookings Institution updated that estimate for higher inflation. Her figure for the same child was three hundred ten thousand six hundred five dollars.

[PAUSE]

Three hundred and ten thousand dollars. And that number does not include college. Not a dollar of it.

LendingTree's 2026 estimate, built on different methods and more recent data, puts the eighteen-year cost at three hundred three thousand four hundred eighteen dollars. In its most expensive state, Hawaii, the figure was more than four hundred twelve thousand.

So the number, however you calculate it, lands around three hundred thousand dollars per child. For two children, six hundred thousand. For the three children that nearly a third of Americans name as the ideal, close to a million dollars.

[LET IT LAND]

Now, let me argue with my own number for a moment, because the people who study this carefully will. These estimates measure average spending, and spending is partly a choice. Families with more money spend more on their children; that does not mean a child requires that money. A second child can wear the first child's coat. A third can share a room. Economists call these economies of scale, and they are real.

That is true. But it misses where the real cost has grown. The largest costs are not the coat and the room. They are the things you cannot hand down. The bedroom you cannot share because the apartment does not have one. The daycare slot you cannot split between two infants. The years of a parent's earnings you cannot get back.

And that last one, the earnings, is the cost that none of these tallies fully captures. Economists call it opportunity cost. It is not what you spend. It is what you give up.

When a parent steps back from paid work to raise a child, or takes the slower track, or turns down the promotion that would require the late nights, the cost of that is real income, often for years. And the better a parent's career prospects, the larger that cost becomes. We have built an economy that rewards uninterrupted work in the prime years, exactly the years when children are born. The price of a child, for a young professional, is not three hundred thousand dollars. It may be far more, measured in the career that bends around the child.

[SLOW]

And there is one more cost that no spreadsheet captures. Time.

We have raised the standard of parenting in this country to a level our grandparents would not recognize. More hours of supervision. More activities. More driving. More of the child's day organized and watched by an adult. Some of that is love. Some of it is fear. And some of it is the law.

Parents today face expectations and rules that make each child more time-consuming than the last generation's children. A family that might once have let a nine-year-old walk to the park alone may now worry about a call to the authorities. Every additional hour a child requires is an hour that must come from a parent or be bought from the market. And either way, it is a cost.

So when the young couple at the kitchen table looks at the red number at the bottom of the spreadsheet, they are not looking at three hundred thousand dollars. They are looking at three hundred thousand dollars, plus the career that might have been, plus the hours that a child now demands, plus the bedroom they do not have, plus the daycare they cannot find, plus the loan payment they are still making.

They are looking at the whole bill. And they are doing what any sensible person does when handed a bill they cannot pay. They are putting it off.

[PAUSE]

Understand this about that choice. It is rational. These are not foolish people. They are not selfish people. They are not people who have been corrupted by consumerism or by screens or by some failure of national character. They are people reading a price tag correctly.

If you want to change what they decide, you will not do it by lecturing them about their values. You will do it by changing the price.

## VIII. How Policy Priced the Family

I have been talking about prices as if they fell from the sky. They did not. Prices are shaped by policy. And the story of how this country priced the family is, in large part, a story of choices made in public buildings.

Let me take you back to June twenty-second, 1944. On that day, President Franklin Roosevelt signed the Servicemen's Readjustment Act. You know it as the G.I. Bill. Among its provisions were home loans to returning veterans, with low interest and no down payment.

What followed was one of the great episodes of family formation in American history. Millions of young families moved out of crowded city apartments and into homes of their own. And the baby boom that followed was not an accident of mood. It rode on a deliberate national decision to put a roof over the heads of young families.

[PAUSE]

Now, honesty requires that I tell you the rest. That bill, as administered, was deeply unjust. In practice it largely excluded Black veterans, through discrimination by banks, by local officials, by the structure of the program itself. The historian Ira Katznelson put the verdict in the title of his book: When Affirmative Action Was White. Any honest account of postwar family policy has to carry that wound with it.

But the lesson I draw is not that we should not have helped young families. It is that we should have helped all of them. The tool worked. It was the hand that held it that was unjust. And when I say this country once knew how to make it possible for young people to start families, I mean that we once knew how, and we chose to do it, and we can choose to do it again, this time for everyone.

[BUILD]

Now jump ahead half a century, to the child tax credit. It is the story of a country that kept almost deciding to stand with families, and kept stepping back.

In 1997, Congress created the child tax credit. Five hundred dollars per child. For most families it was not refundable, which meant that families too poor to owe income tax generally got nothing from it.

In 2017, the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act doubled it to two thousand dollars, and made part of it refundable.

And then came 2021. In the American Rescue Plan, Congress raised the credit to three thousand six hundred dollars for each child under six, and three thousand for each child aged six to seventeen. It made the credit fully refundable, so that the poorest families got the full amount. And it did something this country had never done before. It sent half of it to families every month, starting on July fifteenth, 2021.

Thirty-nine million households received those payments.

What happened next is recorded in the official statistics of the United States government. Under the Census Bureau's Supplemental Poverty Measure, the child poverty rate in 2021 fell to five point two percent. The lowest on record.

And then, on December thirty-first, 2021, the expansion expired. Congress did not renew it.

In 2022, the child poverty rate under that same measure rose to twelve point four percent. It more than doubled in a single year. The Census Bureau reported that refundable tax credits lifted nine point six million people out of poverty in 2021, and six point four million in 2022.

[LET IT LAND]

I want to be fair here, because this is contested ground. The rise in child poverty in 2022 had more than one cause; other pandemic programs ended, and inflation was high. And there was a serious debate, which I respect, about whether a fully refundable credit with no work requirement would discourage work. Reasonable legislators disagreed.

But look at what the episode tells us about the country's priorities. We found a policy that, alongside other pandemic aid, coincided with the lowest child poverty rate ever recorded. And we let it lapse at midnight on New Year's Eve, while the nation's attention was elsewhere. Whatever you think of that decision, do not tell me that the price of a child is a law of nature. It is a choice. We made it in 2021, and we unmade it in 2022.

Now let me bring you to the present, because there has been news.

On July fourth, 2025, President Trump signed the law its authors called the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. It raised the child tax credit from two thousand dollars to two thousand two hundred dollars per child, and indexed it to inflation, so that it would not quietly shrink each year. It also created what it calls Trump Accounts, with a one thousand dollar federal deposit for each citizen child born from 2025 through 2028. It raised the tax credit available to employers who provide childcare, and it raised the limit on dependent care accounts from five thousand to seven thousand five hundred dollars.

[PAUSE]

Give that law its due. Indexing the credit to inflation is a real improvement; the old credit lost value every single year it was not adjusted. Encouraging employers to provide childcare is a good idea. A savings account at birth is a gesture toward the future that I welcome.

Then measure it against the bill we have just added up. Two hundred dollars more a year per child, against a childcare bill of thirteen thousand dollars. A thousand dollars at birth, against a cost of raising a child of three hundred thousand. The refundable portion, the part that reaches families too poor to owe much tax, was not increased. And new Social Security number requirements, by one estimate, put millions of children in mixed-status families outside the credit entirely.

So I say this with respect for those who wrote it. It is a step. It is not a stride. It does not close the gap between the family Americans want and the family they can afford. It is a down payment on a house we have not yet agreed to build.

And policy does not only work through money. It works through rules. Small examples are where you see how a thousand rules add up, so here is one.

Every state in America now requires young children to ride in car seats, and in many states the requirements have been extended to older and older children. That is a safety measure, and it saves lives, and I do not stand against it. But two economists, Jordan Nickerson and David Solomon, noticed something about it. Many ordinary cars cannot fit three car seats across the back row. And so, for a family with two young children, a third child may mean a new car: a minivan, a large SUV, a cost of thousands of dollars that has nothing to do with the baby itself.

Their study, titled "Car Seats as Contraception," estimated that these requirements discouraged several thousand families from having an additional child in 2017 alone.

Several thousand children, in one year, because of the width of a back seat. Now, I am not here to repeal car seat laws. I am here to make a point about how policy works. Each rule, taken alone, has a reason. Each is defended by people who mean well. But no one ever asks what all the rules together do to the cost of raising a family. No one adds them up. There is no line in any government budget called "the cost we have imposed on the decision to have a child." And because no one adds it up, it grows without anyone deciding that it should.

That is how policy priced the family. Not by a single cruel law. By a thousand reasonable ones, and the absence of anyone whose job it was to count.

And there is one more policy that deserves a place in this account, because it is not a rule we made but a promise we never made at all.

In 1993, Congress passed the Family and Medical Leave Act. It was a real achievement, and the people who fought for it deserve our thanks. It guaranteed many workers up to twelve weeks of leave after the birth of a child. But that leave is unpaid. And it does not cover every worker; those at small employers, and those who have not been in their jobs long enough, are left outside it.

Think about what that means at the kitchen table. The law protects your job while you recover from childbirth and care for a newborn, but it does not pay your rent. For a family living paycheck to paycheck, unpaid leave is leave in name only. And so, in the most expensive weeks of a young family's life, many parents return to work before they are ready, or they go without income they cannot spare.

A growing number of states have created paid family leave programs of their own, and that is to their credit. But when the Pew Research Center compared forty-one countries, this was the only one with no national requirement of paid leave for new parents. Whatever you think the right design is, and there are serious debates about how to fund it and who should run it, do not tell me that the absence of any national answer is neutral. It is one more line on the bill. One more red number at the bottom of the spreadsheet.

## IX. The Count

So now we count. We do what Lincoln did, and lay the evidence on the table one piece at a time, until it is too heavy to lift.

Lincoln, at Cooper Union, took the thirty-nine men who signed the Constitution and asked how each of them had acted on the question before him. He went through them, name by name, vote by vote, and at the end he could say that a clear majority had stood where he stood. He did not ask his audience to agree with his opinion. He asked them to look at the record.

So let us look at the record. Not the record of thirty-nine signers. The record of the American family in our time.

[SLOW]

How many Americans say the ideal family has no children? Two percent.

How many say three or more? Forty-five percent, the highest since 1971.

Who is most likely to say three or more? The youngest adults.

How many children do American women say they want? Slightly more than two, steady for decades.

How many are they on track to have? About one point six.

[BUILD]

Where was the fertility rate in 2007, before the financial crisis? About two point one two, at replacement.

Where is it now? About one point five eight, the lowest ever recorded.

What happened to the share of first-time home buyers since 2007? It was cut in half, to twenty-one percent.

How old is the typical first-time buyer? Forty.

What share of home buyers have children at home? Twenty-four percent, an all-time low.

When house prices rise, what happens to births among renters? They fall.

[BUILD]

What does childcare cost, on average, for one child? Thirteen thousand one hundred twenty-eight dollars.

How much did that rise in a single year? More than fifteen hundred dollars, faster than inflation.

In how many states does care for two children cost more than a mortgage? Forty-five, plus the capital.

What share of a single mother's income does it take? Thirty-five percent, five times the government's own benchmark.

How much do Americans owe in student loans? One point six four trillion dollars.

How much later do Americans marry than they did fifty years ago? About seven years.

What does it cost to raise a child to adulthood? About three hundred thousand dollars, before college.

[BUILD]

What happened to child poverty when we expanded the child tax credit? It fell to the lowest rate ever recorded.

What happened when we let it lapse? It more than doubled.

How much did the newest law raise the credit? Two hundred dollars.

And among women who want more children than they have, what is the most common reason they give? A tie. A partner, and money. And money is in that tie.

[PAUSE]

That is the record. I did not have to stretch it. I did not have to invent a single number. I went to the Census Bureau and the Centers for Disease Control and the Federal Reserve and Gallup and the people who sell houses for a living, and I asked them what they found. And every one of them, in their own language, told the same story.

A people who want children. A country that has made children expensive. And a gap between the two that grows every year.

Lincoln, at the end of his count, turned to the people who disagreed with him and said, in effect: if you want to depart from the record, you must bring evidence equal to it. I will say the same. If you want to tell me that Americans simply do not want children anymore, you must explain why they keep telling us they do. If you want to tell me that money has nothing to do with it, you must explain why renters and owners in the same city respond to the same house prices in opposite ways. If you want to tell me the price of a child is a fact of nature, you must explain why child poverty fell to a record low and then more than doubled at the stroke of a congressional pen.

[LET IT LAND]

That is a heavy burden. But I promised you I would not pretend it cannot be met. There are serious people who think they have met it. And so now, before I ask anything of you, I am going to give them the floor.

## X. The Strongest Objection

Here is the case against me, in its strongest form. I will make it as well as I can, because an argument that has never met its best opponent has not earned your trust.

The objection begins with two economists, Melissa Kearney and Phillip Levine. Kearney, you will remember, is one of the authors of the housing study I cited. She is not a person who doubts that money matters. And in work published over the last few years, first in a 2022 article in the Journal of Economic Perspectives with their colleague Luke Pardue, then in a 2025 paper of their own, Kearney and Levine asked the question I have been asking. Why has the American birth rate fallen so steadily since 2007?

They looked at the economy. They looked at policy. They looked at changes in the makeup of the population. And here is what they reported. They could not identify any period-specific economic, social or policy change that statistically explains much of the decline. When they compared states, they found no clear relationship between changes in birth rates and changes in childcare prices or home rental prices.

[PAUSE]

That is a serious blow. The people who have looked hardest at the data say that the prices I have been piling up do not, in their analysis, explain the fall.

What does explain it, in their view? They point to something harder to measure. They found that American women born after the mid-1980s are having fewer children at every age. It is not just that they are waiting. They are having fewer children across their whole childbearing years. And the explanation Kearney and Levine offer is what they call shifting priorities. Each new cohort of young adults, they suggest, places a different weight on children relative to everything else a life can hold: careers, travel, friendship, leisure, self-development.

[BUILD]

And the objection does not stop at our borders. Look abroad, the critics say, and you will see the same thing everywhere.

Look at Finland. Finland has some of the most generous family policies in the world. Subsidized childcare, long parental leave, a famous baby box for every newborn. In 2010, Finland's fertility rate was about one point eight seven, among the highest in Europe. By 2023 it had fallen to about one point two six, the lowest in its recorded history. In 2024, about one point two five. All that generosity, and the rate fell anyway.

Look at Hungary. Hungary has made raising the birth rate a national project, with loans for young married couples, housing support, and income tax exemptions for mothers of several children, extended for mothers of four or more to a lifetime exemption. And for a while, it seemed to work. In 2010, Hungary's fertility rate was about one point two five. By 2021 it had climbed to about one point six one.

Then it fell again. About one point five one in 2023. About one point three nine in 2024. And by early figures, lower still in 2025.

Look at South Korea. South Korea has spent heavily, for two decades, on policies to raise the birth rate. In 2015 its fertility rate was about one point two four. By 2018 it had fallen below one. By 2023 it was about zero point seven two, the lowest in the world.

Then come back home, and look again at that Pew survey. Forty-seven percent of childless adults under fifty say they are unlikely ever to have children.

Ask them why, and the most common answer, from fifty-seven percent of them, is simply that they do not want to. Forty-four percent say they want to focus on other things. Thirty-eight percent point to the state of the world. Money comes further down the list, at thirty-six percent. The critic will say: there you have it. These are people with other plans and darker views of the future. That is culture, not cost.

So here is the objection in a single sentence. You have shown that children are expensive, the critic says. But children have always been expensive, and fertility is falling even where governments pay generously, and the best American research cannot find prices in the data, and the childless themselves mostly say they just do not want children. It is not the price, the critic says. It is the preference. And no tax credit will change what people want.

[LET IT LAND]

That is a strong argument. It is made by honest people with good data. If I could not answer it, I would have to stop and go home.

So first, what I concede.

I concede that money alone will not restore the birth rates of 1957. No serious person believes that. Hungary's experience shows that large incentives can move the numbers for a time and still not hold them. South Korea shows that spending, by itself, is no guarantee. Finland shows that generous family policy does not inoculate a country against decline.

I concede that culture matters. The meaning people attach to children, the place of family in a good life, the expectations around marriage, the hours that screens now take from courtship and friendship: these are real, and they are not all about money.

I concede that some Americans do not want children, and that this is their right, and that a country that respects freedom must respect it without sneering.

And I concede that a speech that promised you a cheap fix would be lying to you.

And I concede the point about worry. A young person who looks at the world and feels afraid, of war, of instability, of a future they cannot picture, may hesitate to bring a child into it for reasons no tax credit can touch. I do not dismiss that fear. But I will say this much. Some of what people call the state of the world is the state of their own prospects. It is easier to believe in the future when you can see your own place in it. A young couple with a home, a manageable bill for care, and a path forward tends to feel the world is a place a child can grow up in. A young couple renting month to month, with debt and no savings, may feel the same world closing in. Fear about the world and fear about the rent are not always as separate as the survey categories make them look.

[PAUSE]

And there is one more thing I will not do. I will not let the objection become permission to do nothing. Because I know what doing nothing looks like. It looks like the kitchen table, seven years on.

Go back to Dana and Marcus. Suppose nothing changes. Suppose they say "maybe next year" and mean it, and then say it again. The rent goes up. The loans shrink slowly. The daycare on the corner raises its fee and keeps its waiting list. They are careful. They are responsible. They wait until the number turns black.

It is a different room now. Not a kitchen, but a clinic waiting room, with a rack of brochures and a television nobody is watching. Dana is thirty-eight. In her lap there is a sheet of paper with prices on it, for treatments their insurance covers only in part. It is a spreadsheet of a different kind. And this time the question mark is not about whether they can afford a child. It is about whether a child will come at all.

[SLOW]

I will not tell you how that story ends, because for real couples it ends both ways, and some of them may be in this room tonight. What I will tell you is that nobody chose that waiting room. No law put them there. A decade of reasonable delays did, each one priced into their lives by a bill they never voted on.

That is what doing nothing looks like. It does not look like a crisis. It looks like a couple doing everything right, and running out of time.

[PAUSE]

So I do not concede the conclusion. Because the objection, for all its strength, has three holes in it. And I am going to walk through each of them.

## XI. The Answer

The first hole is this. Priorities are not formed in a vacuum. They are formed under prices.

Kearney and Levine are careful scholars, and they are careful to say that they cannot pin down why priorities shifted. They describe the shift; they do not claim to know its full cause. And so let me ask the obvious question. Why would the priorities of Americans born after the mid-1980s shift away from children?

Think about who those Americans are. They are the generation that entered adulthood in or after the financial crisis of 2008. They are the generation that took on the largest student debts in history. They are the generation that watched the first-time buyer share fall by half and the first-time buyer age climb toward forty. They are the generation that saw the price of childcare climb year after year.

[BUILD]

When a whole generation grows up under prices like that, it does not just postpone children. It recalculates what a good life is. It learns to build a life that does not depend on the thing it cannot afford. It finds meaning in travel because travel is cheaper than a house. It invests in a career because a career is the only asset within reach. And then, when a researcher asks, it reports a change in priorities. And the researcher, correctly, records a change in priorities.

But a priority formed under a price is not independent of the price. A cross-state comparison in a single decade will not catch a cost that has been rising nationally for a generation and has been absorbed into the expectations of everyone who came of age under it. You cannot see the effect of the tide by comparing one beach to another. The whole ocean rose.

[PAUSE]

I said earlier that a price, held high long enough, teaches people to stop wanting. That is not a rhetorical trick. It is how human beings adapt. If you tell a generation for fifteen years that a family is something you can afford only at forty, you should not be surprised when some of them tell you, at thirty, that they never wanted one.

The second hole is this. The objection proves less than it claims about other countries.

Look at Finland again. Yes, its policies are generous. But generous compared with what? A benefit is generous or stingy only against the costs it meets, and benefits that looked generous in 2010 meet a different world today. I will not pretend to know how much of Finland's decline is about money; I suspect much of it is not. I claim something narrower. A country being kinder than America does not prove that kindness is irrelevant. It proves only that kindness is not sufficient, which I have already conceded.

And look at what Hungary's numbers actually show. For a decade, the rate rose substantially, from one point two five to one point six one. That is not nothing. Behind that change in a rate stand real children in real families. That the gains later eroded tells us that incentives must be sustained and that they cannot do the work alone. It does not tell us they did nothing.

And South Korea? South Korea is the strongest case for my side, not theirs. Its housing costs and its education arms race are notorious. In one national survey from 2005, nearly one in five women in their twenties said the extra cost of education was why they would not have a second child. In a 2012 survey by the health ministry, nine in ten respondents said high education costs made them reluctant to have children. A country can spend money on the birth rate while the underlying costs of raising a child keep rising faster than the spending. That is not evidence that costs do not matter. It is evidence that a subsidy cannot outrun an inflation it does nothing to stop. And note that in 2024 and 2025, the Korean rate ticked up, to about zero point seven five and then about zero point eight. Too early to call a turn. But not a picture of total futility either.

[BUILD]

The third hole is the most important one. It is about what we are trying to do.

The objection assumes that the goal of family policy is to raise the national birth rate to some target, and that if policy cannot hit the target, it has failed. But that is not my goal. That has never been my goal.

My goal is to close the gap between the family Americans want and the family they can have. Not to make anyone have a child. Not to set a quota. To remove the obstacles we put in the way of people who are already trying.

Measured against that goal, the Pew survey does not refute me. It confirms me. Fifty-seven percent of the childless-by-expectation say they simply do not want children. Fine. Let them live as they choose. But thirty-six percent of that same group say they cannot afford to. More than a third of the people who expect never to have children are telling us that money is part of why. And they are only the ones who have given up. Behind them stand the millions more who have not given up yet: the women who want more children than they have, of whom more than a third cite money. The couples at the kitchen table saying, maybe next year.

[PAUSE]

And now let me come back to the other number I asked you to hold. The partner number. Thirty-six percent of women who want more children than they have say they have no suitable partner.

I said I would not pretend that love is a line item. I will not. But I will make an argument, and you can weigh it. Marriage, too, responds to economics. It has long been the pattern, in this country and others, that people tend to marry when they feel they can set up a household, and to wait when they cannot. A young man who cannot afford a place of his own, who is carrying debt, who sees no path to a home, is a young man who often does not feel ready to propose. A young woman looking at a partner in that situation may reasonably wait.

So even the partner gap is not wholly separate from the price gap. The rent that keeps a young couple from having a second child also keeps some young couples from forming at all.

[LET IT LAND]

So here is my answer to the strongest objection. Yes, culture matters. Yes, priorities have shifted. Yes, money will not do everything. But priorities shifted under a generation of rising prices; other countries show that what we spend must keep pace with what we charge; and the goal is not a quota, it is freedom. The freedom to have the family you say you want.

And on that goal, the evidence is overwhelming. People want children. They tell us money is in the way. We know how to lower some of the costs. We have done it before, for some families, for a while. The question is not whether we can. The question is whether we will.

## XII. What a Serious Country Would Do

So what would a serious country do?

I am going to give you an agenda. These are my proposals, not estimates from any agency, and you should judge them as arguments. I am not going to tell you that any one of them will add a precise number of births. I have spent this whole speech refusing to invent numbers, and I am not going to start now. What I will tell you is that each one attacks a cost I have shown you, and that together they would begin to close the gap between what Americans want and what they can afford.

And I am going to organize them the way the bill is organized. Roof. Care. Debt. Cash. Time.

[PAUSE]

First, the roof.

Make it legal to build the starter home again. That is the single most important thing this country could do for young families, and it costs the taxpayer almost nothing. It requires only that we stop forbidding what young families need.

Let a property owner build a duplex where a single house stands. Let them add the apartment over the garage. Let builders put small homes on small lots. Stop requiring parking spaces that nobody asked for, which raise the cost of every apartment. Let apartments rise near the train stations and the job centers where the young actually work. Speed up the permits, so that a home approved does not sit unbuilt for years while carrying costs pile up.

Most of these decisions are made by states and localities, not by Washington. And so I say to every governor and every state legislator: you hold more power over the American birth rate than any president. You can let your cities build. And to every city council and every zoning board: remember who is not in your folding chairs. The nurse on the night shift. The couple putting a toddler to bed across town. The people who are not at your Thursday meeting are the families you are deciding.

[BUILD]

Federal policy can help. Washington can tie a portion of its transportation and infrastructure money to the willingness of local governments to allow more homes. It can stop subsidizing the rules that keep homes scarce. And it can use the tools it used in 1944, the home loan, the guarantee, the lower barrier to a first purchase, but this time without the exclusions that stained the last great effort. A country that once decided to put a roof over the heads of millions of young families can decide to do it again.

[PAUSE]

Second, care.

Build a childcare system in which parents choose and the public pays its share. Not a single government program that tells families how to raise their children. That was the fear in 1971, and it is a fear worth respecting. A system of funding that follows the child to the care the parents choose. A center, if that is what they want. A church program, a cooperative, a home-based provider down the street. And a parent at home, too, if that is the family's choice, because a parent who stays home to raise a child is providing childcare, and a fair policy would not pretend otherwise.

Set a goal that matches the government's own benchmark. No family should have to pay more than seven percent of its income for the care of its young children. We set that benchmark ourselves. We should meet it.

And while we are at it, pay the people who do this work enough to stay in it. A childcare system built on poverty wages is a childcare system built to collapse. The thin margins I described earlier will not be fixed by asking parents to pay more. They can only be fixed if the public shares the cost, the way it already shares the cost of educating a child from age five onward.

Think about that last point. We already have a public commitment to children. It starts at five. We pay, as a society, for kindergarten through twelfth grade, and almost no one thinks that is radical. But the years from birth to five, the years when the costs crush young families hardest, we leave almost entirely to the market. There is no law of nature that says public support for children must begin at five. It is a line we drew. We can draw it somewhere else.

Third, debt and the long road.

Stop making the road to adulthood longer than it needs to be. Government can begin with its own hiring: drop degree requirements for jobs that do not need them, and judge applicants by what they can do. Some states have already begun to do this, and others should follow.

Expand the paths into good work that do not require a mountain of debt: apprenticeships, technical training, earn-while-you-learn programs. A young person who reaches a good wage at twenty-two, with no debt, can start a family at twenty-five instead of thirty-four.

And for those who already carry debt, make repayment fit a family. I will not stand here and promise you that all student debt will be forgiven; that fight has been fought in Congress and in the courts, and I want to talk about what can actually be done. But a repayment system can be built to recognize that a borrower who has just had a child faces new costs. Pause payments around a birth. Cap payments as a share of income in a way that counts the children in the household. Let a growing family breathe.

Fourth, cash.

Make the child tax credit large, fully refundable, and paid regularly. We tried that once. In the year we did, child poverty fell to a record low. The recent law moved the credit to two thousand two hundred dollars and indexed it, and I give it credit for that. But we know from our own experience what a larger, monthly credit can do for families, and we know what happened when it lapsed.

And I want to answer the objection about work directly, because it is a serious one. If the worry is that a fully refundable credit discourages work, then design it to support work and family together. Pay a substantial base benefit for every child in the first years of life, when the costs are highest and when parents are most likely to cut back. Every parent with a newborn is working, whether or not they draw a wage. A country that believes in work should honor the work of raising a child.

I do not claim cash alone will raise the birth rate to replacement. Hungary has taught us that. What cash does is lower the price at the kitchen table. And lowering the price is the point.

Fifth, time.

Give new parents paid leave, so that the birth of a child is not also the loss of a paycheck. Protect the jobs of parents who step back for a time and want to return. And let us look hard, as a country, at the thousand rules that make each child more costly in money and in hours than they need to be. The car seat rule that requires a new car. The supervision expectations that turn a nine-year-old's walk to the park into a legal risk for the parents. Let us count those costs, for once, and ask whether each rule is worth what it charges young families.

[BUILD]

And here is the last proposal, and in some ways the most important. Make someone count.

There is no office in the federal government whose job it is to add up the cost that our laws and regulations impose on the decision to have a child. We count the cost of regulations to businesses. We estimate the effect of laws on the deficit. We do not estimate the effect of laws on the family. So let us start. Every major bill and every major rule should come with a family impact estimate: what does this do to the cost of housing, of care, of raising a child? Not to forbid anything. Just to make sure that the next thousand reasonable rules do not do, silently, what the last thousand did.

That is the agenda. Roof, care, debt, cash, time. And someone whose job it is to count.

[PAUSE]

Now, someone will ask the question every agenda must answer. How do we pay for it?

I will not pretend this is free. Some of it is nearly free; legalizing housing costs the treasury almost nothing. Some of it, especially childcare and a larger child credit, costs real money. And so I will give you an answer that is not a number but a principle.

Ask what we spend on the other end of life. This country has made a solemn commitment to its elderly, through Social Security and Medicare, and I support that commitment. But a commitment to the old is, in the end, a promise paid by the young. Every one of those programs depends on a next generation of workers to fund it. A country that spends generously on the end of life and stingily on its beginning is drawing on a generation it has not helped to be born.

We are not choosing between spending money on families and saving it. We are choosing between investing in the people who will pay for everything else, or not.

## XIII. The Producers of the Future

And that brings me to William Jennings Bryan.

On July ninth, 1896, in the Chicago Coliseum, a young former congressman from Nebraska stood up at the Democratic National Convention to speak on the money question. Bryan was arguing about gold and silver, which is not our argument. But underneath the currency fight was an idea that is very much ours.

Bryan's great move that day was to redefine who counted. The comfortable men of his time talked about "business interests," and they meant the bankers and the financiers. Bryan said: you have made the definition of a businessman too narrow. The farmer who rises before dawn and toils all day is a businessman. The miner who goes down into the earth is a businessman. The merchant at the crossroads store is a businessman. And he said, in effect, that the wealth of the nation rose from them, not from the speculators who traded it.

[PAUSE]

I want to do the same thing tonight. I want to redefine who counts.

Who are the producers of the American future?

They are the parents.

[BUILD]

The mother up at three in the morning with a feverish child, who will still be at her desk at nine, is a producer of the American future. The father who drives the late shift so that he can be home for the morning school run is a producer of the American future. The couple who chose the bigger apartment and the longer commute and the older car because the second child needed a room is producing the future of this country, every day, at their own expense.

Every worker who will pay into Social Security in 2050 is, right now, a child being raised by someone. Every doctor who will treat us in our old age, every nurse, every soldier, every engineer, every teacher of our grandchildren, is today being fed and clothed and driven and worried over by a parent who is paying almost all of the cost.

[SLOW]

We rarely say this aloud. In our system, the cost of raising the next generation is borne mostly by parents. But the benefits of that next generation are shared by everyone. The worker who pays for my retirement was raised by someone else's sacrifice. The child who grows up to fund the pension system was paid for, almost entirely, by his parents, and his taxes will be spread across all of us, parents and the childless alike.

I do not say that to set parents against those without children. Many people without children of their own give enormously to the next generation, as teachers, as aunts and uncles, as neighbors, as taxpayers who fund schools for children they will never meet. And many who are childless did not choose it; some of them sat at the very kitchen table I described and waited too long, or never found the partner, or faced infertility. They deserve compassion, not judgment.

I say it to make one point. Raising a child is not a private hobby, like collecting stamps. It is the production of the future, and the whole society lives on what it produces. And when we leave parents to pay nearly the whole cost of a good that everyone shares, we should not be surprised that we get less of it than we need, and less of it than the parents themselves would choose.

[BUILD]

Bryan said that if you burned down the cities and left the farms, the cities would spring up again, but if you destroyed the farms, grass would grow in the streets of every city in the country. He meant that the producers were the foundation.

Let me say it my own way. You can let the housing market cool and it will recover. You can let the stock market fall and it will rise again. You can run a deficit and pay it down. But if you let a generation of children go unborn, there is no recovery for that. There is no bailout for a missing generation. The classrooms will be empty, then the workplaces, then the tax rolls, and no act of Congress passed in 2050 can go back and make a child born in 2026.

[LET IT LAND]

The comfortable will tell you this is not urgent. They will tell you the birth rate is a long-term concern, a question for demographers, a matter that can be taken up when the budget is more forgiving. I say to them what Bryan's people said to theirs. We have waited long enough for the right time. The young couples of this country have been told "next year" by their own spreadsheets for fifteen years. They do not need to hear it from their government too.

And let me say something to my friends on both sides of the aisle, because this is not a partisan cause and I refuse to let it become one.

To those on the right who speak of family values: I believe you mean it. Then build the houses. Fund the care. Expand the credit. A family value that costs nothing is a slogan. A family value that changes the price at the kitchen table is a policy.

To those on the left who speak of economic justice: I believe you mean it too. Then do not treat a young couple's wish for a third child as a quaint preference. It is as worthy of public support as any other aspiration. The freedom to have the children you want is as much a matter of justice as the freedom not to.

[PAUSE]

There is a coalition waiting to be built here, from the church basement to the union hall, from the suburban mother to the urban renter, from the rural grandparent to the young professional who wonders if she waited too long. They disagree about almost everything. But on this they agree. A young family that wants a child should be able to afford one.

## XIV. Back to the Kitchen Table

Let me take you back to where we started. Back to the kitchen table, and Dana and Marcus, and the spreadsheet named "Baby?" with a question mark.

We are going to run their spreadsheet again. Not in the America we have. In the America we could build.

[PAUSE]

The first line, rent. But in this America, their city has let builders put up the small homes and the duplexes and the apartments near the train. There are more homes, and so the price of the second bedroom is not a cliff. The starter home is back. It is not a mansion. It is two bedrooms and a small yard on a small lot, and it is within reach of two working people at thirty-one instead of forty.

The next line, the daycare. In this America, the public shares the cost the way it shares the cost of first grade. They pay a portion they can manage, a portion no larger than the benchmark the government set for itself. And if Dana wants to stay home for the first year, that choice is honored too, because the support follows the child and not the building.

Then the loans. In this America, Marcus's payments are set as a share of income that counts the new child, and they pause for the months around the birth. The debt is still there. It is no longer a wall.

Then the credit. A real child benefit, arriving regularly, sized for the years when the costs are highest. Not a fortune. But enough to change the color of a number.

And then time. Paid leave, so that the birth is not also a lost paycheck. A job that will be there when they come back.

[SLOW]

And at the bottom of the spreadsheet, there is a number. And for the first time, it is not red.

[LET IT LAND]

I do not promise you that every couple in that America would have a child. Some would choose not to, and that would be their right. I do not promise you that the birth rate would return to the boom years. It would not. I do not promise you that money cures loneliness or finds anyone a partner or answers the deepest questions about meaning and purpose that every generation must answer for itself.

What I promise you is this. In that America, when Dana and Marcus decide, they will be deciding about their lives, and not about a price that we set for them without asking. If they say "not yet," it will be because they mean "not yet," and not because a zoning board and a daycare invoice and a loan servicer said it for them.

That is what freedom means at a kitchen table. Not the freedom to be told no by the market and then be lectured about your values. The freedom to choose, with a fair chance of being able to carry out what you choose.

[PAUSE]

And let me speak for a moment to the people who already said yes. The parents in this room, and listening tonight, who looked at the red number and had the child anyway. You stretched. You sacrificed. You took the smaller apartment, the older car, the slower career, the longer nights. You paid for the future out of your own pockets, and most of the country never thanked you.

I am thanking you. And I am telling you that you should not have had to do it alone. The fight I am asking for tonight is not only for the families that have not yet formed. It is for you, so that your second child, or your third, is not a sentence to another decade of strain. And it is for your children, so that when they sit at their own kitchen table, they find a better spreadsheet than you did.

And to the young people listening, who are not yet at that table, or who are sitting at it right now and saying "maybe next year": I am not here to tell you what to want. That is yours. But I am here to tell you that if what you want is a family, you are not foolish for wanting it, and you are not alone in finding it hard. The difficulty is not a verdict on you. It is a verdict on the country we built around you. And countries can be rebuilt.

[BUILD]

We have rebuilt it before. We put a roof over the heads of a generation in the 1940s, imperfectly and unjustly in part, but on a scale that changed the country. We built a public school system that carries every child from five to eighteen. We built a retirement system that keeps the old out of poverty. In 2021, for one year, we sent a monthly benefit to the families of tens of millions of children, and child poverty fell to a record low.

These were choices. Each of them was once called too expensive, too ambitious, too far outside the role of government. And each of them, once made, became so much a part of American life that we can barely imagine the country without it.

So do not tell me we cannot do this. We have done harder things. We have simply not decided that this thing matters.

## XV. You Shall Not Price the Cradle Out of Reach

Let me close.

For fifteen years, the young families of this country have done everything we asked of them. We told them to go to school, and they went. We told them to borrow, and they borrowed. We told them to work hard and wait their turn, and they worked, and they waited.

They waited for the rent to come down. It did not.

They waited for the daycare bill to level off. It did not.

They waited for the starter home. They are still waiting at forty.

We have heard from them. Not in protests in the street, but in the quietest way a people can speak. They have spoken in the births that did not happen. They have spoken in the weddings pushed back seven years. They have spoken in the third child who became the second, and the second child who became the only, and the only child who became a "maybe someday."

They have told the pollsters what they want. Two children, three children, a family. They have told the surveys what stands in the way. A partner, and money. And they have told us, in the numbers that come every spring from the Centers for Disease Control, what happens when nobody listens.

[PAUSE]

We have petitioned for a fair price on a home, and we have been told the market will provide.

We have pleaded for care for our children, and we have been told it is not the government's business.

We have asked for a credit that lasts, and we have watched it lapse at midnight on New Year's Eve.

We petition no more. We plead no more. We ask no more.

We say instead: the family is not a luxury good. A child is not a line item to be postponed until the debts are paid and the career is built and the house is bought at forty. The hope of raising children is the oldest and most ordinary hope there is, and a country that prices it out of reach of its own young people has forgotten what a country is for.

To the zoning boards that protect a price above a family: we will be at your meetings. We will be in those folding chairs.

To the legislators who let the credit lapse: we will be at your elections.

To everyone who says this can wait: the children we are talking about cannot wait. They do not exist yet. And if we do not act, some of them never will.

[SLOW]

Lincoln ended his evening at Cooper Union by asking his listeners to have faith that right makes might, and to dare to do their duty as they understood it. Bryan ended his afternoon in Chicago with an image of sacrifice so stark that the hall fell silent before it erupted.

I will not borrow their words. I will tell you mine.

There is a cradle in this country that is waiting to be filled. It sits in an apartment with no second bedroom. It sits in a spreadsheet with a red number at the bottom. It sits in the heart of a young woman who told a survey she wants three children and has none. It sits in the hope of a young man who will not propose until he can afford a home.

It is not empty because they do not want it filled. It is empty because we put a price on it.

And somewhere tonight, at a kitchen table, two people are looking at a red number and saying "maybe next year." They have said it long enough. Let us be the country that changes the number, so that next year can finally become this year.

[BUILD]

So hear me, everyone who holds a pen that writes a budget, a code, a rule. You may tax the luxury. You may price the vacation, the second car, the house at the beach. But you shall not lay the cost of the whole future on the backs of the young and call it their choice. You shall not set the price of the cradle beyond the reach of the people who would rock it.

[PAUSE]

You shall not price the cradle out of reach.

[LET IT LAND]

Thank you.
