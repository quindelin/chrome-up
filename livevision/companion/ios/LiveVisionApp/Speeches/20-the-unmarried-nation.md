# The Unmarried Nation

## I. The Empty Chair

Good evening, my friends.

There is a chair at the end of your Thanksgiving table that nobody talks about.

You know the table. The turkey. The uncle who argues about football. The grandmother who still makes the pie from her mother's recipe. And at the far end, a chair with nothing on it but a coat somebody threw over it on the way in.

Twenty years ago that chair held a new boyfriend. A nervous fiancée. A young wife with a baby who would not stop squirming.

[PAUSE]

Nobody mentions the chair. It would be rude. Your son is thirty-one. He has a good job, he lives two states away, and when your aunt asks whether he is seeing anyone, he smiles and changes the subject. Your daughter is twenty-nine. She has two degrees and a demanding career, and she tells you, with a tired laugh, that there is nobody out there worth the trouble.

You love them. You do not want to pry. So you pass the potatoes and say nothing.

Tonight I am going to say something.

I want to say it the way you would across a kitchen table. No podium voice. No slogans. Just a couple of honest hours about something that is happening in nearly every family I know, and probably in yours.

Because that empty chair is not one family's private sorrow. It sits in millions of homes. It is one of the largest changes in the way Americans live that has ever happened in peacetime, and it has happened so quietly, so politely, one family at a time, that as a nation we have barely noticed.

We talk endlessly about the falling birth rate. We argue about whether it matters, whose fault it is, whether the government should pay people to have babies. But we skip the step that comes before the baby.

[SLOW]

Before there is a child, there is a couple.

And before there is a couple, there is a meeting. A glance. A first awkward dinner. A second one that goes better. A drive home with the radio on and neither person wanting the night to end.

That is the step that is breaking. America is not only having fewer children. America is forming fewer couples. Fewer marriages. Fewer partnerships of any kind. Fewer dates. Even, the surveys tell us, less sex. And the decline is uneven. It has opened a gap between our young men and our young women, in schooling, in politics, in hope, so wide that in some places they seem to live in two different countries.

[LET IT LAND]

I call it the unmarried nation. I do not mean that as an insult. Many of the finest people I know are single, some by choice and some by circumstance, and there is nothing shameful in either. A good single life is a good life.

But a country is not one life. A country is the sum of millions of choices and chances. When all of them begin to fall in the same direction at the same time, a country has to ask why.

So here is the road we will travel tonight, and I will tell you each time we reach a new stretch of it.

First, what is actually happening, with numbers I have checked and sources you can check too. Second, why it is happening, and where the evidence is strong and where it is only suggestive. Third, the strongest argument against everything I am about to say, answered fairly. And fourth, what we can do about it, and what I am going to ask of you.

I believe this is a problem a free people can solve. Not by force. Not by shame. By rebuilding the ground on which love grows.

[PAUSE]

It will take a while. Pour yourself something warm. Let us begin.

## II. The River at the Edge of the World

Before the numbers, a story more than two thousand years old.

In the year three hundred and twenty-six before Christ, Alexander of Macedon stood on the bank of a river in the Punjab, in what is now northern India. The Greeks called it the Hyphasis. Today it is the Beas.

He had been marching for eight years. His army had crossed the Hellespont, conquered Asia Minor, taken Egypt, broken the Persian Empire, and climbed over the Hindu Kush. They had fought in heat that killed horses and through rains that rotted their clothes and their boots. They had won every battle they fought.

On the far side of the river, the scouts said, lay rich land, good farmers, brave fighters, and more elephants than anyone had ever seen. Alexander wanted to cross.

[PAUSE]

His men did not.

The ancient historian Arrian tells us the soldiers gathered in little knots around the camp. The moderate ones grumbled about their lot. The bolder ones said they would go no further, even if the king himself led the way.

So Alexander called his officers together and gave one of the great rallying speeches of the ancient world.

He reminded them of everything they had already crossed. He named the lands one by one. Ionia. The Hellespont. Phrygia. Lydia. Egypt. Babylon. Persia. Bactria. The great rivers of India. After all of that, he asked, why would they shrink from one more river?

He told them that for a brave man there is no end to labour except the labour itself, if it leads to something glorious. And he told them, and this is the part I love, that the labours were common to all of them. He was not sitting safe in a tent while they bled. They shared the danger, and they would share the reward.

It is a magnificent speech. Read it and you will want to pick up a spear.

And then there was silence.

A long silence, Arrian says. The officers did not dare argue with the king, and they did not want to agree with him either. Alexander asked again and again for anyone who thought differently to speak. Nothing.

Finally an old general named Coenus stood up. He had fought in every battle. Nobody could call him a coward. And he said, in effect: Majesty, look how few of us are left. Many died in battle. More died of disease. The rest are worn out in body, and more worn out in spirit.

Then he said the thing that turned the whole army.

[SLOW]

The men who still had living parents, he said, yearned to see them once more. They yearned for their wives and their children. They yearned for their native land.

When Coenus finished, the officers applauded, and many of them wept.

Alexander was furious. He went into his tent and would not come out for three days, waiting for the army to change its mind. It did not. The man no enemy had ever defeated was turned back by his own men's longing for home.

When he told them they would return, Arrian says, the soldiers shouted and cried for joy. Some came to his tent to bless him, because he had let himself be conquered by them alone.

He built twelve great altars on that riverbank. Then he turned around.

[LET IT LAND]

My friends, I have thought about that story a great deal. Because in a way I am trying to do what Alexander did. I want to rally a tired people. I want to ask a generation that is exhausted, anxious, broke and lonely to cross one more river.

But I have come to believe that Coenus had the better argument.

What moved those soldiers was not glory, or gold, or elephants. It was a picture in their minds. A door opening. A wife. Children running down a path. A mother's face. They had marched to the edge of the known world, and what they wanted at the end of it was someone to come home to.

That has not changed in two thousand three hundred years.

And here is the cruel twist of our moment. Many of our young people have marched a long way too. They crossed every river we pointed them toward. They studied hard and took the tests. They took on the loans. They moved to the cities where the jobs were. They worked the long hours and kept their heads down. They did everything we asked.

And when they reach the end of that march and look around for the home they were supposed to be building, too many of them find nobody there.

[PAUSE]

So the river I am asking us to cross tonight is not a river of conquest. It is the river between two people. A young man and a young woman, both lonely, both wary, both staring at screens in apartments a few blocks apart. That is the Hyphasis of our time. It is narrower than any river Alexander ever saw, and somehow it has become harder to cross.

Hold on to that river. And hold on to Coenus. We will come back to both of them before the night is over.

Now, the first stretch of our road: what is actually happening.

## III. What the Numbers Say

I promised you numbers I had checked. Let me lay them out the way Franklin Roosevelt laid out the banking system in his first fireside chat: plainly, one step at a time, because you are perfectly capable of following them. I will give you one or two at a time, and then tell you what they mean.

Start with the long view.

In nineteen sixty, according to the Pew Research Center, seventy-two percent of American adults were married.

By twenty-ten, it was fifty-one percent. Barely half.

Among young adults the change was far sharper. In nineteen sixty, fifty-nine percent of Americans between eighteen and twenty-nine were married. By twenty-ten, twenty percent.

[PAUSE]

In your grandparents' generation, most people in their twenties were married. In your children's generation, four out of five are not.

Some of that is simple delay. In nineteen sixty, the typical woman married for the first time at about twenty, and the typical man at about twenty-three. By the time of a twenty-eleven Pew report, it was twenty-six and a half for women and nearly twenty-nine for men. It has kept rising since.

Delay by itself is not a catastrophe. A couple who marry at twenty-eight instead of twenty-two may be more mature, more stable, more likely to last. I am not here to romanticize teenage weddings.

But delay has a way of turning into absence.

[SLOW]

In twenty twenty-three, Pew published a short report that should have stopped the country in its tracks. In twenty twenty-one, it found, a record twenty-five percent of forty-year-old Americans had never been married.

One in four.

In twenty-ten, it was twenty percent. In nineteen eighty, six percent. Pew's researchers looked back through more than a century of census data. The old record had stood since nineteen ten, at sixteen percent. We have not just broken it. We have left it far behind.

And people who have not married by forty mostly do not marry later. Of those who were still unmarried at forty in two thousand one, Pew found, only about one in four had married by sixty.

Let me ask you a question, and you do not have to answer it out loud. Think of the forty-year-olds you know. Your nephew. Your colleague. The woman next door. How many of them are on their own? A generation ago, you would have had to think hard to name one. Today you can probably name several.

Now someone will say: people are living together without marrying. Cohabitation has replaced marriage. The ring is just a piece of paper.

That is partly true, and worth taking seriously. So let us look.

In twenty twenty-one, Pew examined Americans in their prime years, twenty-five to fifty-four, and asked a broader question. Not whether they were married, but whether they were partnered at all.

In nineteen ninety, sixty-seven percent of that age group were married. Four percent were living with a partner.

By twenty nineteen, married had fallen to fifty-three percent. Living together had risen to nine.

Do the arithmetic. Cohabitation went up five points. Marriage went down fourteen. Living together did not fill the hole. It filled about a third of it.

So the share of prime-age adults with no spouse and no partner rose from twenty-nine percent to thirty-eight.

[LET IT LAND]

Nearly four in ten Americans, in the years when people have always built families, are building them with nobody.

And here is a detail that matters for everything else tonight. In nineteen ninety, men and women in that age group were equally likely to be on their own: twenty-nine percent each. By twenty nineteen, it was thirty-nine percent of men and thirty-six percent of women. The men have fallen further.

Now let me be fair about the latest numbers, because this is not a straight line down.

The CDC counts marriages every year. In twenty twenty-two the marriage rate was six point two per thousand Americans. In twenty twenty-three, six point one. In twenty twenty-four, the latest provisional figure, six point three. That is a little over two point one million weddings.

So the marriage rate has steadied since the pandemic. Weddings that were postponed in twenty twenty have happened. That is good news, and I will not pretend otherwise.

But steadying at a low level is not the same as recovering. The wedding halls are busy again. The share of adults who will never walk into one is still at a record high.

One more thing about who is marrying, because it tells us something important.

Among those forty-year-olds, the share who had never married was about one in three for people with a high school diploma or less. For people with a bachelor's degree or more, it was eighteen percent.

Marriage in America has become, in part, a class marker. It is holding up among the college-educated and collapsing among working people. The families who could most use two incomes and two parents are the least likely to have them.

And look at the men in that prime-age study. Ninety-one percent of partnered men had jobs. Among unpartnered men, seventy-three percent.

The partnered men had median earnings of fifty-seven thousand dollars. The unpartnered men, thirty-five thousand six hundred.

And thirty-one percent of unpartnered men were living with their parents. Among partnered men, two percent.

[SLOW]

Those numbers do not tell us which way the arrow points. Does a good job make a man more likely to marry, or does marriage make a man work harder and earn more? Probably both. A steady job makes a man a better prospect. A wife and child give a man a reason to get up in the morning.

But whichever way the arrow points, the picture is the same. There is a large and growing group of American men who are not working full time, not earning much, living in their childhood bedrooms, and partnered with no one. And there is a large and growing group of American women who look at those men and decide, quite reasonably, that they would rather be alone.

Neither side is wicked. The market they are meeting in is broken, and broken markets can be fixed.

But before I talk about fixing it, I need to tell you why it matters. Some people will hear all this and shrug. People marry less. So what?

Here is so what.

## IV. Fewer Couples, Fewer Children

There is a simple fact at the center of this subject, so obvious that experts sometimes forget to say it out loud.

[SLOW]

Babies come from couples.

Not from governments. Not from tax credits. Not from think-tank reports or presidential speeches. A child begins when two people trust each other enough to make a life together, even for a night, and in the overwhelming majority of cases for a great deal longer than a night.

So when the number of couples falls, the number of children falls with it. You can offer all the baby bonuses in the world. If there is nobody to have the baby with, the bonus sits unclaimed.

Here is how that shows up in the American numbers.

In April of this year, the National Center for Health Statistics published its provisional count of births for twenty twenty-five. About three million six hundred and six thousand babies were born in the United States last year. That was about one percent fewer than the year before.

The general fertility rate, which counts births per thousand women of childbearing age, fell from fifty-three point eight to fifty-three point one. The birth rate for teenagers fell seven percent, to the lowest level ever recorded.

The drop in teen births is good news. Nobody here wants fifteen-year-olds raising babies. Some of the decline in American fertility is exactly the decline we wanted, and we should say so.

But the decline does not stop with teenagers. It runs through women in their early twenties and their late twenties, the years when, for most of human history, most families began. By the best international estimates, the American total fertility rate is now about one point six children per woman.

A population replaces itself at about two point one. We are well below that line, and we have been for well over a decade.

So where are the missing children?

[PAUSE]

Here is where the couples come in.

In twenty twenty-four, the CDC tells us, thirty-nine point five percent of American births were to unmarried mothers. Turn that around. Roughly three in every five American babies were born to married parents.

Now picture a country where three in five babies are born inside marriage, and the share of young adults who are married falls from well over half to one in five. You do not need a doctorate in demography to see the result. You are emptying the room where most of the babies are made.

Among the births outside marriage, a large share are to couples living together. Children born outside any partnership at all are a smaller slice still.

Let me put a face on it. This man is a composite, not a real person, but you have met him.

Call him Mark. He is forty-four. He coaches his nephew's Little League team every spring, and he is the best coach on the field. He knows every kid's name and every kid's batting average. At the end-of-season pizza party, one of the mothers thanks him and asks, kindly, whether he has kids of his own. He says no, not yet, and laughs, and goes back to handing out trophies. In the parking lot afterward, he sits in his car for a while before he starts the engine.

Mark is a number in a Pew study.

Among men aged twenty-five to fifty-four who were married or living with a partner, sixty-one percent were living with a child of their own. Among men the same age with no partner, eight percent.

[LET IT LAND]

Sixty-one percent. Eight percent.

That is the whole argument in two numbers. When a man has a partner, he usually has children in his home. When he does not, he almost never does.

For women the gap is smaller, because single mothers are far more common than single fathers. Thirty-two percent of unpartnered women were living with their own child, against sixty percent of partnered women. But the direction is the same.

Some demographers go further. They argue that most of the fall in births across the developed world is not married couples having fewer children. Married couples, they say, still want and still have something close to two. The fall comes from fewer people becoming married couples at all.

I have not been able to pin down a precise figure for how much of the American decline that explains, and I will not invent one for you. Researchers slice it different ways. But here is my own judgment, and you can weigh it. When the share of people in partnerships collapses, and partnership is where nearly all children are raised, the collapse in coupling must be a large part of the collapse in births. Perhaps the largest part.

And that changes how we should think about solutions.

For years, the debate about birth rates has been a debate about money. Cheaper childcare. A bigger child tax credit. Longer parental leave. I support many of those ideas, and I will come back to them.

But notice what they assume. They assume there is already a couple at a kitchen table, doing the math on a second or third child. They are policies for people who have already crossed the river.

What about the people still standing on the bank? The thirty-year-old man who has not been on a date in two years? The thirty-four-year-old woman who has given up on the apps and decided to freeze her eggs and hope? No child tax credit reaches them. They have no child, and no one to have one with.

If we want more children in America, and I believe a nation confident in its future does, we have to start one step earlier.

[SLOW]

Before there is a child, there is a couple.

One more thing before I go further. I am not saying everyone must marry, or that every couple must have children. Some people do not want children. Some cannot have them. Some of the most generous lives I know belong to people who never had children of their own and poured themselves into nieces and nephews, students and neighbors. Mark, the coach, is one of them, and his nephew will remember him all his life.

What I am saying is this. In my experience, most young Americans, once you sit with them long enough for the jokes to run out, still hope to find a partner. Most still hope, someday, for children. The gap is not mainly between what people want and what the government wants. It is between what people want for themselves and what they are actually able to build.

When a whole generation is falling short of its own hopes, that is no longer a private matter. It is a public emergency, disguised as a thousand private disappointments.

[PAUSE]

A word of history, because honesty requires it.

People sometimes talk as if the American family of the nineteen fifties, married at twenty, three or four children, a house in the suburbs, was the natural state of humankind, and everything since has been a fall from grace. That is wrong. The years after the Second World War were unusual. Young people who had lived through a depression and a war married early and had children in a rush. The baby boom was a historical exception, not the rule.

So I am not asking us to go back to nineteen fifty-five. We could not if we tried. And much of what that era demanded of women, and of anyone who did not fit its mold, we should not want back.

But notice something about that exception. It came right after a generation had endured enormous hardship, far harder than anything our young people face. And when the hardship lifted, those young people did not retreat into themselves. They reached for each other. They built families on very little, in small houses, with one car if they were lucky.

What made the difference was not wealth. They were poorer than we are. It was something closer to confidence. A belief that the future was worth building, and that you did not have to build it alone.

That confidence is what we have lost. I think we can recover it.

There is one more reason this matters, beyond the count of children. It matters for the children who are born.

The economist Melissa Kearney, whose work I will return to, has made the case that children raised by two parents have real advantages on average, in resources, in time, in stability, and that the decline of marriage has fallen hardest on families who were already struggling. That is no judgment on single parents, many of whom do heroic work every day against long odds. It is a plain recognition that raising a child is the hardest job there is, and it is easier with two people sharing the load.

So the unmarried nation is not only a nation with fewer children. More and more, it is a nation where the children we do have are raised with fewer hands to hold them.

Now let us look at what is going wrong, starting with the most intimate place of all.

## V. The Quiet Recession

There is an awkward subject we have to discuss, and I will do it as gracefully as an old fireside allows.

A few years ago, writers began using a phrase that made people laugh, and then stop laughing: the sex recession.

The generation everyone assumed must be the most sexually active in history, raised with dating apps and the internet and every kind of freedom, was in fact having less sex than its parents and grandparents.

At first many people waved it away. Young people are just more honest on surveys, they said. Or pickier. Or waiting longer.

Then the careful researchers looked.

In twenty twenty, a team led by Peter Ueda published a study in the medical journal JAMA Network Open. They used the General Social Survey, one of the longest-running and most respected surveys of American life, and asked a simple question. How many young adults reported no sex at all in the past year?

Among men aged eighteen to twenty-four, in the years two thousand to two thousand two, it was about nineteen percent.

By twenty sixteen to twenty eighteen, it was about thirty-one percent.

[SLOW]

Nearly one young man in three had gone a whole year without a single sexual partner.

Among young women the share also rose, from about fifteen percent to about nineteen. But the researchers found that change was not statistically significant. The big shift was among the men.

They found something else, which takes us back to that Pew data on work. Among men, being out of work or working only part-time went with higher odds of having no sex. So did low income. A man earning under ten thousand dollars a year was much more likely to be sexually inactive than a man earning fifty thousand or more. Among women, income and work made little difference.

[PAUSE]

I can already hear the objection, and it is fair. Is this really a problem? Plenty of people, many religious people among them, would say young people having less casual sex is a good thing. Fewer unplanned pregnancies. Fewer broken hearts. Fewer diseases. Teen births at record lows.

I take that seriously. If the sex recession meant young people were waiting for committed, loving relationships before becoming intimate, I would cheer it from this stage.

But that is not what the data show. The fall in sex is happening alongside a fall in relationships. Young people are not trading hookups for marriages. They are losing both.

Look at Pew's numbers on singles. In early twenty twenty-three, Pew found that sixty-three percent of American men under thirty called themselves single.

Among women the same age, thirty-four percent.

[LET IT LAND]

Nearly two in three young men. One in three young women.

How can that be, if there are roughly as many young men as young women?

Part of the answer is that young women often date men a few years older, so a twenty-four-year-old woman's boyfriend may be twenty-nine, or thirty-two, and outside that group. Part of it is that a smaller number of men may be dating more than one woman, while a larger number date no one. And part of it may be that men and women describe the same situation differently. A woman who has been seeing someone for a month may say she is in a relationship. He may say he is single.

However you explain it, the human reality is the same. A very large number of young men are alone.

And here is what worried me most in that report. Not how many are single. How many have stopped looking.

In twenty nineteen, sixty-one percent of single men said they were looking for a relationship or dates. By twenty twenty-two, fifty percent.

Among single women, the share looking slipped too, from thirty-eight percent to thirty-five.

Across all singles, the share looking at all fell from forty-nine percent to forty-two. In three years.

[SLOW]

My friends, that is what giving up looks like in a survey.

Remember Alexander's camp on the bank of the Hyphasis. Arrian tells us he called his council because he wanted to act before the mood in the camp grew any worse. The danger was not the enemy across the river. It was the mood on this side of it. The little knots of men grumbling. The silence when he asked for someone to speak.

That is where we are. Our trouble is on this side of the river too. A generation disappointed often enough that it has decided disappointment is safer than hope.

And I understand that. Rejection hurts. Being ignored hurts more. A date with someone who looks nothing like their photo, or spends the evening on their phone, or never texts back, is exhausting. After enough of that, staying home with a screen and a streaming service starts to feel like self-respect.

Let me give you a face for that number too. She is a composite, not a real person.

Call her Sarah. She is twenty-seven. Last New Year's Eve she sat on her bathroom floor and deleted every dating app on her phone, one by one, and felt lighter than she had in months. She has a good job, a book club, a sister she talks to every day. She tells people she is taking a break. That was ten months ago. The break has quietly become the plan. On Sunday nights, when the week ahead looks long, she sometimes opens the app store, types in a name, and closes it again before it loads.

Sarah has not given up on love. She has given up on the machinery. And right now, for most young people, the machinery is the only door they can see.

[PAUSE]

So let me say something gently to anyone listening who has stopped looking.

The loneliness you feel is not proof that something is wrong with you. Millions of people your age feel it too. You are not failing at a game everyone else is winning. Most people are losing at it, and almost everyone is pretending otherwise.

The system you have been trying to find love inside is badly designed. It was never built to help you find a partner. It was built for something else.

That is the first stretch of our road: what is happening. Fewer marriages, fewer partners, fewer people even looking, and the men falling furthest.

Now the second stretch, the longest: why. It begins with the machine in your pocket that promised to find you love.

## VI. The Marketplace of Faces

For most of human history, here is how people met the person they married.

Through family. Through church or synagogue or mosque. Through school. Through work. Through a friend who said, my cousin is moving to town, you should meet her. At dances, at county fairs, at the neighbor's barbecue.

All of those ways had one thing in common. They happened inside a web of people who knew you. Your reputation came with you. If a young man treated a young woman badly, her brother knew, her friends knew, the pastor knew, and his mother heard about it by Sunday.

That web could be stifling. It enforced rules that were often unfair, especially to women and to people who did not fit in. I do not want all of it back.

But it did something important. It turned strangers into acquaintances before they became partners. It vouched for people.

Then, in the space of about a decade, much of that changed.

Pew reported in twenty twenty-three that three in ten American adults have used a dating site or app. Among adults under thirty, more than half: fifty-three percent.

About one in ten partnered Americans met their partner online. Among partnered adults under thirty, about one in five.

I want to be fair to the apps. For a lot of people they have been a gift. They have helped people in small towns, gay and lesbian Americans who could not easily find each other, divorced parents with no time for bars, the shy, the newly arrived. Real marriages, many of them happy, began with a swipe. I know some of those couples, and so do you.

But listen to what the people who use the apps say about them.

Among recent users in that same Pew study, fifty-four percent of women said they felt overwhelmed by the number of messages they got. Only twenty-five percent of men said the same.

And sixty-four percent of men said they felt insecure about how few messages they got. Among women, forty percent.

[SLOW]

Women drowning. Men starving. In the same pool, at the same time.

[LET IT LAND]

That is a marketplace that produces exhaustion on one side and humiliation on the other.

Here is how I have come to understand it. When you meet someone through a friend, or at church, or at work, you meet one person at a time, in three dimensions, with a voice and a laugh and a way of holding a coffee cup. You see them be kind to a waiter. You see them in a bad mood and a good one.

On an app, you meet a hundred people at once, in two dimensions, as a photograph and a few lines of text. You judge them in a fraction of a second, and they judge you. And because the choice seems endless, every single choice seems cheap.

A woman with a hundred messages has no reason to give any one man the benefit of the doubt. There are ninety-nine more. So she filters hard, on height, on looks, on income, on a single photo, because she has to filter somehow.

A man with no messages has every reason to despair. So he swipes on everyone. That makes his attention worthless to the women receiving it. So they filter harder. So he gets even fewer replies.

Nobody in that loop is a villain. She is not shallow. He is not entitled. Both are responding sensibly to a machine that rewards the wrong behavior.

And the machine has a business model. I am not accusing anyone of a conspiracy. But a dating company earns money when people keep using its product. A couple who meet, fall in love and delete the app forever are a success story and a lost customer at the same moment. That does not mean the companies want you single. It means their incentives and yours do not line up perfectly.

[PAUSE]

Let me paint you a picture. It is a composite, built from the kinds of stories so many families tell.

A young woman in nineteen fifty-something goes to a church dance with her sister. She does not want to go. Her shoes pinch. A young man just back from the service is standing by the punch bowl, and his friend dares him to ask the shy girl in the blue dress to dance. He does, badly. He steps on her foot. She laughs. He asks if he can walk her home. Her sister walks three steps behind them the whole way, which is the first of many tests he will have to pass.

By the next summer they are married. Her father has asked around about him. His mother has met her at church. Half the town knows them both. When they fight, as every couple does, there are a dozen people who know them well enough to say: you two belong together. Work it out.

Now picture the same two people today. She scrolls past his photo in under a second because he is not smiling in it. He sends the same message to her and forty other women on the same night, because that is what the advice videos tell him to do. Neither knows a single person who knows the other. If they do meet, and it goes badly once, there is no sister, no pastor, no mother, no town to nudge them toward a second chance. There is only the next face on the screen.

[SLOW]

Same two people. Same hearts. Completely different odds.

There are signs that young people have noticed. In twenty twenty-five, Fortune reported that Match Group, the company behind Tinder and Hinge and many other apps, was cutting about thirteen percent of its workforce amid weak demand from younger users. You hear more and more about running clubs, supper clubs and in-person singles nights filling up, and about young people who say they would rather meet someone in real life.

That is hopeful. The generation that grew up inside the marketplace of faces is starting to look for the door.

But here is my worry. Take away the app and send a young person out to meet someone the old-fashioned way. Where, exactly, do they go?

The church they never attended? The bowling league that closed? The friends who are all at home on their own phones? The office that went remote?

We did not only change how people meet. We tore down most of the places where they used to meet, and the apps moved into the empty lot.

And the reason so many of those places emptied is a small glass rectangle that nearly every one of us is carrying right now.

## VII. Alone Together

Take your phone out of your pocket for a moment. Or, if you were sensible and left it in the car, just picture it.

[PAUSE]

It is an astonishing object. It holds nearly every book ever written, every song ever recorded, a map of the whole world, a camera better than the ones professionals used when I was young, and a direct line to nearly every human being on earth.

And somehow, in the years since it arrived in every pocket, we have grown lonelier.

The social psychologist Jonathan Haidt published a book in twenty twenty-four called The Anxious Generation. His argument, briefly: between about twenty-ten and twenty-fifteen, childhood in the developed world was rewired. Teenagers moved from a childhood built around play, in person, with friends and siblings and neighbors, to a childhood built around a phone. He argues that this helps explain the sharp rise in anxiety, depression and self-harm among young people that began right around then, in many countries at once.

That argument is contested, and you should know it. Serious researchers, the psychologist Candice Odgers among them, writing in the journal Nature, argue that the evidence does not show social media causing large harms on its own, and that cause and effect may partly run the other way. Anxious kids may reach for phones, rather than phones making kids anxious. The debate is not settled, and anyone who tells you it is settled is selling something.

But you do not need to settle the mental health debate to see what phones have done to time. A day has twenty-four hours. If several of them go to a screen, they come from somewhere. From sleep, from homework, from family dinner, and above all from time face to face with other people.

[SLOW]

And face to face is where love begins.

Consider friendship, the soil romance usually grows in. In twenty twenty-one, the Survey Center on American Life asked Americans how many close friends they had, and compared the answers with a similar survey from nineteen ninety.

In nineteen ninety, three percent of Americans said they had no close friends at all. By twenty twenty-one, twelve percent.

Back then, a third of Americans, thirty-three percent, said they had ten or more close friends. By twenty twenty-one, thirteen percent.

[LET IT LAND]

The share of Americans with no close friends quadrupled in thirty years.

Now think about how couples have always formed. Very often, through friends. Your friend's roommate. Your friend's sister. The guy your friend brought to the party. A wide circle of friends is a wide circle of possible introductions. A narrow circle is a narrow chance.

When friendship thins, romance thins with it.

There is something else the phone does, and it is subtler. It makes being alone comfortable.

Loneliness used to hurt in a way that pushed you out the door. A young man alone in a rented room on a Saturday night, with a radio and four walls, felt the walls closing in. So he went out. To the dance hall, the diner, the church social. Not because he was brave, but because staying home was unbearable.

Today the same young man has a phone that will entertain him until four in the morning. Games that never end. Videos that never stop. Pornography, endless and free, which I will mention only once and plainly, because many parents, and many young men themselves, worry that it puts a screen where a person should be. The phone does not cure his loneliness. It numbs it just enough that he never has to do the frightening thing that would cure it.

[SLOW]

The phone is a painkiller. Like many painkillers, it treats the symptom and leaves the disease alone.

For many young women the pattern looks different. The phone is less a source of games than a source of comparison: a constant parade of other people's beautiful lives, perfect relationships and flawless faces, and a constant stream of messages from strangers. Some flattering. Some threatening. Many just tiring. It is no surprise that a young woman who spends her evenings fending off strangers online would rather not spend her weekend meeting more of them in person.

Let me tell you about something I have seen in more than one restaurant. Maybe you have seen it too.

A young couple sit across from each other at dinner. They are clearly on a date. They have dressed up a little. And for long minutes at a time, both of them are looking down at their phones.

They are not being rude, exactly. They have never known a world where you did not do that. The phone sits on the table like a third guest, and it is the most interesting one there.

Now picture that same pair seventy years ago. Nothing on the table but a candle and a salt shaker. They have no choice but to look at each other. For two hours they are forced into the terrifying and wonderful work of paying attention to another human being.

[PAUSE]

Love, at bottom, is a kind of attention. And we have built a machine that is very, very good at taking attention away.

I am not proposing we smash our phones. I could not live without mine, and I suspect you could not either. But we should stop pretending the phone is neutral. It is the most powerful device ever invented for keeping people apart while making them feel connected.

There are things we can do, and I will come to them. Haidt himself proposes some: keep phones out of schools for the whole school day, give children basic phones before smartphones, raise the age at which kids can open social media accounts, and let children play freely in the real world again. Many schools and states have moved in that direction in the last two years.

Those are good ideas for children. Adults need a version too. We need to rebuild a social life that does not run through a screen.

Because the phone has done one more thing, and it may be the most dangerous of all. It has sorted our young men and our young women into separate worlds, with separate feeds, separate heroes, separate villains and separate truths.

Which brings us to the gap.

## VIII. Two Countries in One Generation

This next part is uncomfortable to discuss in public, because whatever you say about it, somebody hears an insult. So let me start with a promise.

I am not going to blame young women for doing well. I am not going to blame young men for doing badly. Both are cheap, and both are wrong. I want to describe a gap that has opened between them, and ask what it means for a country that depends, as every country always has, on young men and young women finding each other.

Start with school.

In the fall of twenty twenty-one, according to the National Center for Education Statistics, fifty-eight percent of American undergraduates were women. Forty-two percent were men.

In raw numbers, about eight point nine million women and six point five million men.

In my parents' day the gap ran the other way. Men far outnumbered women on campus. Opening higher education to women was one of the finest achievements of the last century, and every father of a daughter here should be grateful for it. I certainly am.

But the pendulum did not stop in the middle. It swung past. Today there are nearly four women in college for every three men.

[PAUSE]

Now ask what that means for marriage.

For most of history, women have tended to marry men with at least as much education and income as they had. You may think that preference is old-fashioned. You may think it will fade. So far, it seems to be fading only slowly. Many educated women still want a partner who matches them.

Put those facts together. A growing pool of educated women. A shrinking pool of educated men. A preference that has not caught up. The arithmetic is unforgiving. A great many women who did everything right, who studied and worked and built careers, will look around in their thirties and find that the men they would have chosen are taken, and the men who remain are men they have trouble imagining a future with.

Now look from the other side. Remember the men Pew described: lower employment, lower earnings, three in ten living with their parents. Picture a young man without a degree, in a town where the factory closed, working part-time at a warehouse, with no clear path up. He is not a villain. He is not lazy. The economy and the school system have offered him very little, and every time he opens a dating app he sees exactly how little he looks like on paper.

What does that man have to offer? He has the things that have always mattered most: loyalty, humor, strength, a willingness to show up. But those do not fit in a profile. And he may have absorbed the message, from a hundred directions, that he is not needed.

[SLOW]

A nation that tells its young men they are not needed should not be surprised when they stop showing up.

The writer and scholar Richard Reeves has spent years making this argument, in his book Of Boys and Men. He points out that boys fall behind girls in school from an early age, that their brains in some respects tend to mature later, that they are more likely to be suspended or diagnosed with learning or behavioral difficulties, and less likely to graduate. He has proposed letting boys start school a year later, recruiting more male teachers, and investing far more in vocational and technical education. You need not agree with every proposal to see that he is describing something real.

Let me make it personal.

Think of two cousins. I will call them Jake and Emma. They are not real people, but you will recognize them. They grew up three houses apart and went to the same elementary school. Emma was always the better student. Not because Jake was dim, but because she could sit still, do the homework and please the teacher, and he could not. Not yet.

Emma went to the state university, then to a master's program in the city. She works in health care administration, earns a good salary, and lives in an apartment with two roommates and a cat. She has been on perhaps sixty first dates in five years. Most went nowhere. Many of the men, she says, seemed younger than her. Not in age. In seriousness.

Jake did a semester at community college, did not like it, and left. He works at a distribution center, on and off. He lives in his old bedroom. He spends his evenings gaming with friends he has never met in person. He tried the apps for a while, got almost no matches, and quit. He tells his friends he is fine.

He is not fine.

[PAUSE]

Now let me be fair to Emma, because her story is easy to tell carelessly.

The answer is not for women like Emma to lower their standards. Most of her standards are not shallow at all. She wants a man who will carry his share of the housework and the child care, and she has watched friends marry men who did not. She wants a man who is emotionally steady, who can talk about a hard day without shutting down or blowing up. She wants a man who respects her ambitions instead of resenting them. Those are exactly the things a good marriage needs.

So Emma does not want too much. We have done far too little to raise young men who can offer it.

We told our daughters they could be anything, and we were right. We forgot to tell our sons what they were for. We gave girls a new story and left boys with an old one that no longer fit the world. Then we were surprised when the two stories would not fit together.

[SLOW]

So before the gender gap is anything else, it is a failure of the adults. It is our job, as parents and teachers and mentors, to give young men a picture of manhood that is strong and gentle at once, ambitious and faithful, confident and kind. A picture young women would actually want to build a life with.

And here is the thing that breaks my heart. If Emma and Jake were strangers instead of cousins, they would not even appear on each other's screens. Their worlds no longer overlap. Different schools, different jobs, different neighborhoods, different feeds.

And more and more, different politics.

## IX. The Ballot and the Bedroom

Here is a number that would have surprised almost any political scientist twenty-five years ago.

In twenty twenty-four, Gallup published an analysis of how young Americans, eighteen to twenty-nine, describe their own politics. It averaged its surveys over three stretches of years.

From two thousand one to two thousand seven, twenty-eight percent of young women called themselves liberal. Among young men, twenty-five percent. A gap of three points. Barely noticeable.

From two thousand eight to twenty sixteen: thirty-two percent of young women, twenty-seven percent of young men. A gap of five.

From twenty seventeen to twenty twenty-four, forty percent of young women called themselves liberal.

Young men? Still twenty-five percent.

[LET IT LAND]

A gap of fifteen points, and growing. Young women moved sharply left. Young men stayed where they were.

Gallup also found that on specific issues, from the environment to abortion to guns, young women had moved substantially and young men less, so the gaps between them widened. On most issues, young women's views now sit much closer to those of older liberals, while young men are split.

I am not going to tell you tonight which side is right. Some of you are liberal and some of you are conservative, and I respect you both, and that is not my argument. My argument is about what this does to coupling.

Politics used to be something couples argued about at the dinner table before they went to bed. Plenty of long marriages have had one Republican and one Democrat in them. Some of the happiest couples I know cancel each other's votes every November and laugh about it.

But something changed when politics stopped being about policy and started being about identity. When a party became a kind of tribe, with its own music, its own news, its own enemies and its own sense of who is good and who is bad. When a vote stopped being a choice and became a verdict on your character.

[PAUSE]

Today many young people say they would not date someone who voted the other way. Whatever the exact share, you have heard it, and you may have said it. Political views go on dating profiles. One hot-button issue can end a conversation before it starts.

Now combine that with the Gallup numbers. If young women are moving left and young men are not, and if both increasingly refuse to date across the line, you have taken a dating market that was already strained and cut a canyon down the middle of it.

Why has the gap opened? Honest scholars disagree, and I will not pretend to settle it. Some point to the different paths men and women have taken through school and work. Some point to the movements of the last decade around sexual harassment and women's rights, which many young women experienced as a long-overdue reckoning and some young men experienced as an accusation aimed at them personally. Some point to the phone again, to algorithms that feed young men one diet of videos and young women another, each designed to provoke, each rewarding anger with attention.

I suspect all of those play a part. Whatever the causes, a young man and a young woman who grew up on the same street can reach adulthood with almost no shared picture of the world. And it is hard to fall in love with someone whose whole picture of the world strikes you as alien.

If you want to see where that road can lead, look at South Korea.

South Korea is one of the great success stories of the last century. A country among the poorest in the world after its war became, within two generations, one of the richest, best-educated and most technologically advanced nations on earth.

And South Korea now has one of the lowest birth rates any nation has ever recorded.

In twenty fifteen, its total fertility rate was about one point two four children per woman.

By twenty eighteen, it had fallen below one.

In twenty twenty-three, it hit about zero point seven two. Less than one child per woman. Each generation roughly a third the size of the one before.

[SLOW]

Alongside that collapse, South Korea has seen one of the sharpest political splits between young men and young women anywhere in the democratic world. Its presidential election of twenty twenty-two was decided by less than one percentage point. Among voters aged eighteen to twenty-nine, the major broadcasters' exit poll found that about fifty-nine percent of young men voted for the conservative candidate, Yoon Suk Yeol.

And about fifty-eight percent of young women voted for his liberal opponent, Lee Jae-myung.

Young men and young women of the same country, the same age, the same schools, voting almost as mirror images of each other.

I am not saying the gender divide in politics caused South Korea's birth rate to collapse. The causes there are many: crushing work hours, brutal competition for schools and jobs, the very high cost of housing in Seoul, and expectations about who does the housework and the child care that many young women find unacceptable. The gender war there is as much a symptom of those pressures as a cause of anything.

But that is exactly my point. When young men and women feel the system is pitting them against each other, when each sees the other as a rival rather than a partner, they stop coming together. And when they stop coming together, the children stop coming.

There is one small piece of hope in the Korean story. In twenty twenty-four, after years of decline, the fertility rate rose slightly, to about zero point seven five, the first increase in nearly a decade. It is far too early to call that a turnaround. But it reminds us these trends are not laws of physics. They are the result of choices, by people and by governments, and choices can change.

[PAUSE]

So here is what I want to say to the young men and young women listening tonight, across whatever political line divides you.

The person on the other side of that line is worried about the same rent, the same student loans, the same uncertain future you are. Underneath all the noise, they want very much what you want. Someone to come home to. Someone who will be glad to see them.

Your political opinions matter. Hold them. Argue for them. Vote for them. But do not let a feed that profits from your anger convince you that half your generation is unworthy of love.

Alexander's army was made of Macedonians and Greeks from many cities, peoples who had fought each other for generations and had every reason for grudges. On the march they became something else. They became people who shared the labour.

That is what we need now. Not agreement. Shared labour.

## X. The Strongest Objection

There is one more cause on our road, and it is the one that hits hardest. But before we reach it, I owe you the third thing I promised.

I have spent a long time making my case. A speech that only makes its own case is a sermon, not an argument. So let me give the other side the best hearing I can, in the strongest form I can find.

[PAUSE]

Here is the objection.

Everything you have described, someone might say, is the result of freedom. And freedom is good.

For most of human history, marriage was not a free choice, especially for women. A woman married because she had no other way to eat, no right to own property, no realistic way to earn a living, no entry to most professions, and in many places no legal standing apart from her father or her husband. She married because the alternative was poverty and disgrace. She stayed married because divorce was nearly impossible and leaving meant ruin.

The old marriage rates, the objection goes, were high because the cage was locked.

Then the cage was opened. In nineteen sixty, the federal government approved the first birth control pill. Women entered colleges and professions in enormous numbers. Laws changed so that a woman could get a credit card, own a business, leave a violent husband. And as soon as women had real choices, many chose to marry later, or less, or not at all.

From this point of view, the unmarried nation is a liberation, not a tragedy. A woman who is single at thirty-five because she has not met a man worth marrying is not a symptom of social collapse. She is a free person making a free choice her great-grandmother never had. And if men are struggling to adapt to a world where women no longer need them to survive, the answer is for men to become better partners, not for society to engineer more marriages.

As for the birth rate, the objection continues, the world has eight billion people. The planet is warming. Fewer children may be a blessing. And how many children a woman has is her business, not the nation's. Every time governments have tried to tell women how many babies to have, from fascist Italy to communist Romania to China's one-child policy, the results have been cruel.

[SLOW]

That is a serious argument. Let me tell you what it gets right, and then what it gets wrong.

It gets right that the freedom of women to choose whether and whom to marry is a precious achievement. I would not trade it for any birth rate. Any answer that pressures women back into dependence, or shames them for their choices, or restricts their rights, is an answer I will never support. Let me say that as plainly as I can. The cure for the unmarried nation is not less freedom.

It gets right that history is full of cruel population policies, and that a free country must never treat its citizens as breeding stock. Every idea I offer tonight widens people's choices. None narrows them.

And it gets right that men have a great deal of growing to do. Many young women who have given up on dating did not give up on a whim. They gave up after being ghosted, insulted, pressured, or simply bored by men who never learned to court a woman with respect. Men who want to be chosen have to become worth choosing. I will say more about that.

But here is what the objection gets wrong.

It assumes we are watching people freely choose the lives they want. For some people that is exactly true. For many others, the evidence tells a different story.

Remember the forty-year-olds. One in four never married, a record. Did a quarter of Americans set out at twenty-two to stay single for life? I do not believe it, and I do not think most of them believe it either. When I talk with single people in their thirties and forties, very few tell me this is what they planned. Many tell me, quietly, that it just did not happen. They were waiting for the right time, or the right person, or the right job, and the years slipped by.

Remember the single young men in that Pew survey: the share even looking fell from sixty-one percent to fifty in three years. Freedom does not look like that. Resignation does.

And remember the class divide. If falling marriage were simply liberated women choosing independence, you would expect it to be strongest among the most educated and affluent women, the ones with the most options. It is the opposite. Marriage is holding up best among the college-educated and collapsing among working people. The economist Melissa Kearney, in her twenty twenty-three book The Two-Parent Privilege, argues that the decline of marriage has contributed to economic disadvantage in America and has hit the most vulnerable families hardest. Children of the affluent are still mostly raised by two parents. Children of the poor increasingly are not.

[LET IT LAND]

The people losing marriage are mostly not the people with the most freedom. They are mostly the people with the least.

And here is the deepest thing the objection misses.

Freedom is not only the absence of a cage. It is also the presence of a path.

A young man is free to start a business, in the sense that no law forbids it. But if he has no capital, no training, no network and no one who believes in him, that freedom is mostly theory. A young woman is free to marry a good man, in the sense that no law forbids it. But if she lives in a town where most young men have left or given up, and she meets the rest through an app that shows them to her as a stream of disappointing photographs, that freedom is mostly theory too.

Real freedom requires real options. For millions of young Americans, the options for building a family have been shrinking.

As for the planet: I care about the environment. But in my judgment, how many children a family has is the wrong lever for climate policy. Cleaner energy and better technology are the right ones. And a country aging fast, with fewer workers supporting more retirees every year, will find it harder, not easier, to afford the investments a cleaner future requires. A society that has stopped believing in its own future rarely plans well for it.

And as for whose business it is, I agree completely. How many children a particular woman has is nobody's business. But whether a nation's people are able to have the families they want is very much the nation's business. When a whole generation falls short of its own hopes, a decent country asks why and clears the obstacles away. We do exactly that for every other kind of hope, from owning a home to going to college to retiring with dignity, and nobody calls it coercion.

[PAUSE]

So I accept the objection's love of freedom. I just think it has not followed that love far enough. A country that truly loves freedom wants its people free to build the lives they dream of. And many of them dream of a family.

Now, the last cause on our road, and the most practical obstacle of all.

## XI. The Money and the House

Whenever I talk with young people about marriage and children, the conversation arrives at money within about five minutes.

I do not want to overstate it. If money were the whole story, rich countries would have the highest birth rates and the highest marriage rates, and they do not. Within America, marriage is stronger among the well-off, but birth rates have fallen even among people who are doing well. And many couples in hard times, in the Depression and after the war, married young and raised families in small houses on very little.

So money is not everything. But it is not nothing. It works on coupling in two ways worth understanding.

The first is what you might call the marriageability problem. Remember the Pew numbers: unpartnered men are much less likely to have jobs, earn much less, and are far more likely to live with their parents. Remember the sex recession study: among men, low income and lack of full-time work went with being sexually inactive.

Like it or not, many women, and many men too, still think of marriage as something you do once you have your footing. A steady job. A place of your own. Some savings. For a lot of Americans, marriage has become a capstone, something you do after you have built your life, instead of a cornerstone, something you build your life upon.

[SLOW]

When a large share of young men cannot reach that footing, when wages for men without degrees have been weak for a generation, they are not seen as ready to marry, and often do not see themselves that way. So they wait. And waiting, as we have seen, has a way of turning into never.

The second way money works on coupling is through the house.

Ask any young couple what stops them from marrying, or having a child, or having a second, and very often the answer is not the wedding or the hospital bill. It is the rent. It is the down payment. It is the discovery that a two-bedroom apartment in the city where the jobs are costs more than one of their paychecks.

And many young people cannot even begin. A young man living with his parents at twenty-eight is not in a strong position to start a romance. A young woman sharing an apartment with two roommates to make the rent has no room for a relationship to grow. Housing is not only where you raise children. It is whether you have a door you can close, a place you can invite someone back to for dinner, a life of your own to share.

[PAUSE]

When we made housing scarce and expensive, through decades of zoning rules, permit delays and resistance to building, we did not only make homes cost more. We made adulthood cost more. And we pushed back the age at which young people feel ready to start one.

There is a third financial problem, and government created it all by itself.

In many of our safety-net programs, a couple can lose benefits by marrying. Two low-income adults living apart may each qualify for help. Married, their incomes are added together, and they may lose health coverage, food assistance, a tax credit or child care help. Economists call these marriage penalties, and they run through parts of our tax code and our benefit programs.

The size of the penalty varies enormously from family to family, and scholars argue about how much it actually changes behavior. But consider the message. We have built a system in which, for some of the poorest Americans, the government charges a fee for getting married.

If you set out to design a policy to discourage marriage among the people who most need its stability, you could hardly do better.

And then there is debt. Millions of young Americans have student loans they began paying in their early twenties and will still be paying in their forties. A young woman with a large balance may hesitate to marry a man with a large balance of his own. A young man with debt may feel he has nothing to offer. Debt never shows up on a dating profile. It shows up in every conversation about the future.

Let me make this concrete with one more composite couple, because numbers alone can feel abstract.

Call them Daniel and Maria. They are twenty-nine. They have been together four years. They love each other, and when friends ask, they both say they will get married someday.

Daniel is a paramedic. Maria teaches second grade. Together they earn a respectable income. But they live in a one-bedroom apartment where the rent has risen sharply since they moved in. Each still carries student loans. They have looked at houses in their area and given up. They have priced child care and laughed, the way you laugh at a joke that is not funny.

So they wait. They will marry, they tell themselves, when they can afford a real wedding, and have a child when they can afford a second bedroom. Each year the second bedroom recedes a little further. Maria starts to hear her friends talk about fertility clinics.

[SLOW]

And one night, at three in the morning, Daniel sits in the back of an ambulance after a call. He has just helped carry somebody's grandfather down three flights of stairs, and the old man's whole family followed them out to the curb. Children and grandchildren in their pajamas, holding on to each other. Daniel wipes down the stretcher. He fills out the form. And he wonders whether anyone will ever stand on a curb in their pajamas for him.

[LET IT LAND]

Nobody is stopping Daniel and Maria from marrying. No law forbids it. But everything about the way we have arranged the economy of young adulthood tells them: not yet. And not yet, said often enough, becomes not ever.

My friends, this is the bottom of the valley. A generation that did everything we asked is being told, by the rent and the loans and the arithmetic, to wait for a life that keeps backing away. If you have felt discouraged listening to me tonight, this is the place to feel it.

So let me sum up what money does. It does not stop people from falling in love. It stops them from feeling ready to act on it. It delays the wedding, the move-in, the first child. And then the second child never comes, because by the time the couple feels ready, they are thirty-eight and tired.

Alexander told his men the labours were common, that he shared the danger and they would share the reward. Our young people have carried a great deal of labour: the loans, the rent, the long hours, the uncertain jobs. Too often, the reward they were promised, a home and a family of their own, has moved further off every time they reached for it.

[PAUSE]

But that is a promise we can still keep. This is where the road turns.

## XII. Rebuilding the Ground

Here is the fourth and last stretch of our road, the one I have been waiting all evening to walk with you: what we can do.

Franklin Roosevelt, in that first fireside chat, did not stop at explaining why the banks had failed. He told people what was being done, and what they could do. So let me do the same. First, what government can do. Then, what only the rest of us can do, which in the end matters more.

I have seven proposals. I will number them as I go, so you can keep count.

First: put phones in their place, starting with children.

The case for keeping smartphones out of classrooms for the whole school day is now strong enough, and the cost low enough, that I believe every school in America should do it. Many already have. Let children spend the school day looking at teachers and at each other. Let lunch be loud again. Let recess be where children learn to negotiate, to lose, to make up, to be brave.

Beyond school, the age at which children can open social media accounts should be raised and actually enforced, and phone makers should give parents simple tools to make that possible. Whatever you think of Haidt's larger claims, giving childhood back to children is a principle parents of every political stripe can share.

[SLOW]

Why does this belong in a speech about marriage? Because the children of today are the couples of twenty years from now. A child who learns to make friends face to face, to read a room, to sit through awkwardness, to take a rejection and try again, is a child who will one day be able to walk across a room and say hello to a stranger. We are not only protecting their mental health. We are protecting their capacity to love.

Second: take the boys' education gap seriously.

We spent decades, rightly, opening every door to girls. Keep every one of those doors open. And now give the same energy to boys who are falling behind. That means more male teachers, especially in the early grades, where a boy may go years without seeing a man at the front of a classroom. It means looking hard at ideas like Richard Reeves's proposal to let boys start school a year later. And above all, it means treating vocational and technical education as a first-class path, not a consolation prize, with real investment, real apprenticeships and real respect.

A young man who learns a skilled trade, as an electrician, a welder, a machinist, a nurse, can earn a good living, stand on his own feet and offer a partner a stable future. That path should be as visible and as honored as the path to a four-year degree.

Third: end the marriage penalties.

No American should lose health coverage, food assistance or a tax credit simply for marrying the person they love. Congress should go through the tax code and the major benefit programs line by line and smooth out the cliffs that punish marriage at the bottom of the income ladder. It will cost money. I believe it is worth every dollar. A government that wants stable families should at least stop charging people to form them.

[PAUSE]

Fourth: build homes.

This is not a partisan idea. Cities run by both parties have made it too hard to build. Let people build more homes, more kinds of homes, closer to the jobs. Reform the zoning rules that forbid anything but large single-family houses on large lots. Speed up permits. Allow the duplex, the townhouse, the apartment over the garage. Every young couple who can afford a place of their own a few years earlier has a few more years to build a family.

Fifth: stand behind families once they form.

I said earlier that the usual pro-family policies are aimed at people who have already crossed the river. That does not make them useless. It makes them the far half of the bridge. A young couple deciding whether to marry and start a family looks ahead. If they see child care that costs as much as a mortgage, parental leave that does not exist and a tax code that does little for parents, they hesitate. If they see a country that will meet them halfway, they step forward. A generous child tax credit, help with child care and paid leave for new parents tell young people that the nation is on their side.

Sixth: rebuild the places where people meet.

This one sounds soft. I believe it may be the most important of all.

For generations, Americans met each other in what sociologists call third places. Not home, not work, but somewhere in between. The church hall. The union hall. The bowling alley. The diner. The library. The park. The community dance. The volunteer fire company. The softball league.

Picture one of them working the way it should. A public library on a Thursday night in some ordinary town. The lights are on late. In one room, a dozen people in their twenties are learning to fix bicycles. In another, a language circle is arguing happily about verbs. A young woman who moved to town in the spring asks the young man next to her how to straighten a bent wheel. He does not really know either. They figure it out together. Nobody swiped. Nobody filtered. Somebody just left the lights on.

That library is not a real place. But it could be your library, next month.

Many of our third places are gone or struggling. Government can help, a little. Keep libraries open in the evenings. Fund parks and recreation leagues for adults, not just children. Make it easy, not hard, to open a café or a music venue or a community space. Support national and community service programs that put young people of different backgrounds side by side, working on something real, as the Civilian Conservation Corps once did in Roosevelt's day.

[SLOW]

Seventh, and last: measure what matters.

Here is a small fact that tells you how little attention we pay. The federal government tracks unemployment every month, inflation every month, retail sales every month. But go to the CDC's own page on marriage and divorce, and you will find that its latest divorce figures cover only forty-five states and the District of Columbia, because not every state reports. We count every barrel of oil and every bushel of corn more carefully than we count the families our country is, or is not, forming.

What a nation measures, it manages. I want a regular, serious national report on how Americans are coupling: how many are dating, partnering, marrying, separating, and why. Not to pry into anyone's private life, but so the people we elect can no longer pretend this is not happening.

So: phones in their place. Boys brought back in. Penalties ended. Homes built. Families backed. Places to meet. And honest numbers.

[PAUSE]

But most of this cannot be done by government at all. It has to be done by us.

## XIII. What Only We Can Do

So now I want to speak to you directly. Not as citizens of a nation, but as parents, grandparents, aunts and uncles, friends, pastors, coaches, bosses and neighbors. And to the young people listening, the ones who will cross this river or not.

To the older generation first.

Stop telling young people to wait.

For thirty years, the advice we have given our children has been some version of this: finish your education, establish your career, see the world, find yourself, and then, someday, think about settling down. We meant well. We wanted them to have chances we never had. But without quite meaning to, we told them that marriage and children are what you do after life has given you everything else, instead of part of what makes life worth living.

[SLOW]

Tell them the truth instead. Tell them the best things in your life were not your promotions. Tell them that marrying young and poor was hard and wonderful and that you would do it again. Or, if your marriage failed, tell them what you learned, and that you still believe in the idea. Tell them children are exhausting and expensive and the greatest joy you have ever known. They will not hear it from their feeds. They have to hear it from you.

And do something even more old-fashioned. Introduce people.

Every community used to have its matchmakers. The aunt who knew everybody. The pastor who noticed that the new schoolteacher and the young mechanic both sat alone at the potluck. The friend who threw a dinner party and quietly seated two single people side by side. That work was never glamorous, but it built more families than any app ever will.

Most of us knew someone like that. Picture her, a composite of a few I have known. Aunt Ruth. She ran the coffee hour after church for thirty years, and she kept a kind of ledger in her head of every unattached person under forty in the parish. She was not subtle. She would hand you a plate of cookies and say, take these over to the young man by the window, he looks hungry. Half the town rolled its eyes at her. A good number of the town's marriages started with her cookies.

Be that person. If you know a good young man and a good young woman who might like each other, say so. Throw the dinner. Make the introduction. Risk being a little embarrassing. The worst that can happen is an awkward evening. The best that can happen is a grandchild you will never know you helped bring into the world.

[PAUSE]

Now, to the young men.

I know it is hard out there. The apps feel rigged, the schools did not fit you, the jobs are uncertain, and the culture sends you mixed messages about whether you are needed at all. I know you hear voices online telling you that women are the enemy, that the game is lost, that you should give up or get angry.

Do not listen to them.

You are needed. Your strength, your loyalty, your steadiness, your humor, your willingness to show up and keep showing up. But you have to bring it into the world, not into a screen. Get off the couch. Get the training. Take the job, even if it is not the one you dreamed of, and work your way up. Join something: a gym, a team, a church, a volunteer crew, a club. Be around people. And when you meet a woman you like, treat her with the respect you would want a man to show your sister. Be kind. Be direct. Be brave enough to ask, and gracious enough to accept a no.

[SLOW]

Women are not a prize you win by gaming a system. They are partners you earn by becoming a man worth partnering with. That is no burden. It is the oldest adventure there is.

And to the young women.

I know you have been disappointed. You have dealt with men who ghosted you, pressured you, belittled you, or simply never grew up. You have worked hard to build a life you are proud of, and you are right not to settle for someone who would tear it down.

But I want to ask you, gently, to keep a door open.

Some of the best men you will ever meet do not have impressive résumés. They may not have a degree. They may be an inch shorter than you would like. They may be awkward in a text message and wonderful at a kitchen table. He may be the quiet guy at work, the friend of a friend, the man in your running club who never quite worked up the nerve. Give him a second date. Look for character over credentials. Look for the man who will show up when your car breaks down at midnight, and when your mother is in the hospital.

And if you find yourself thinking that politics makes a man impossible to love, ask whether it is his values you object to, or the version of him your feed has taught you to expect.

[PAUSE]

And to all the young people, men and women both: put the phone down sometimes. Go where people are. Say yes to the invitation. Host the dinner yourself. Introduce your friends to each other. Ask someone out in person, the frightening, old-fashioned way. You will be rejected sometimes. Everyone always has been. Your grandparents were rejected. They tried again.

[LET IT LAND]

Courage is what love has always required. The apps promised to make it safe, and that promise was the problem. Love was never meant to be safe. It was meant to be worth it.

## XIV. Coenus Was Right

Let me go back, one last time, to Alexander.

I told you that at the Hyphasis he turned around. I did not tell you what he did on the way home. It surprised me when I read it, and I think it belongs at the end of this long evening.

The march back was terrible. Part of the army went through the Gedrosian desert, and many died. But at last they reached the Persian city of Susa. And there, Arrian tells us, Alexander did what the historian called the most popular thing he ever did.

He held a wedding.

Not one wedding. Dozens. Alexander married, and he gave brides to about eighty of his Companions in a single ceremony. Seats were set in a row for the bridegrooms. After the banquet the brides came in, and each sat down beside her husband. Each man took his bride by the right hand and kissed her, the king first. And Alexander gave every one of them a dowry.

Then he went further. He ordered that the names of all the ordinary Macedonian soldiers who had married women in Asia be written down. There were more than ten thousand of them. And he gave every one a wedding gift.

I want to be honest about this story, as I have tried to be honest all night. Those marriages were political. They were meant to bind a conquering army to a conquered empire. And I doubt very much that the brides at Susa were asked what they wanted. That is no model for anything we should do today, and I hold it up for a different reason.

I hold it up because even the greatest conqueror of the ancient world, at the end of the longest march in history, understood something we seem to have forgotten. When the fighting is over, people need homes. They need someone beside them. A leader who wants his people to have a future does not only lead them into battle. He sits them down at a banquet, and he pays for the wedding.

Not long after, the army mutinied again, at a town called Opis, angry that Alexander was sending veterans home and bringing Persians into the ranks. And Alexander gave another speech. He reminded the men what he had done for them. He said he had celebrated their weddings with his own. He said the children of many of them would be kin to his own children. And he reminded them that he had paid off their debts, without asking too closely how they had run them up.

[SLOW]

He paid for the weddings. He forgave the debts. He made the soldiers' children part of his own family.

I am not asking any president to forgive every loan or pay for every wedding. I am asking us to remember that a nation's future is not built on monuments or markets alone. It is built in the small rooms where two people decide to share a life. A wise people, and a wise government, clears the path to those rooms instead of blocking it.

So let me draw it all together.

We are the unmarried nation.

One in four of our forty-year-olds has never married.

Nearly four in ten of our prime-age adults have no spouse or partner at all.

Nearly two-thirds of our young men call themselves single, and nearly a third of the youngest went a whole year without intimacy.

Our young women are moving one way in politics and our young men another. Our young people meet through a marketplace that drowns half of them and starves the other half. Our friendships have thinned. Our gathering places have emptied. Our homes cost too much. And at the end of the line, the births keep falling, year after year.

But hear me now.

None of this was decreed by fate. None of it is written in our genes. All of it was built, decision by decision, over a few decades, by a society that did not notice what it was doing. And what was built can be rebuilt.

[BUILD]

We can give childhood back to our children, and with it the ability to look a stranger in the eye.

We can lift up our boys without pulling down our girls.

We can stop charging the poor to marry.

We can build homes, and let the young have doors of their own.

We can stand behind young families when they form, so the second child is not a luxury.

We can reopen the halls, the parks, the leagues and the dinner tables where people have always found each other.

We can introduce people. We can tell the truth about what makes a life good. We can stop telling our children to wait.

And to our young people, I say what Alexander said to his weary army, because I believe it in my bones.

You have already crossed so much.

You crossed a recession that greeted many of you as you came of age. You crossed a pandemic that stole your proms and your graduations and two years of your youth. You crossed tuition bills and rent bills and a job market that changes faster than anyone can keep up with. You crossed a flood of information and misinformation that no generation before you had to wade through. You have been told from every direction that the world is ending, that the future is closed, that yours is the first generation to do worse than its parents.

And you are still here. Still working. Still hoping, even if you would not admit it out loud.

So I am not asking you to cross a great river tonight. I am asking you to cross a small one. The one between you and the person across the room. The one between your apartment and the dinner party you were invited to. The one between the screen in your hand and the face in front of you.

[SLOW]

And here is where I part ways with Alexander.

He said that for a brave man there is no end to labour except the labour itself. He believed glory was the goal. He wanted to march to the edge of the world.

But Coenus was right.

When the old general stood up in that silent council and spoke of the soldiers' longing for their parents, their wives, their children and their homes, he spoke for every human being who has ever lived. That longing is not weakness, and it is not a lack of ambition. It is the deepest and most honorable desire we have. It is the reason all the other labour is worth doing.

The men at the Hyphasis wept for joy when they were told they could go home.

Our tragedy is that so many of our young people have marched to the edge of the world and found no home to go back to. No one waiting at the door. No children running down the path.

[LET IT LAND]

Let us build that home for them. Let us build it together.

My friends, it is late, and I have kept you a long time. Franklin Roosevelt, ending that first talk with the American people in nineteen thirty-three, told them the problem he had described was theirs as much as his, and that together they could not fail. I will end with something like that, in my own words.

This is not the government's problem alone, and it is not yours alone. It is ours. It sits at every Thanksgiving table with an empty chair. It lives in every apartment where a young man is gaming at three in the morning because he cannot face another empty evening. It lives in every phone where a young woman is scrolling through a hundred faces and seeing nobody. It rides in the back of every ambulance where a young man like Daniel wonders who will ever stand on the curb for him. It lives in every grandparent who wonders, quietly, whether there will ever be a grandchild.

And it can be answered. Not by force. Not by shame. Not by nostalgia for a past that was never as simple as we remember. It can be answered by courage and kindness, by good laws and better habits, by a people deciding together that the most important thing a free country can protect is the freedom to love and be loved, and to raise the next generation in a home of its own.

Because before there is a child, there is a couple. And before there is a couple, there is one brave hello.

I can picture what it looks like when it works. Daniel and Maria in a small second bedroom with a can of paint, arguing about the color. Jake at a trade school graduation, with his mother crying in the third row. Emma at a friend's dinner party, seated next to a man she would never have swiped on, laughing at something he said. Sarah with her phone face down on a café table, because the person across from her is more interesting. Mark at the end of another Little League season, with someone in the bleachers who came to watch him.

None of them are real people. Every one of them is possible.

[BUILD]

So go home tonight. Call your son. Call your daughter. Call the friend you have been meaning to introduce to someone. Put the phone face down on the table at dinner. Say yes to the next invitation. Ask the question you have been afraid to ask.

Cross the river.

[PAUSE]

And next Thanksgiving, when you look down the table, may that empty chair be filled.

Good night, my friends. God bless you all.
