# The Friendship Recession

## I. The Empty Chair

It is nine o'clock on a Friday night.

A man sits at his kitchen table. He is thirty-eight. He has a decent job, which he does from a spare bedroom with a ring light and a headset. He has a phone that holds the numbers of four hundred people. He has a couch, a streaming subscription, and a delivery app that knows his order before he does.

And in a country of three hundred and forty million people, there is not one human being he could call tonight and say: come over, I'm making dinner, bring nothing.

[PAUSE]

I made him up. He is a composite, not a real person, and I tell you that in the first minute because everything else tonight will be as true as I can make it. But I suspect you have met him a hundred times without ever learning his name.

He is not in crisis. He would not call himself lonely. Ask him and he will say he is fine, he is busy, he is tired, things are a lot right now. His friends are scattered. Everybody has kids. They keep meaning to get together. He will say all the things we say.

There is a chair across from him at the table. Nobody has sat in it in a long time.

[LET IT LAND]

Tonight I am going to talk about that chair. About the millions of chairs like it, in apartments and farmhouses and retirement communities and college dorms. About the plain fact that somebody used to be there.

Somebody used to be there. A neighbor who dropped by without texting first. A bowling team that met on Tuesdays. A congregation that noticed when you missed two Sundays in a row. A lodge, a union hall, a card game, a diner counter where the waitress knew your name. A friend from work who became a friend for life.

Somebody used to be there. And now, for a great many of us, nobody is.

Some economists and writers have already given this a name, and I am going to use it. The friendship recession.

I choose that word on purpose. When the economy contracts, we do not stand in front of the unemployed and tell them to try harder at having jobs. We ask what changed. We look at the data. We ask what moved underneath people's feet. And then we act together, because some problems are too big to be solved one household at a time.

[SLOW]

So here is my whole argument in one sentence, and I will keep coming back to it. We did not stop needing one another. We let the places where we found one another disappear. And what was taken apart can be built again.

Over the next two hours I will make that case in four steps.

First, that the contraction is real. Not nostalgia, not the grumpy story every generation tells about the next. You can see it in how Americans spend their hours, how many friends they say they have, how often they eat alone, and how few of the old institutions are still standing.

Second, that it matters. Connection is not a luxury you get to once the serious business of life is done. It is part of the serious business of life. It is written into the body.

Third, that it has causes, and many of them are public structures rather than private choices. How we build our towns. How we organize our work. How we design our technology. How we let the institutions that once gathered us wither and close.

And fourth, that rebuilding is possible but not automatic. It takes places, and policy, and something from each of us that no policy can supply.

[PAUSE]

I will borrow, along the way, from two very different voices. One is an Athenian general standing over his city's war dead almost two and a half thousand years ago, telling the living what their city was and why it was worth it. The other is an American who escaped slavery and taught a nation, by repeating a hard truth until it could not be ignored, to look at what it had become.

Both of them knew something we have forgotten. A people is not a pile of individuals. A city is a thing you do together. And when you stop doing it, it stops existing.

So let us count the chairs.

## II. The Diagnosis

In the spring of two thousand twenty-three, the Surgeon General of the United States, Dr. Vivek Murthy, did something unusual. He issued a formal public health advisory, the kind of document that office reserves for urgent threats to the nation's health, and he issued it not about a virus, not about a drug, not about a chemical in the water, but about loneliness.

The title was blunt. Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation.

Think about the history of that office. Surgeon General's reports are the documents that told Americans, in the nineteen sixties, that cigarettes cause cancer. They are the documents that have named the dangers of drunk driving, of secondhand smoke, of opioids. When the nation's doctor picks a subject for an advisory, he is saying: this is not a lifestyle tip. This is a threat to public health.

And here is what he said.

About one in two adults in America reported experiencing loneliness. Not a fringe. Not a sad corner of the population. Half.

He said that the mortality impact of being socially disconnected is similar to that of smoking up to fifteen cigarettes a day.

[PAUSE]

Fifteen cigarettes a day.

He reported research showing that loneliness raises the risk of premature death by about twenty-six percent, and that social isolation raises it by about twenty-nine percent. He laid out the links that researchers have found between poor social connection and heart disease, stroke, dementia, depression and anxiety.

And then the advisory made a point that I think deserves more attention than it received. The decline began before the pandemic, and the pandemic sped it up. Or, to put it in my own words, the pandemic poured fuel on a fire that was already burning.

Already burning. This is the point that gets lost. It is tempting to think of loneliness as a hangover from two thousand twenty, from lockdowns and masks and school closures, a temporary wound that time will heal now that we can gather again. But the advisory's own data, as we will see in a moment, showed the decline starting long before anyone had heard of the coronavirus. The pandemic did not start the fire. It found a house that was already full of dry wood.

The advisory insists on keeping two words apart, and the difference matters for everything that follows.

Loneliness is a feeling. It is the painful gap between the connection you have and the connection you need. You can be lonely in a marriage. You can be lonely in a crowded office. You can be lonely with four thousand followers.

Isolation is a condition. It is the objective fact of having few relationships and little contact. You can be isolated and not feel lonely, at least for a while. Some people genuinely prefer a quiet life.

The advisory's argument, and mine tonight, is that both are rising, that both are dangerous, and that they feed one another. The isolated become lonely. The lonely, research suggests, often become more wary of others, more likely to read rejection into neutral faces, and so they withdraw further. It is a spiral. And spirals, left alone, do not usually reverse on their own.

[SLOW]

The Surgeon General did not stop at diagnosis. He proposed what he called a national strategy to advance social connection, built on six pillars.

Strengthen social infrastructure in local communities. That means the physical places, the parks and libraries and community centers, and the programs and institutions that fill them.

Enact pro-connection public policies. That means asking, of every law, whether it brings people together or pushes them apart. Transportation, housing, family leave, the design of the workweek.

Mobilize the health sector. That means training doctors and nurses to ask about social connection the way they ask about smoking and exercise, and to treat disconnection as a real risk factor.

Reform digital environments. That means asking hard questions of the technologies that now mediate so much of our social lives.

Deepen our knowledge. That means measuring connection consistently, so we can tell what is working.

And cultivate a culture of connection. That means the values and norms that make it normal to reach out, to show up, to include.

Notice what those six pillars have in common. Only the last one is primarily about individual behavior. The other five are about institutions. About places. About policy. About systems.

The nation's doctor looked at the loneliness epidemic and did not prescribe a self-help book. He prescribed a rebuilding of public life.

[PAUSE]

To be fair, an advisory is not a law. It carries no funding. It commands no agency to do anything. News coverage at the time noted, correctly, that the declaration was meant to raise awareness and would not by itself unlock federal money or programs.

So, three years later, an uncomfortable question. Did we listen? Did the alarm change anything?

In some places, yes. There are libraries that have reinvented themselves as community living rooms. There are cities experimenting with social prescribing, where a doctor can refer a lonely patient to a walking group or a volunteer program the way she would refer them to a specialist. There are, as I will tell you later, bills sitting in Congress right now that would take the first small federal steps.

But on the whole, if we are honest, the alarm rang and the country rolled over and went back to sleep. Or rather, went back to scrolling.

So let us not be satisfied with the alarm. Let us read the evidence ourselves. And we begin with the first claim: that the contraction is real.

## III. The Ledger of Hours

Every year, the federal government asks a large sample of Americans a remarkably simple question. What did you do yesterday? Hour by hour, activity by activity. Who were you with?

It is called the American Time Use Survey. It began in two thousand three. And because it asks the same question the same way, year after year, it gives us something precious: a ledger. Not of what people say they value, but of how they actually spend the only currency that every human being receives in equal measure. Time.

Let me read you from that ledger.

In two thousand three, according to the Census Bureau's reporting of this survey, the average American spent about forty-seven minutes a day socializing with friends, or attending or hosting social events. A decade later, in two thousand thirteen, it was about forty-three minutes. By two thousand twenty-three, it was thirty-four minutes.

[PAUSE]

Thirteen minutes a day may not sound like much. But a day is a small unit. Multiply it across a year and it is about eighty hours. Two full work weeks of human company, gone, for the average person, every single year.

Count the chairs.

The Surgeon General's advisory, drawing on research using the same survey, put it in monthly terms. Between two thousand three and two thousand twenty, time spent with friends fell by roughly twenty hours a month. And time spent alone rose by roughly twenty-four hours a month.

Twenty hours a month of friendship, subtracted. Twenty-four hours a month of solitude, added.

And it was worst among the young. For Americans aged fifteen to twenty-four, the advisory reported that time spent in person with friends fell by about seventy percent over that period. Even before the pandemic, by two thousand nineteen, the youngest Americans had already lost about an hour a day of time with their friends. An hour, every day, gone.

[LET IT LAND]

Think about what that means. The years from fifteen to twenty-four are the years when, for all of human history, people have learned how to be friends. How to make up after a fight. How to read a room. How to stay up too late talking about nothing and everything. How to belong to a group without dissolving into it. Those are not skills you can download. They are learned the way language is learned, by immersion. And we have cut the immersion by more than two thirds.

There used to be somebody on the porch, in the parking lot, at the mall, in the basement with the video game controller. Now the young are home, in their rooms, alone with the glow.

Here is another way of reading the ledger. A sociologist named Neal Caren recently mapped this same survey, nearly a quarter of a million responses from two thousand three through two thousand twenty-four, hour by hour, day by day. And he found something I have not been able to forget.

In the early two thousands, American social life had a rhythm. A pulse. Modest time with friends on weekdays, and then it built, and built, and peaked on Friday night and Saturday night. The weekend was the heartbeat of social life.

Since the middle of the twenty-tens, that rhythm has flattened. Friday night, in the aggregate, now looks like an ordinary weeknight. Saturday is still a little higher, but much less than before. And total time with friends, in his analysis, has fallen by more than half.

[SLOW]

Friday night now looks like a weeknight.

There is something almost unbearably sad in that sentence. For generations, Friday night was the promise that kept people going through the week. The dance. The game. The bar. The church supper. The party. The night you put on a clean shirt and went out to see who was there.

Now, for many of us, Friday night is Tuesday night with a later bedtime.

Let me read you one more line from the ledger, and it is the one that I think hits closest to home.

The World Happiness Report, an annual international study, devoted part of its two thousand twenty-five edition to the question of whether people share meals. Drawing on the time use data, it found that in two thousand twenty-three, about one in four American adults said they had eaten every single meal the previous day alone. Breakfast, lunch and dinner. Alone.

That share, the report found, had risen by about fifty-three percent since two thousand three. And in that same report, America had fallen to twenty-fourth place in the happiness rankings.

[PAUSE]

Every meal, alone.

The table is the oldest institution we have. Older than the church, older than the state, older than the market. Before there were cities, there were people sitting around a fire, sharing what they had hunted or gathered. Every culture on earth has rituals of the shared meal. The word companion itself, at its root, means someone you share bread with.

And now one in four of us, on any given day, breaks no bread with anyone at all.

Somebody used to be there. Across the table. Passing the salt. Asking how your day was, and waiting for the answer.

A caution, because I promised you honesty. Some of this is the natural result of things that are not bad in themselves. More people live alone than in past generations, partly because more people can afford to, partly because people marry later and live longer. An older widow eating alone is not the same as a teenager eating alone in his room while his parents eat alone in theirs. Some solitude is chosen and welcome.

But the ledger does not lie about the direction. Across age groups, across the years, before the pandemic and after it, the hours we give to one another have been shrinking. The hours we spend alone have been growing. And the institutions that used to structure our time together, the Friday night, the Sunday dinner, the weekly meeting, have been quietly dissolving.

That is what a recession looks like. Not one dramatic crash, but a steady contraction, quarter after quarter, until one day you look up and realize that the economy you remember is gone.

Count the chairs. The ledger has counted them for us, and the number keeps going down.

That is the first claim. The contraction is real. Now the second: does it matter?

## IV. What the Body Knows

Someone will say: fine, people see their friends less. That is sad. But is it a public health crisis? Is it really in the same category as smoking, or obesity, or addiction? Isn't this a matter for poets and therapists, not for doctors and lawmakers?

It is a fair question. Let me answer it with evidence.

In two thousand ten, three researchers, Julianne Holt-Lunstad, Timothy Smith and Bradley Layton, published a paper in the journal PLoS Medicine. They did what scientists call a meta-analysis. Instead of running one study, they gathered up every good study they could find that measured people's social relationships and then followed them over time to see who lived and who died.

They found one hundred forty-eight studies. Together those studies had followed more than three hundred thousand people.

And what they found was that people with stronger social relationships had substantially better odds of survival over the follow-up period than people with weaker ones. The headline number, an odds ratio of one and a half, was often reported as a fifty percent greater likelihood of survival.

[PAUSE]

A speech is not a license to exaggerate, so let me be precise. An odds ratio is not quite the same thing as saying your chance of living is fifty percent higher. And these were observational studies. They show an association, a strong and consistent one, not a laboratory proof of cause and effect. Perhaps people who are already sick withdraw from friends. Perhaps something else drives both.

But here is what makes the finding hard to dismiss. The effect held across age, across sex, across initial health status, across cause of death, across how long people were followed. And the authors concluded that the influence of social relationships on the risk of death was comparable to well-established risk factors. They named them. Smoking. Alcohol. And they found it exceeded the influence of things like physical inactivity and obesity.

And the strongest associations were not for the simplest measures, like whether you lived alone. They were for the richest measures. How integrated you were into a web of relationships. How many roles you played in other people's lives. Whether you belonged.

[SLOW]

Belonging is not a feeling that floats above the body. It is in the body.

Consider one of the longest studies of human life ever conducted. In nineteen thirty-eight, researchers at Harvard began following two hundred sixty-eight young men who were then sophomores at the college. Later, a second group was folded in: more than four hundred men who had grown up in some of Boston's poorest neighborhoods. Researchers followed these men for decades. Through wars and careers and marriages and divorces, through illness and old age. Eventually they began to study their wives and their children.

It is now called the Harvard Study of Adult Development. Its current director, the psychiatrist Robert Waldinger, has spent years explaining what all that data showed.

And the single clearest message was not about wealth. It was not about fame. It was not about cholesterol.

It was about relationships.

Waldinger has said that the participants who were most satisfied in their relationships at age fifty were the healthiest at age eighty. It was not their cholesterol levels in middle age that predicted how they would grow old. It was how satisfied they were in their relationships. And he has put it in words that I cannot improve on. Loneliness kills, he said. It is as powerful as smoking or alcoholism.

[LET IT LAND]

The man who did all the right things for his body and neglected his friends did worse, on average, than the man who carried a few extra pounds but had people who would sit with him in the hospital.

Somebody used to be there. And whether somebody is there may decide how long you are there.

Widen the lens. In two thousand twenty-three, the World Health Organization created a Commission on Social Connection, co-chaired by the same Dr. Murthy and by Chido Mpemba, an advisor to the chairperson of the African Union. In June of two thousand twenty-five, that commission released its flagship report.

It estimated that about one in six people on earth is affected by loneliness.

It estimated that loneliness is linked to about one hundred deaths every hour. More than eight hundred and seventy-one thousand deaths a year.

[PAUSE]

One hundred deaths an hour. In the time it takes me to finish this speech, by that estimate, about two hundred people somewhere on this planet will have died with loneliness as part of the story of their death.

The report found that lonely people are about twice as likely to become depressed. It linked loneliness and isolation to stroke, heart disease, diabetes, cognitive decline and early death. It found that lonely teenagers were more likely to do worse in school. And the director-general of the World Health Organization warned that, left unaddressed, loneliness would keep costing societies billions in health care, education and employment.

Why would this be? Why would something as soft as friendship be as hard as a heart attack?

Scientists are still working out the mechanisms, and I will not pretend they are settled. But the broad picture they describe is this. For almost all of human history, being alone was dangerous. A human being cut off from the group was a human being exposed to predators, to hunger, to cold, to enemies. So our bodies evolved to treat social disconnection as a threat, the way they treat hunger or thirst. Loneliness, on this account, is an alarm. It is the body saying: you are exposed, get back to the group.

And when the alarm rings and rings and never stops, the body stays on alert. Stress responses that were meant to last minutes last for months. Sleep gets worse. Inflammation, researchers have found, tends to rise. The things that protect us, the walk with a friend, the person who notices you have not been eating, the spouse who nags you to see the doctor, quietly disappear.

That last point is easy to miss and enormously important. Friends are not only good for us in some mysterious biological way. They are good for us in very practical ways. They drive us to appointments. They notice when we are drinking too much. They tell us that the mole looks different. They bring soup. They show up.

[BUILD]

So when someone tells you that loneliness is a soft issue, a matter for greeting cards, tell them about the meta-analysis of three hundred thousand people. Tell them about the Harvard men at fifty and at eighty. Tell them about one hundred deaths an hour. Tell them the nation's doctor compared it to smoking up to fifteen cigarettes a day.

We did not treat smoking as a private choice to be solved by willpower alone. We changed laws. We changed buildings. We changed advertising. We changed culture. We changed what was normal.

We can do that again. But first we have to believe that what is at stake is life, and not only comfort.

That is the second claim. The contraction is real, and it is lethal. Before we ask what caused it, let me show you what we lost.

## V. The Life of the City

Come with me a very long way back. To Athens, in the winter after the first year of a long and terrible war.

The custom of the Athenians was to hold a public funeral for those who had died in battle that year. The bones of the dead were laid in cypress coffins, one for each tribe, and carried in procession to the public tomb in what was called the Beautiful suburb of the city. And a man chosen by the city for his wisdom and standing would rise and speak over them.

That year, the man was Pericles. And the speech he gave, as the historian Thucydides recorded it, has become one of the most famous speeches in the history of the world.

What is striking about it, if you read it today, is how little of it is about the dead. Pericles barely mentions them by name. Instead he talks about the city. About Athens. About the way of life these men had died to protect. He praises its laws, its openness, its festivals, its generosity, its willingness to let people live as they please.

And in the middle of that praise comes a line I will read to you in the old Victorian translation by Richard Crawley.

He says that the Athenians, unlike any other nation, regard the man "who takes no part in these duties not as unambitious but as useless."

[PAUSE]

Not as unambitious, but as useless.

He is talking about public life. About taking part in the affairs of the city. The Greeks had a word for the person who withdrew entirely into private concerns, who did not show up to the assembly, who did not care about the common life. That word, idiotes, is the ancestor of our word idiot. It did not mean stupid. It meant private. Merely private. A person who had shrunk his world to the size of his own house.

I will not stand here and hold up ancient Athens as a model. It was not one. It was a slave society. Women were shut out of public life. Its democracy was for a minority of the people who lived there. Pericles himself was the leader of an empire that grew more brutal as the war went on. Any honest use of that speech has to hold all of that at once.

But the idea at the center of it is worth rescuing. The idea that a human life is not complete when it is only private. That there is a kind of flourishing you can only reach in common with others. That a city is not a place where people happen to live near each other, but a thing that people do together, every day, in assemblies and festivals and markets and gymnasiums and shared meals.

And notice one more line, because it is about friendship directly. Pericles says the Athenians are singular in their generosity, "acquiring our friends by conferring, not by receiving, favours."

Acquiring friends by giving, not by getting. Hold on to that. We will come back to it at the end.

[SLOW]

Jump forward more than two thousand years, to a young French aristocrat traveling through the United States in the eighteen thirties. His name was Alexis de Tocqueville, and he came, officially, to study American prisons. He ended up writing what is still perhaps the most perceptive book ever written about this country, Democracy in America.

And what astonished Tocqueville most about Americans was not their elections or their constitution. It was their clubs.

In the old translation by Henry Reeve, he wrote that "Americans of all ages, all conditions, and all dispositions, constantly form associations."

He was dazzled by it. Americans, he observed, formed associations of a thousand kinds. Religious and moral, serious and silly, huge and tiny. They formed associations to give entertainments, to found schools, to build inns, to construct churches, to distribute books, to send missionaries to the other side of the world. Where in France you would see the government at the head of a new undertaking, and in England a lord, in the United States, he said, you would be sure to find an association.

[PAUSE]

This was the American genius, as Tocqueville saw it. In a democracy, where no one was born to rule and every individual was in some sense on his own, people faced a danger: that each person would withdraw into a small circle of family and friends and leave the larger society to fend for itself. Tocqueville called that tendency individualism, and he worried about it. But Americans, he thought, had found the antidote. They joined. They gathered. They learned, in their thousands of little associations, the art of doing things together. And in learning that art, they kept their democracy alive.

He even wrote that if people are to remain civilized, or to become so, the art of associating together must grow and improve.

Must grow and improve. Not merely survive. Grow.

So here is the inheritance. From Athens, the idea that a person who withdraws entirely into private life has lost something essential, both for himself and for his city. From Tocqueville's America, the idea that a free people stays free by constantly gathering in voluntary associations, by practicing, again and again, the art of doing things together.

That is the life of the city. That is what we had. Not perfectly, never perfectly. American associations were often segregated by race, often closed to women, often exclusive and petty and small-minded. Any honest account has to say that too. But the habit of joining was real, and it was ours, and for a long time it was the envy of the world.

[BUILD]

Somebody used to be there. At the barn raising. At the Grange hall. At the church social and the union picnic and the Rotary lunch and the Elks lodge fish fry. At the PTA meeting in the school gym with the folding chairs. At the volunteer fire department pancake breakfast.

What happened to them is the third part of my argument. Where did everybody go?

## VI. Bowling Alone

In January of nineteen ninety-five, a political scientist at Harvard named Robert Putnam published an essay in a small academic journal. It was called Bowling Alone: America's Declining Social Capital. It was not the kind of thing that usually makes the evening news. But it touched a nerve so deep that within months it was being argued over in magazines and newspapers across the country. Five years later he expanded it into a book of the same name, and that book, Bowling Alone, became one of the most influential works of social science of its generation.

The title came from a small, strange fact. More Americans were bowling than before. But fewer of them were bowling in leagues. As Putnam's work was summarized at the time, between nineteen eighty and nineteen ninety-three the number of Americans who bowled went up by about ten percent, while league bowling fell by about forty percent.

[PAUSE]

People were still going to the lanes. They were just going alone, or with one or two others. The league, with its weekly schedule, its teammates, its standings, its beer afterward and its arguments about the scorekeeping, was disappearing.

Putnam did not care about bowling for its own sake. He cared about what bowling leagues represented. They were one small example of a vast web of voluntary associations, the very web that Tocqueville had marveled at, that had knit Americans together for generations.

And Putnam found the same pattern everywhere he looked. Membership in parent teacher associations, which had soared in the first half of the twentieth century, fell from more than twelve million in nineteen sixty-four to barely five million in nineteen eighty-two, and by the mid nineties had recovered only to about seven million. Fraternal organizations like the Elks, the Lions, the Masons, the Shriners, saw their rolls shrink and their members grow older. Union membership declined. Attendance at public meetings declined. Having friends over for dinner declined. Even playing cards, the great American social pastime of the mid twentieth century, declined.

He gave the thing a name. Social capital. The idea is simple. Just as physical capital is tools and machines, and human capital is skills and education, social capital is the networks of relationships among people, and the trust and habits of cooperation that grow from those networks. It is the reason a neighbor will watch your kids in an emergency. It is the reason a town can organize a food drive in an afternoon. It is the reason a stranger returns your wallet.

And social capital, Putnam argued, was draining away.

[SLOW]

Why? He looked hard at the suspects, and his answers were careful. Some of it, he thought, was the pressure of time and money, especially as more households had two working parents. Some of it was suburban sprawl and the long commute, which took hours that had once gone to community life. A great deal of it, he argued, was television, which privatized leisure, pulling people off the porch and into the living room. And the biggest single factor in his analysis was generational. The generation that had come of age during the Depression and the Second World War, the most civically engaged generation in American history, was slowly being replaced by children and grandchildren who never joined in the same way.

Notice that Putnam wrote all of this before the smartphone. Before social media. Before the gig economy, before streaming, before remote work. The decline he documented was already well underway by the year two thousand.

Which means that what we are living through now is not a new collapse. It is the continuation of a long one. A recession that began more than half a century ago and has simply never ended.

Then there are the largest associations of all. The churches, synagogues, mosques and temples.

For most of the twentieth century, whatever else was happening in American life, one thing held remarkably steady. When the Gallup organization first asked Americans in nineteen thirty-seven whether they belonged to a church or synagogue, seventy-three percent said yes. And for the next six decades, the number stayed close to seventy percent.

Then it began to fall. In nineteen ninety-nine, it was seventy percent. By two thousand twenty, it was forty-seven percent. For the first time in Gallup's eight decades of measuring, a minority of Americans belonged to a house of worship.

[PAUSE]

Let me be plain: I am not making a theological argument tonight. I am not telling anyone what to believe or whether to believe. Many Americans have left religious institutions for reasons of conscience, and some for reasons of real harm done to them. That is their right, and some of those reasons deserve to be heard with humility by those institutions.

My point is a sociological one, and it would be the same whatever you think about God. For a very large share of Americans, the congregation was the single most important source of community they had. It was where you met your spouse and where you buried your parents. It was where somebody brought a casserole when you had a baby, and where a dozen people showed up to help you move. It was where the old and the young sat in the same room every week. It was where you were known.

When membership falls from seven in ten to fewer than half, something enormous goes with it. And in most places, nothing has replaced it.

Somebody used to be there. In the pew behind you. In the fellowship hall with the bad coffee. At the hospital bedside, because the pastor or the rabbi or the imam came, and the women's circle came, and the men's group came.

[BUILD]

Count the institutions that have hollowed out in a single lifetime. The bowling league. The lodge. The union hall. The PTA. The congregation. The local newspaper, where your neighbors' names appeared when they married or were promoted or died. The main street diner, outcompeted by the drive through. The department store lunch counter. The movie palace, replaced by a screen in every room.

Count the chairs. Count the empty ones in a single lifetime.

And understand what those places had in common. They were not just places to do an activity. They were places to see the same people, again and again, without having to plan it. That is the secret ingredient of friendship that we keep forgetting. Researchers who study how friendships form keep returning to the same few conditions. Proximity. Repeated, unplanned interaction. A setting that encourages people to let their guard down. The old institutions provided all three, automatically, every week.

You did not have to text anyone to make plans to see the people at the lodge. You just went to the lodge. You did not have to coordinate four calendars to see the people from church. You just went to church. The friendship grew in the cracks of the routine.

When the routine disappears, friendship does not merely become less frequent. It becomes a project. Something you have to schedule, coordinate, plan. And for busy, tired, overextended people, projects are the first thing to go.

That is the quiet mechanism of the friendship recession. We did not decide to stop having friends. We let the institutions that made friendship automatic disappear, and then we discovered that friendship without institutions is very, very hard.

Putnam himself, in later years, offered an important note of hope. He pointed out that America had been through something like this before. At the end of the nineteenth century, in the Gilded Age, industrialization and immigration and the explosive growth of cities had torn people away from small towns and old communities. Social life was fractured. Inequality was extreme. And what followed was an extraordinary burst of civic invention. In a few decades, Americans founded or expanded a remarkable list of the associations that would define the twentieth century. Many of the civic clubs, the scouting movements, the settlement houses, the parent teacher associations, the women's clubs and the professional societies trace their roots to that era.

In other words, the associations that we are now losing were not ancient. They were built. Built by people who looked around at a disconnected, atomized country and decided to do something about it.

[LET IT LAND]

We are not the first generation to face a friendship recession. We may yet be a generation that ends one.

## VII. The Men Who Have No One

One group has been hit harder than almost anyone else in the data. And for that very reason, they are the least likely to tell you about it.

Men.

In May of two thousand twenty-one, the Survey Center on American Life conducted a large national survey of American friendships. And it compared the results to a similar survey from nineteen ninety. The findings became, for a moment, a national conversation.

In nineteen ninety, about three percent of American men said they had no close friends.

In two thousand twenty-one, it was fifteen percent.

[PAUSE]

A fivefold increase. One in seven American men, by that measure, had no one they would call a close friend.

Look at the other end of the scale. In nineteen ninety, more than half of men, fifty-five percent, said they had six or more close friends.

By two thousand twenty-one, it was twenty-seven percent. Half as many.

Women's circles shrank too. In nineteen ninety, forty-one percent of women had six or more close friends. By two thousand twenty-one, twenty-four percent. This is not only a male problem. But the men started higher, fell further, and were more likely to end up with no one at all.

And look at what happens to young men in particular. In nineteen ninety, when a young man had a personal problem, nearly half said the first person they would turn to was a friend. By two thousand twenty-one, that had fallen to about one in five. And the share who would turn first to their parents had roughly doubled, from seventeen percent to thirty-six percent.

I do not say that to mock anyone for calling their mother. Thank God for mothers. But think about what it means for a young man at twenty-five or thirty to have no peer he would trust with his troubles. The friend from his own generation, who is going through the same things, who can tell him he is not crazy, who will be around long after his parents are gone, has gone missing.

[SLOW]

The same survey asked about the past week. Three questions. I will give you the men first, then the women.

Had they shared their personal feelings with a friend in a private conversation? About thirty percent of men. Forty-eight percent of women.

Had they received emotional support from a friend? Twenty-one percent of men. Forty-one percent of women.

Had they told a friend they loved them? Twenty-five percent of men. Forty-nine percent of women.

In every case the women's number was far higher. In two of the three, it was nearly double.

Add a more recent number. In May of two thousand twenty-five, the Gallup organization published an analysis of its world poll, comparing loneliness across the wealthy democracies of the OECD. It found that a quarter of American men aged fifteen to thirty-four said they had felt lonely for a lot of the previous day. Among all other American adults, it was seventeen percent. And across the OECD countries, the median for young men was just fifteen percent.

Young American men, in other words, are among the loneliest young men in the developed world. Gallup found that the United States was one of only three of these countries where young men reported more loneliness than everyone else, and it had the widest gap of the three.

Before I go further, a word to the women in this room, because no one should hear this section as saying that only men are hurting.

The same Gallup analysis found a second American exception. Women in the middle of life, aged thirty-five to fifty-four, reported daily loneliness at twenty percent, against an OECD median for women their age of fourteen percent. These are the women who are often carrying the heaviest load in the country. Raising children, caring for aging parents, holding down jobs, and very often serving as the unpaid social secretary for an entire family. It is possible to be surrounded by people all day, needed by everyone, and still have no one who asks how you are.

And the friendship survey of two thousand twenty-one found that young women were the group most likely to have lost touch with friends during the pandemic year. Nearly six in ten young women said they had lost touch with at least a few friends, and about one in six said they had lost touch with most of them.

So the recession has many faces. The young man with no one to call. The mother in midlife with everyone to care for and no one to care for her. The teenager who lost a year of friendship and never got it back. They are not competing for sympathy. They are casualties of the same contraction.

But stay with the men a little longer, because their losses have a particular shape, and the shape tells us something about the cure.

[PAUSE]

Why? Why would men, who for generations dominated the clubs and the lodges and the union halls and the bars, now be the ones left with no one?

I think the answer is in the very structure of male friendship as many men were raised to practice it.

There is an old observation, repeated by many researchers who study friendship, that women's friendships tend to be face to face, and men's tend to be side by side. Women, on average, are more likely to build friendships through talk, through sharing what they feel, through the conversation itself. Men, on average, are more likely to build friendships through doing things together. Fixing the truck. Watching the game. Playing on the team. Working the same shift. Fishing. Bowling in the league.

These are generalizations, and every one of you can name exceptions. But if there is truth in them, then you can see what happened. Side by side friendship depends on having something to stand beside each other at. It depends on the activity. On the team, the shift, the league, the lodge, the job site. Take away the institution, and the side by side friendship has nothing to hold it up.

Women's friendships were damaged by the collapse of institutions too. But a friendship built on talk can survive on talk. A phone call, a text thread, a coffee. A friendship built on the Tuesday night league dies when the league dies.

[BUILD]

Think of everything that happened to men's institutions at once. The factory closed, and with it the shift, the break room, the bar across the street at quitting time. The union local shrank. The lodge aged out. The church emptied, and men had long been less likely than women to fill the pews in the first place. The military, which once put millions of young men shoulder to shoulder, became a small professional force.

Men married later. When they did marry, many made their wife the manager of the couple's whole social life. And when the marriage ended, they discovered they had no friends of their own. The survey found the same pattern: the overwhelming majority of married men said their spouse was the first person they would talk to about a problem.

And then there is the culture. Generations of boys were taught that needing people is weakness. That a real man handles it. That you do not say I love you to your friend, you punch him in the arm. That you do not call to say you are struggling, you wait until someone asks, and nobody asks.

Somebody used to be there. The guys on the line. The guys in the league. The guys from the unit. And now so many men are home, alone, with a screen and a silence they have no words for.

Hear this carefully, because there are people in our public life who would take the loneliness of men and turn it into a weapon. Who would tell lonely young men that their pain is someone else's fault. That women, or immigrants, or some other group, stole what they should have had. That is a lie, and it is a dangerous one. Resentment is not connection. A movement built on grievance gives a lonely man an enemy, but it does not give him a friend.

The honest answer is harder and more hopeful. Men are not lonely because someone took something from them. Men are lonely because the institutions that held up their friendships collapsed, and because they were taught a way of being that left them unable to build new ones. Both of those things can change.

[SLOW]

So to the men listening tonight, let me speak directly.

You are not weak for wanting friends. You are human. The need for other people is not a flaw in your design. It is the design.

And it is not too late. The research on friendship consistently finds that it takes time, many hours together, to move from acquaintance to friend. Which means the solution is not a dramatic gesture. It is showing up. Joining the thing. Going back next week. Being the one who sends the text that says, a few of us are watching the game Saturday, come by.

Somebody used to be there. You can be the one who is there now.

## VIII. The Office That Emptied

That brings us to the last great social institution most American adults still had. The one that survived the decline of the lodge and the league and the congregation. The one that, for millions of people, had quietly become the main place where they saw other human beings.

The office.

We do not usually think of work as a social institution. We think of it as the thing we do for money. But consider. For most of the late twentieth century, many working adults spent more of their waking weekday hours with coworkers than with anyone else, including their own families. The workplace was where people met their spouses, found their best friends, learned the gossip, celebrated birthdays with a sheet cake in the break room, went out for drinks on Friday, and grieved together when someone died.

For many people whose churches had emptied and whose neighborhoods had gone quiet, the job was the last place left where they were part of something. Where people knew their name. Where somebody would notice if they did not show up.

[PAUSE]

And then, in the spring of two thousand twenty, almost overnight, a large share of that institution vanished.

Economists Jose Maria Barrero, Nicholas Bloom and Steven Davis have tracked working arrangements in the United States through a long running survey. Their work suggests that before the pandemic, only about seven percent of full paid workdays were worked from home. During the pandemic, that share exploded. And then, rather than snapping back, it settled. For the past few years, by their measures, roughly a quarter of all paid workdays in America have been worked from home.

A quarter of the workweek of the country, moved from the shared space to the spare bedroom.

Remote work deserves fairness, so let me be fair to it. For many people, it has been a genuine gift. It gave back hours that had been wasted in traffic. It let parents be home when their kids got off the bus. It opened up jobs to people with disabilities who found offices exhausting or inaccessible. It let people live near aging parents, or in towns they loved but where good jobs were scarce. Plenty of people who work from home spend the time they saved on commuting with their families or in their communities. I am not here to tell anyone to go back to a cubicle and a two hour commute.

[SLOW]

But I am here to tell you that we made one of the largest changes to the social structure of adult life in a century, and we made it almost entirely on the basis of productivity and convenience, without asking what it would do to friendship.

Think about how workplace friendships actually form. They form in exactly the way I described earlier. Proximity. Repeated, unplanned interaction. The conversation at the coffee machine that wanders off topic. The person in the next cubicle who hears you sigh and asks what is wrong. The lunch that becomes a habit. The project that throws you together under pressure. The Friday drinks.

None of that happens on a video call. A video call is a meeting. It has an agenda. It has a start and a stop. When the meeting ends, the window closes, and you are alone in the room again. There is no walk back to your desks together. There is no lingering. There is no accident.

And think about who is most affected. A forty-five-year-old with a spouse, children, and a network built over decades may lose relatively little by working from home.

Now picture someone else, and again, she is an illustration, not a real person. Call her Ines. She is twenty-four. She has just moved to Denver for her first real job. Her college friends have scattered across six states. She knows no one in the city.

In another decade, the office would have handed Ines a social life without her asking. The woman at the next desk would have invited her to trivia. Someone would have dragged her to the softball game on Thursday nights. By Christmas she would have had a lunch table, a holiday party, a few people who knew how she took her coffee.

Instead she logs on from a studio apartment at eight and logs off at five. Her coworkers are squares on a screen. She has lived in a city of millions for a year, and she could not name three people in it who would notice if she left.

[PAUSE]

There used to be somebody at the next desk. For Ines, there never was.

[PAUSE]

There is one more thing about remote work that I think is underappreciated. It does not only remove the office. It removes the reason to leave the house at all.

When you commute, even if you hate the commute, you are out in the world. You pass the same people on the train. You stop at the same coffee shop and the barista learns your order. You eat lunch somewhere near the office. You run errands on the way home. Your days have edges, and those edges are full of small, weak connections, the kind of passing relationships that sociologists say matter more than we think.

When you work from home, and order your groceries to the door, and stream your entertainment, and do your banking on your phone, it is entirely possible to go days without speaking to another human being face to face. Not because you decided to become a hermit. But because every single convenience, each one reasonable on its own, removed one more occasion to be among people.

[BUILD]

That is the pattern I want you to see. No one sat down and decided to make Americans lonely. We made a thousand reasonable choices. Drive through instead of the diner. Streaming instead of the theater. Delivery instead of the store. Video call instead of the office. Self checkout instead of the cashier. Each one saved a little time, a little money, a little friction.

And friction, it turns out, was where the friendships were.

So what do we do? I am not going to stand here and demand that every company order everyone back to the office five days a week. Some of those orders have more to do with control than with community. But I am going to ask employers, and workers, and policymakers, to put connection back into the calculation.

If you run a company with remote workers, ask yourself whether your people have any way to actually know one another. Not another mandatory video happy hour, which everyone dreads. Real time together, in person, with nothing on the agenda.

If you work from home, ask yourself where your edges went. Find a coworking space, a library, a coffee shop where you can work among people. Join something in your neighborhood that meets in person. Use the hours you saved on commuting for something that puts you in a room with other people.

And if you make policy, ask what the end of the daily commute means for downtowns, for transit, for the cafes and lunch counters and shops that served as the informal commons of working life. Some of those places are struggling. Some have already closed. When they go, the city loses part of its living room.

We gained something real when the office emptied. Let us not pretend we lost nothing.

## IX. The Screen in the Hand

I cannot give a speech about loneliness in the year two thousand twenty-six without talking about the device that is, right now, very likely in your pocket. This is a subject that has generated a great deal of heat and lost a fair amount of light, so I will go slowly.

Here is what we know. The steepest declines in time with friends, especially among the young, track closely with the years in which smartphones and social media became nearly universal. Caren's analysis of the time use data puts the flattening of Friday night in the middle of the twenty-tens. That is when the smartphone went from a novelty to an appendage.

Here is what we do not know with certainty. Researchers are genuinely divided on how much of the rise in loneliness, anxiety and depression among young people is caused by these technologies, as opposed to merely accompanying them. Some scholars argue the evidence for harm is strong and growing. Others argue that the effects found in many studies are small, and that panic about new technology is as old as the printing press. Honest people disagree. I am not going to pretend the science is settled.

[PAUSE]

But let me offer you an argument that does not depend on settling that dispute.

Every hour has only sixty minutes in it. Every evening has only so many hours. Whatever else these devices do or do not do to our minds, they compete for our time. And they are, by design, extraordinarily good at winning that competition. Some of the most talented engineers and designers in the world have spent careers figuring out how to keep us looking a little longer. The infinite scroll. The autoplay. The notification. The streak.

A friend cannot compete with that on those terms. A friend is inconvenient. A friend has a schedule that does not match yours. A friend lives across town. A friend sometimes says things you do not want to hear. A friend is not optimized for engagement.

So even if no single hour on a phone does any harm at all, the hours add up. And they come from somewhere. When the ledger shows young people losing about an hour a day of time with friends over the same years that screen time ballooned, you do not need a randomized trial to wonder where the hours went.

[SLOW]

There is a second argument, and it is about what connection online can and cannot do.

I do not want to dismiss online friendship. For a gay teenager in a small town, for a person with a rare disease, for someone homebound by disability, for immigrants keeping in touch with family across an ocean, the internet has been a lifeline. People find their people online, sometimes people they could never have found any other way. That is real, and it is good.

But for most of us, most of the time, online contact works best as a supplement to presence, not a substitute for it. A text thread can keep a friendship alive between visits. It is much worse at building one from nothing. You can know the vacation photos of three hundred people and not have one of them to call when your father dies.

And there is something about physical presence that we are only beginning to appreciate. The shared meal. The hug. The hand on the shoulder at the funeral. The laughter that spreads through a room. The way you can sit in silence with a good friend and feel completely accompanied. These are not lesser forms of communication that technology has improved upon. They are the original forms, the ones our bodies were built for.

Somebody used to be there. Not on a screen. In the room.

[BUILD]

The Surgeon General's fourth pillar was to reform digital environments, and I think that is exactly the right frame. The problem is not that the technology exists. The problem is that it was built to capture attention rather than to serve connection. That is a design choice. Design choices can be made differently.

Platforms could be built to help people meet in person rather than to keep them scrolling alone. Some of the most hopeful uses of technology are exactly that: apps that help neighbors share tools, find pickup basketball games, organize dinners, discover local groups.

Schools could decide, as many now have, that the school day is a time for students to be with one another rather than with their phones. Families could decide that the dinner table is a place without screens. Each of us could decide that when we are with a friend, we are with the friend.

None of this is technophobia. It is a decision about what comes first. It says that the people in front of us matter more than the people on the screen, and that our attention, which is the most precious thing we have to give, should go first to the people who share our lives.

And now we face a new frontier. Artificial companions, chatbots built to be our friends, our confidants, even our partners, are spreading quickly. Some lonely people may find real comfort in them, and I do not want to mock anyone's comfort. But I will say this plainly, as an opinion and not a finding. A machine that is always available, always agreeable and never needs anything from you is not a friend. It may be a balm. It may even be a bridge. But if we let it become a replacement, we will have solved the problem of loneliness the way a painkiller solves a broken leg.

Friendship, remember, in Pericles' words, is acquired by conferring favors, not by receiving them. A friend is someone you give to. You cannot give anything to a product. And a life in which no one ever needs you is not a connected life, however pleasant the conversation.

[PAUSE]

Let me say one more word, to the parents listening tonight, because so much of this lands on you.

Many of you are fighting this battle at your own kitchen table every evening. You are trying to pry a phone out of a teenager's hand. You are wondering whether your child has any real friends, or only followers. You are watching a son disappear into his room for hours. You are watching a daughter measure herself against a feed of perfect lives.

You cannot win that battle alone, and you should not have to. When every other child in the class has a phone, the parent who says no is not protecting a child. She is isolating one. That is precisely why these questions are collective. When a whole school decides the school day is phone free, no single child is left out. When a group of families on the same block agree to hold off on smartphones until high school, no single child is the odd one out. When a town builds a skate park, a teen center, a library with a gaming room and a maker space, it gives young people somewhere to go that is not a screen.

That is the lesson of this whole section, and in a way of this whole speech. Problems that are created collectively cannot be solved individually. They have to be solved together. Which is, of course, the one thing a lonely society finds hardest to do.

So there are the causes. The institutions that hollowed out. The office that emptied. The screen that filled the silence. Before I tell you what we can do about them, I owe you the case against me.

## X. The Strongest Objection

I have been making a case for over an hour. If I have done my job, some of you have been building a case against me in your heads. Good. A speech that cannot survive its strongest objection does not deserve your agreement. So let me make that objection as powerfully as I can, and then let me answer it.

Here is the objection.

Every generation thinks the next one is falling apart. In the nineteen fifties, critics warned that Americans had become a lonely crowd of conformists in their suburban boxes. In the nineteen seventies, they called it the me decade. In the nineties, it was bowling alone. Now it is the friendship recession. Moral panic about the decline of community is one of the most reliable genres in American writing, and the community somehow never quite dies.

And the data, the objection continues, are shakier than you are letting on. Loneliness is measured with surveys, and surveys change. Gallup itself says it cannot yet tell how loneliness has changed over time, because it only began measuring loneliness around the world in two thousand twenty-three. The friendship survey that found men with no close friends rising from three to fifteen percent was comparing two different surveys, decades apart, with different methods. Even the time use data are less tidy than the headlines. The economist Bryce Ward, who has studied these numbers closely, found that time with friends was roughly flat from two thousand three to around two thousand thirteen, and that the steep drop came afterward. Other analysts have argued that when you break the data down by age, the decline for many adults is much gentler than the averages suggest.

[PAUSE]

And then the deepest version of the objection. Maybe this is simply what people want. We are richer than our grandparents. We can afford to live alone, and many of us like it. We have more entertainment at our fingertips than any king in history. Maybe introverts finally have a world built for them. Maybe the old institutions, the lodges and churches and leagues, were often stifling, conformist, exclusive, and people left them because they were glad to be free. Who are you to tell people they should want more company than they choose?

That is the objection. I think it is serious. Let me answer it in four parts.

First, on the data. Yes, every individual measure has flaws. That is true of almost every social trend worth knowing about. But the case for a friendship recession does not rest on any single number. It rests on the convergence of many different kinds of evidence, collected by different people, using different methods, all pointing the same direction. Federal time use diaries. Independent polls on friendship. Gallup's church membership series going back to the nineteen thirties. Putnam's decades of data on associations. The World Happiness Report on solitary meals. International comparisons from Gallup's world poll. When measures this different keep telling the same story, the story is probably true, even if each individual number deserves a margin of error.

And notice that Ward's finding does not weaken the argument. It sharpens it. If the decline in time with friends accelerated after the early twenty-tens, that tells us something about causes. It points us toward what changed in those years. It gives us a target.

[SLOW]

Second, on moral panic. It is true that people have worried about the decline of community before. But it is also true that sometimes they were right. Putnam was not merely complaining in nineteen ninety-five. The membership of the institutions he studied really did fall, and has kept falling. The fact that someone cried wolf in the past does not mean there is no wolf now. The question is not whether the story sounds familiar. The question is whether the evidence supports it. I have tried tonight to show you that it does.

Third, on choice. I take seriously the point that much of this is chosen. I would never tell a person who loves solitude that they must fill their calendar. Solitude is not loneliness. Some of the best things in human life, prayer, reading, art, deep thought, require being alone.

But here is the problem with the argument from choice. If this were simply what people wanted, they would be happier for it. They are not. Half of American adults report loneliness. Young men in this country are lonelier than young men across most of the developed world. When people get what they truly want, they do not usually report feeling worse.

And more fundamentally, choices are made within structures. A person who lives in a neighborhood with no sidewalks, no park, no cafe, no library within walking distance, whose job is remote, whose church closed, whose friends moved away for work, whose town lost its main street to a highway bypass, did not exactly choose isolation. They chose among the options they had. And the options have been narrowing for half a century. To call the result a free choice is like calling it a free choice to eat fast food in a neighborhood with no grocery store.

[PAUSE]

Fourth, and finally, on the old institutions. Here I agree with the objection more than you might expect. Many of the institutions we lost were exclusive. Many lodges barred Black Americans, Jews, Catholics, women. Many congregations wounded people. Many small towns were suffocating for anyone who did not fit. Nostalgia is not a policy. I am not asking anyone to go back to nineteen fifty-five.

But the answer to an exclusive institution is not no institutions. It is better ones. The generation that built the associations of the twentieth century did not sit around mourning the village. They invented new forms for a new world. Our task is the same. Not to restore what was lost, but to build what is needed. Institutions that are open to everyone. That fit how people actually live now. That make friendship easy again, the way the old ones did, without the exclusions that made too many of the old ones cruel.

So I accept the objection's warnings. Be careful with data. Be humble about causes. Do not romanticize the past. Respect people's freedom. All of that, yes.

[BUILD]

And still, the chairs are empty. Still, the ledger keeps counting down. Still, the body pays. Still, the men have no one to call. Still, Friday night looks like Tuesday. And still, somebody used to be there, and now nobody is.

The objection asks me to be careful. I will be careful. It does not give me permission to be silent.

## XI. The Democracy of Friends

So let me be careful, and let me tell you how deep this goes. I have talked about loneliness as a matter of health, and of happiness, and of the private pain of the empty chair. But there is one more cost I have not counted, and it is the one Pericles and Tocqueville cared about most.

Self government.

Tocqueville believed that the habit of association was not merely pleasant. He believed it was the school in which a free people learned to govern themselves. In the club, the congregation, the town meeting, the volunteer company, Americans learned to run a meeting, to hear an opponent out, to compromise, to elect a treasurer and hold him accountable, to work beside someone they disagreed with toward a goal they shared. Democracy, in that view, is not something that happens once every two or four years in a voting booth. It is something practiced every week, in a thousand small rooms.

When those rooms empty, the practice stops. And a people that stops practicing self government will, sooner or later, forget how to do it.

[PAUSE]

Think about what we have seen in our politics over these same decades. Trust in one another and in our institutions has fallen. Our parties have hardened into tribes. Many Americans say they find it stressful even to talk politics with someone who disagrees. We increasingly know our political opponents not as neighbors, coworkers, or fellow parishioners, but as caricatures on a screen.

I cannot prove to you that the friendship recession caused all of that, and I will not pretend that I can. Politics has many causes. But I will offer you an interpretation, and I hold it as an interpretation. It is very hard to hate a person you bowl with every Tuesday. It is very easy to hate a category of people you have never met. When the institutions that once put people of different views in the same room disappeared, we lost one of the great natural cures for polarization. The fellow member. The teammate. The person you disagree with about everything, and who still shows up with a casserole when your mother dies.

The political philosopher Hannah Arendt, who lived through the darkest years of the twentieth century, argued that loneliness, the experience of not belonging to the world at all, was part of the soil in which the totalitarian movements grew. People who have lost their place among others, she suggested, become available to movements that promise them belonging at the price of their freedom.

[SLOW]

I am not saying we are on that road. I am saying that a lonely people is a vulnerable people. Vulnerable to demagogues who offer an enemy in place of a friend. Vulnerable to conspiracy theories that offer a sense of secret community. Vulnerable to online movements that give a young man a tribe and a target and call it brotherhood. Loneliness does not only make people sick. It can make them available.

[LET IT LAND]

That is the bottom of the argument. A people that has lost its friends can lose its freedom with them. If I stopped here, you would go home frightened, and you would be right to.

But I am not stopping here. Because the reverse is also true. A connected people is a resilient people. When a hurricane hits, the neighborhoods that recover fastest are often the ones where people already knew each other, where someone knew which elderly neighbor lived alone, where the church or the block association could organize in hours. When a factory closes, the towns that find their way back are often the ones with strong civic institutions that could gather people and plan. When a pandemic strikes, the communities with dense networks of trust can share information, care for the sick, and hold together.

Social connection is not only a private good. It is a public good. It is part of the infrastructure of a free and resilient nation, as surely as roads and power lines.

[BUILD]

That is why Pericles called the citizen who takes no part in public life not unambitious but useless. Not because private life is worthless. But because a city is only as strong as the web of relationships that holds it together. Each citizen who withdraws pulls out one thread. Withdraw enough threads, and the whole fabric comes apart.

And that is why the friendship recession is not a matter only for therapists and poets. It is a matter for citizens. For mayors and city councils. For pastors and principals. For employers and union leaders. For the people who design our technology and the people who write our laws.

And for you. Because the republic is not out there somewhere. It is in the room you gather in, or fail to. It is at the table you set, or leave empty. It is in the invitation you send, or do not.

Somebody used to be there, holding up their end of the common life. Somebody can be there again.

Which brings me to the fourth claim, and the reason for this whole evening. What was taken apart can be built again.

## XII. What Other Nations Tried

If loneliness is a public problem, it is fair to ask what public action looks like. And here, for once, we do not have to imagine. Other nations have already begun.

In January of two thousand eighteen, the government of the United Kingdom did something that was mocked at the time and has since been imitated. It appointed a minister for loneliness.

The story behind that appointment is worth telling. A member of Parliament named Jo Cox had made loneliness one of her causes. In two thousand sixteen, she was murdered. Her colleagues from across party lines carried on her work through a commission in her name. And in two thousand seventeen, the Jo Cox Commission on Loneliness reported that more than nine million people in the United Kingdom often or always felt lonely.

The government responded. Tracey Crouch, then the sports minister, was given the loneliness portfolio on top of her existing job. And in October of two thousand eighteen, Britain published what was described as the world's first government strategy on loneliness, with sixty commitments across nine government departments.

[PAUSE]

Sixty commitments across nine departments. That is the key detail. Britain did not treat loneliness as a problem for one office. It treated it as something that touched transport, housing, health, culture, the design of communities. That is exactly what the Surgeon General meant when he called for pro-connection policy. Ask every part of government what it is doing to bring people together or push them apart.

Japan followed. In February of two thousand twenty-one, amid a painful rise in suicides during the pandemic, Prime Minister Yoshihide Suga appointed Tetsushi Sakamoto as Japan's minister for loneliness and isolation. The stated aim was to prevent social loneliness and isolation and to protect the ties between people. That June, the British and Japanese ministers held a joint meeting to share what they had learned.

Then the World Health Organization took the issue global, launching its Commission on Social Connection in two thousand twenty-three and releasing its flagship report in two thousand twenty-five. That report laid out a roadmap built around policy, research, interventions, better measurement, and public engagement. It called on nations to invest in social infrastructure, the parks and libraries and cafes where people actually meet. And it urged individuals to do small things: reach out to a friend, put away the phone during conversation, greet a neighbor, join a local group, volunteer.

[SLOW]

A minister is not a miracle. A strategy is not a solution. Britain has had several loneliness ministers since two thousand eighteen, often holding the post alongside other duties, and nobody would claim that Britain has solved loneliness. Japan still struggles. Appointing someone to worry about a problem is not the same as fixing it.

But these efforts did something important. They named the problem as public. They said, this is the government's business, not because government can make people friends, but because government shapes the conditions in which friendship grows or dies. Government builds the roads that divide neighborhoods or the parks that join them. Government funds the libraries or closes them. Government writes the zoning that allows a corner cafe or forbids it. Government sets the rules for the platforms that consume our attention. Pretending government has nothing to do with loneliness is pretending that government has nothing to do with how we live.

And what of the United States?

Here the story is more modest. There is no American minister of loneliness. There is no federal strategy with funding behind it. The Surgeon General's advisory remains, three years on, mostly advice.

But there are beginnings. This past June, Senator Chris Murphy of Connecticut introduced two bills. One, the Addressing Social Isolation and Loneliness in Older Adults Act, would provide sixty-two and a half million dollars a year to community organizations doing outreach and support for isolated seniors. The other, the National Strategy for Social Connection Act, would expand research on loneliness at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and create an Office of Social Connection Policy within the executive branch to advise the President.

[PAUSE]

Sixty-two and a half million dollars. Let me put that number in perspective. In a federal budget measured in trillions, that is a rounding error. It is a small, careful, first step.

I do not know whether those bills will pass. Most bills do not. But notice that they exist. And notice what they propose. Not a government that makes friends for you. A government that studies the problem, coordinates a response, and supports the local organizations, the senior centers and the volunteer groups and the meal programs, that actually do the work of connection.

That is the right model. It is Tocqueville's model, really. Not the state replacing the association, but the state making room for the association to flourish.

And I would say this to every member of Congress of either party. Loneliness is not a left or right issue. It shows up in red counties and blue cities. It shows up among rural farmers and urban renters. Conservatives worry about the decline of family, faith and community. Progressives worry about the conditions of work, housing and public space. Both are describing the same collapse from different angles. Here, if anywhere, is a problem worth working on together.

Britain gave the job to a minister. Japan gave it to a minister. The world gave it to a commission. America, so far, has given it to an advisory and a pair of bills.

[BUILD]

We can do better. We have done better. The country that so astonished Tocqueville with its genius for association can surely find a way to make association easy again.

## XIII. Rebuilding the Commons

So what would it actually take to end the friendship recession? Let me be concrete, because a diagnosis without a treatment is just a sadder way of describing the disease.

Let me start with a phrase coined by the sociologist Ray Oldenburg in a book he published in nineteen eighty-nine called The Great Good Place. He argued that a healthy life needs three kinds of places. The first place is home. The second place is work. And the third place is everything else: the cafe, the pub, the barbershop, the diner, the bookstore, the park bench, the general store. The places where people gather informally, without an invitation, where there are regulars, where conversation is the main activity, where everyone is on equal footing, where the mood is playful.

Oldenburg warned, decades ago, that America was losing its third places. That we were building a country of homes and workplaces connected by highways, with nothing in between. And now, with the office emptying, many people have lost their second place too. They have one place. Home. And home, for a great many people, means alone.

[PAUSE]

So the first task of rebuilding is to rebuild the third places. And this is where public policy matters enormously, because the existence of third places is not an accident. It is shaped by law.

Start with zoning. In much of the United States, it is literally illegal to open a corner cafe or a small shop in a residential neighborhood. We separated where people live from where people gather, and then we wondered why people stopped gathering. Cities and towns can change that. They can allow small, neighborhood scale businesses, the coffee shop, the bakery, the corner store, within walking distance of homes. Not a mall you drive to. A place you walk to, where you see the same faces.

Then the street itself. A neighborhood where you cannot walk is a neighborhood where you cannot run into anyone. Sidewalks, crosswalks, benches, shade trees, front porches close to the street. These sound like small things. They are the architecture of chance encounters, and chance encounters are the raw material of friendship.

Then the public institutions we already have. The library is the last great free third place in American life. It asks nothing of you. You do not have to buy anything. You can stay as long as you like. Across this country, librarians have turned their buildings into community living rooms, hosting everything from job clinics to knitting circles to chess clubs to English classes. Fund them. Keep them open in the evenings and on weekends, when working people can come. Treat them as what they are: public health infrastructure.

Picture it, and this is an illustration, not a particular town. A branch library that stays open until nine on a Tuesday. In one corner, a retired machinist teaching two teenagers to play chess. At the long table, a conversation class where a nurse from Manila and a cook from Oaxaca practice their English on a widower who comes mostly for the company. Nobody there paid a cent. Nobody needed an invitation. And by the third Tuesday, they all know each other's names.

[SLOW]

The same goes for parks, recreation centers, community pools, public basketball courts, senior centers, community gardens. Every one of these is a place where people of different ages and backgrounds can share space without paying for the privilege. Every one of them has been on the chopping block in some budget crisis or another. The World Health Organization's commission named exactly these kinds of places, parks and libraries and cafes, as social infrastructure. Infrastructure is the right word. We do not ask whether roads turn a profit. We should not ask it of the places where a community comes together.

Then the second pillar, pro-connection policy. This means asking, of every major decision government makes, a simple question. Does this make it easier or harder for people to be in one another's lives?

Housing. When people are priced out of the neighborhoods where they grew up, they lose their networks. When young adults cannot afford to live near their friends, friendships break. When older adults cannot downsize in their own communities, they are uprooted at precisely the age when new friendships are hardest to form. Housing policy that lets people stay near the people they love is connection policy.

Transportation. A person who cannot drive, a teenager, an older adult, a person with a disability, a person who cannot afford a car, is cut off from social life in a place without public transit. Reliable buses and trains are not only an economic issue. They are a loneliness issue.

Time. Friendship requires time, and many Americans have none. Workers juggling multiple jobs, unpredictable shift schedules posted a few days in advance, no paid leave, no paid time off. You cannot join a weekly league if you do not know your hours from one week to the next. Policies that give working people predictable schedules and real time off are, among other things, policies that make friendship possible.

[PAUSE]

Then the health sector, the third pillar. Imagine a world where your doctor asked you, at every checkup, not just whether you smoke and how much you drink, but how many people you could call in an emergency. Where loneliness was treated as the risk factor the research says it is. Where a physician could refer a lonely patient to a walking group, a volunteer program, a community choir, a men's shed, the way she now refers them to physical therapy. That practice, often called social prescribing, has been tried in Britain and elsewhere. It deserves serious testing here.

And then the schools. If the young are where the decline is steepest, the schools are where rebuilding has to begin. Recess. Clubs. Sports for everyone, not just the most talented. Drama, band, debate, robotics. Service projects that put young people in their communities. Phone free school days, so that the hallway and the lunchroom can do what they have always done, which is teach young people how to be with each other.

[BUILD]

Let me be very clear about what I am not proposing. I am not proposing that government become your friend. Government cannot do that. It should not try. A friendship arranged by a federal agency is not a friendship.

What I am proposing is that government stop making friendship harder, and start making the conditions for it easier. Build the places. Allow the cafes. Fund the libraries. Run the buses. Protect people's time. Train the doctors. Open the schools after hours. And then get out of the way, and let people do what people have always done when they are given a place to gather. Let them make their own associations, a thousand kinds, serious and silly, huge and tiny, just as Tocqueville saw them do.

The Gilded Age generation did not wait for a government program to found the associations of the twentieth century. But they did benefit from public parks, public libraries, public schools, public squares. The commons made the associations possible. The associations made the commons alive.

That is the partnership we need again. Public places. Private initiative. Common life.

## XIV. Rebuilding Ourselves

Now from the public to the personal. There is a part of this that no law can do. No zoning code can make you send the text. No library can make you walk through its doors. No bill in Congress can make you say yes to the invitation, or make you be the one who issues it.

The friendship recession is structural. But it is also, in the end, made of millions of small decisions. And it will be ended, if it is ended, by millions of small decisions in the other direction.

So let me offer you a few. Not as a self help program. As a civic duty.

[PAUSE]

The first is the standing invitation. The single most powerful thing the old institutions did was make gathering automatic. Recurring. Same time, same place, every week, whether or not anyone felt like it. You can rebuild that in your own life.

Let me give you an example. Imagine, and this is an illustration, not a real family, a couple in their forties who decide that every Tuesday night, their door is open for dinner. Not a fancy dinner. A pot of soup, a loaf of bread. Anybody they know is welcome. No need to RSVP. Bring something or bring nothing. Some weeks, two people come. Some weeks, twelve. Some weeks, nobody. But they keep doing it. And over a year, over two years, something happens. People begin to know each other. A neighbor meets a coworker meets an old friend from college. The table becomes a little institution. And when one of the regulars gets sick, the Tuesday crowd organizes the meals. When one of them loses a job, the Tuesday crowd knows someone who is hiring.

That is how institutions begin. Not with a charter. With a habit.

The second is to join something and keep going. Researchers who study friendship find, again and again, that it simply takes time. Many hours together, spread over weeks and months. The first meeting of any group is awkward. The second is slightly less awkward. By the tenth, you are a regular. Most people quit after the first or second. Do not quit. Join the choir, the running club, the volunteer fire company, the community garden, the trivia team, the book club, the congregation, the softball league. And go back. The going back is the whole thing.

[SLOW]

The third is to host. Hospitality has quietly declined in American life. We worry the house is not clean enough, the food is not good enough, we do not have time. But nobody remembers whether your floors were clean. They remember that you invited them. Lower the bar. Order the pizza. Open the door.

The fourth is to be the one who reaches out. Most of us are waiting for someone else to make the first move. Most of us assume the other person is busy, or not interested, or has enough friends already. And I suspect most of us badly underestimate how glad the other person will be to hear from us. So be the one. Send the text. Make the call. Say, I was thinking about you, let's get lunch.

The fifth is to say the true thing. Especially to the men, and I include myself. When your friend asks how you are, try, once in a while, telling him. Not every time. Not a flood. But a little more than fine. Friendship deepens when people let themselves be known. And you may be surprised how often the other person was waiting for permission to say the true thing too.

[PAUSE]

The sixth is to give. Remember Pericles. We acquire friends by conferring favors, not by receiving them. The surest way into a community is to serve it. Volunteer. Bring the meal. Drive the neighbor to the appointment. Coach the team. Shovel the elderly widow's walk. Friendship does not only lead to generosity. Generosity leads to friendship. Service is the side door into belonging, and it is always open.

The seventh is to put the phone down when someone is in front of you. Not because the phone is evil. Because the person in front of you deserves your attention, and attention is the most basic form of love.

And the eighth is to notice who is missing. Every community has people at the edges. The new family on the block. The widower who stopped coming to church. The coworker who never joins the group at lunch. The young man who moved to town for a job and knows nobody. The old woman on the third floor whose groceries are delivered and who has not had a visitor in months. Look for them. Invite them. Be the person who notices.

[BUILD]

I know how small these things sound. A pot of soup. A text message. A second trip to the choir rehearsal. They sound almost embarrassingly small next to the vast forces I described earlier. The collapse of institutions. The emptying of offices. The redesign of attention.

But think about how the friendship recession happened. Not in one catastrophe. In a thousand small subtractions. A league that folded. A diner that closed. A Friday night spent scrolling. A call not made. An invitation not issued. A neighbor never met.

If it came apart by small subtractions, it can be put back together by small additions. One table. One invitation. One regular. One association. Multiplied across a country of three hundred and forty million people.

Somebody used to be there. And the only way somebody will be there again is if somebody decides to be.

Why not you?

## XV. Set the Table

So let me close where I began. With a chair.

There is a man, the one I asked you to picture at the start. Thirty-eight years old. A spare bedroom office and a ring light. Four hundred contacts in his phone. A Friday night that looks like a Tuesday. A chair across the table that nobody has sat in for a long time.

I told you he was not real. But you know he is real. You know him. Perhaps you are him. Perhaps you are his sister, who lives three states away and keeps meaning to call. Perhaps you are his old college roommate, who thinks about him every few months and never quite sends the text. Perhaps you are his neighbor, who has lived on the other side of the wall for four years and does not know his name.

And perhaps you are the woman down the hall, widowed last spring, whose children call on Sundays and whose other six days are silent. Perhaps you are the nineteen year old in the dorm room, who has a thousand followers and no one to eat dinner with. Perhaps you are the father who moved for the job and has not made a single friend in the new city. Perhaps you are the retiree whose whole social world was the office, and who discovered on his last day that it went away with the badge.

Somebody used to be there for every one of them.

[SLOW]

Pericles stood over the graves of the Athenian dead and told the living what their city was. A school for all of Greece, he called it. A place where people gathered and argued and celebrated together, where friends were won by giving, where the citizen who withdrew was counted useless, and where the common life was worth defending.

I cannot stand over our dead tonight. But the World Health Organization tells us that loneliness is part of the story of about a hundred deaths an hour. And our own Surgeon General has told us that disconnection is as dangerous to our bodies as smoking.

So let me ask the question Pericles answered. What is our city? What is our common life? What is the thing we share that is worth defending?

If the answer is a screen in every room, a delivery at every door, and a chair at every table that nobody sits in, then we have built a country of comfortable strangers. And no people in history has ever been made great by comfort alone.

But that is not the whole answer. It does not have to be. Because there is another American tradition, older and deeper than our isolation. The tradition Tocqueville saw. The genius for association. The barn raising and the church supper and the volunteer fire company. The union local and the bowling league and the PTA. The tradition of a people who, when the old communities collapsed in the Gilded Age, sat down and invented new ones.

That tradition is not dead. It is waiting.

[BUILD]

So here is what I ask.

Set the table. Set it this week, not someday. Put out an extra chair, and fill it.

Set the table for the neighbor whose name you never learned. Set the table for the friend you have not called in a year. Set the table for the young man down the street who looks like he has nowhere to be. Set the table for the widow on the third floor.

Set the table in your town. Go to the council meeting and fight for the library hours, the park, the bus line, the corner cafe. Ask every candidate who wants your vote what they will do to make your community a place where people can find one another.

Set the table at work. If you run a company, give your people a reason to be in a room together that is not a meeting. If you are a worker, eat lunch with someone.

Set the table in your school. Put the phones away. Give the kids back the hallway, the lunchroom, the club, the team.

Set the table in your congregation, your league, your lodge, your choir. And if your town has none of those, set the table by starting one.

Set the table in Congress. Pass the modest bills. Fund the research. Name the problem in law, as Japan has done. Give it someone in charge, as Britain and Japan have done. Make the commons possible again.

And set the table in your own heart. Decide that you will be the one who reaches out first. That you will say the true thing when your friend asks how you are. That you will give before you receive, because that is how friends are won. That you will go back to the group the second time, and the third, and the tenth, until you are a regular. That you will notice who is missing.

Remember the one sentence I promised to keep coming back to. We did not stop needing one another. We let the places where we found one another disappear. And what was taken apart can be built again.

The friendship recession was not decreed by fate. It was built, slowly, by a thousand choices, public and private, made one at a time without anyone quite noticing. It can be unbuilt the same way. One table. One invitation. One association. One neighborhood. One town.

[SLOW]

So picture him one more time. Nine o'clock on a Friday night. The man at the kitchen table, with the chair across from him.

And this time his phone lights up, and it is not an app. It is a person. The message says: come over, I'm making dinner, bring nothing.

He stands up. He finds his keys. And somewhere across town, a chair that has been empty for a long time is about to be filled.

[PAUSE]

Somebody used to be there. At the table, in the pew, on the team, at the next desk, on the porch, across the fence, at the bedside.

Somebody can be there again. All it takes is for somebody to decide.

Count the chairs. Then fill one.

[LET IT LAND]

Let it be us. Let it be you. Let it be tonight.

Set the table.
