# The Empty Cradle

## I. The Empty Classroom

Picture a school in the countryside of North Gyeongsang, in the south-east of the Republic of Korea, on the first school day of March.

The building is in good repair. The floors have been waxed. There are coat hooks at child height and a row of small chairs. Somebody has drawn a welcome sign on the board in coloured chalk. Under it, in a teacher's careful hand: we were expecting you.

And no one comes.

[PAUSE]

That school is one I asked you to imagine. The numbers behind it are not imagined.

In the spring of 2024, one hundred and twelve elementary schools across South Korea had no new first graders at all. Not a single child.

In 2025 the education ministry said forty-nine schools would close that year. Thirty-eight of them were elementary schools. Over the years the country has shut more than four thousand.

Think about what a first-grade class is. It is a promise a society makes to itself. It says there will be readers, nurses, carpenters, poets and voters in twenty years. It says someone will sit in these chairs, and someone after them.

When the chairs stay empty, the promise is not broken in one dramatic moment. It is simply not renewed.

[LET IT LAND]

My friends, I have come to talk about the empty cradle.

I mean the plain fact that across most of the world, rich and middle-income, democratic and one-party, Catholic and Confucian and secular, people are having fewer children than it takes to replace themselves. In some places, far fewer.

I will give you the numbers later, and there are many. For now I want to give you something harder to hold than a number. I want to give you a sense of scale.

So listen to one year.

In 2025, Japan recorded fewer births to its own citizens than in any year since its records began in 1899.

In 2025, Italy recorded its fewest births since the Second World War.

In 2025, France, for decades Europe's demographic exception, recorded more deaths than births for the first time since that war.

In 2025, China's population fell for the fourth year running. It registered fewer births than in any year since its records began in 1949.

And in 2025, the United States hit the lowest general fertility rate in its recorded history.

One year. The year we have just lived through.

Now I must be careful from the very first minute, because this subject draws exaggeration the way a magnet draws iron filings.

Humanity is not going extinct. Nobody serious thinks the last baby is about to be born. The world population today is larger than it has ever been, and according to the United Nations it will keep growing for roughly another sixty years before it peaks.

So this is not a speech about the end of the human race.

It is about something quieter, slower, and in its way stranger: the first time in modern history that humanity will choose, through billions of private decisions, to grow smaller. It is about a civilizational shrinking. It is about countries growing older than any countries have ever been. It is about towns that empty out, schools that close, houses where nobody lives, and pension systems built on the arithmetic of a world that no longer exists.

And it is about the gap, which I will show you is real and measurable, between the number of children people say they want and the number they actually have.

That gap is the heart of this speech.

[SLOW]

Because if people simply wanted fewer children, and had them, freely and gladly, then I would have little to say. A free people may choose to be a smaller people. That is their right.

But that is not what the evidence shows. What it shows is a world full of people who wanted two children and had one, who wanted one and had none, and who waited for a stable job, an affordable home, a partner who would share the work, a moment that never came.

That is not freedom fulfilled. It is freedom unfinished.

So I have come to make three arguments, and I will tell you each time we pass from one to the next.

The first is about the facts. The fall in fertility is real, deep and nearly worldwide, and we should describe it accurately, without panic and without denial.

The second is about the stakes. The consequences are serious but manageable, if we start now, and only if we start now.

The third is about the duty. What we owe the children who might yet be born is not a lecture, a quota or a guilt trip. It is a society in which having a family is once again a reasonable thing for an ordinary person to do.

Facts. Stakes. Duty.

That duty is the promise every generation makes to the next. The welcome sign on the chalkboard is a small version of it. Four words in chalk: we were expecting you.

Tonight I want to ask whether we still mean them.

## II. What a Funeral Oration Is For

In the winter of the first year of the Peloponnesian War, the Athenians held a public funeral for the men who had fallen in the opening campaigns.

Thucydides, the historian, tells us how it was done. The bones of the dead were laid out in a tent for three days before the ceremony. Cypress coffins, one for each tribe, were carried in procession, with an empty bier for the missing whose bodies had not been found. And then a man chosen by the city climbed onto a platform to speak.

The man was Pericles.

[PAUSE]

You might ask why a speech about babies borrows its shape from a speech about the dead.

Here is the answer. Pericles did not spend most of his time talking about the dead. He spent it talking about the city: what Athens was, what kind of life it made possible, why it was worth handing on. He praised the ancestors who, as he put it, handed the country down free from generation to generation. Then he praised the fathers who added to what they had received. Only after all of that did he turn to the fallen, and finally to the living and what they owed.

A funeral oration, rightly understood, is not about death. It is about inheritance. It asks what we received, what we made of it, and what we will leave.

That is exactly the question the empty cradle puts to us.

There is one passage in that speech I cannot stop thinking about. Near the end, Pericles turns to the grieving parents in the crowd. To those still young enough, he says, in the old Crawley translation, that they "must bear up in the hope of having others in their stead."

Then he adds a reason, and it is a political reason. He says that "never can a fair or just policy be expected of the citizen who does not, like his fellows, bring to the decision the interests and apprehensions of a father."

[PAUSE]

Now, I will be honest with you about the limits of that line, because Pericles' honesty about his city did not extend to everyone in it. He was speaking to men. He spoke of fathers, not mothers. Women in Athens could not vote, and the speech gives them one cold sentence of advice. Athens kept slaves. The man who gave this speech was leading his city into a war that would end in its ruin.

So I do not hold Pericles up as a saint. I hold him up as a witness to one idea that is older than him and still true.

The idea is this. A society makes better decisions when the people making them have a stake in the future, when they are thinking about someone who will still be here in fifty years.

That stake does not have to come from being a parent. Teachers have it. Grandparents and aunts and uncles have it. So does anyone who plants a tree whose shade they will never sit in. But a society in which fewer and fewer people have children, and fewer and fewer people live near children, will slowly lose some of that instinct to look ahead. Its politics will drift toward the present, toward the people who already exist and vote, and away from the people who do not yet exist and cannot.

That is one of the hidden costs of the empty cradle. It does not only shrink the future. It quiets the future's voice in the room where decisions are made.

[PAUSE]

There is a darker footnote to that speech, and I think we should hear it.

In the very next chapter of his history, Thucydides turns to the following summer, and to the plague that struck Athens. The city Pericles had just praised so proudly was ravaged by disease. Pericles himself died within a few years. The war he had led his city into dragged on for decades and ended in defeat.

I do not tell you this to be gloomy. I tell you because it is a lesson in humility. Pericles stood on that platform and spoke of a city that would be admired by succeeding ages, and it was. But he could not see what was coming the following summer. None of us can.

We should speak about the future with the same mixture of confidence and humility. Confident that our choices matter. Humble about how much we can foresee.

Two thousand three hundred years after Pericles, on a battlefield in Pennsylvania, another leader dedicated a cemetery.

Abraham Lincoln spoke for only a couple of minutes. The famous orator who went before him had spoken for about two hours. Lincoln's address was fewer than three hundred words long, and it outlived nearly everything else said that year.

Lincoln did what Pericles did, in miniature. He began with the founders: "Four score and seven years ago our fathers brought forth on this continent, a new nation." He moved to the present struggle. And then he turned, as Pericles turned, to the living: "It is for us the living, rather, to be dedicated here to the unfinished work."

Why was Lincoln's speech so short, and why did the short speech last?

I think it is because he understood that at a graveside, long explanations are an insult. The dead had already said everything that needed saying. So he said only what the living needed to hear: where they came from, what was at stake, and what was now asked of them.

He even admitted the limits of his own words. "We can not dedicate," he said, "we can not consecrate, we can not hallow this ground." The men who struggled there had done that already. Words could not add to it.

I am going to speak to you for much longer than Lincoln did, because the subject requires evidence and argument, and because I owe you both. But I will try to keep his discipline in one respect. At the end, I will ask only what the living can actually do.

[LET IT LAND]

Unfinished work. That is the phrase I want to carry through this speech.

Every society is unfinished work. It is a relay, not a monument. It exists only as long as one generation hands it to the next. The Athenians understood that. Lincoln understood it. His closing hope, that government of the people, by the people, for the people, "shall not perish from the earth", was not a hope about one generation. It was a hope about all the generations to come.

Here is what I ask you to notice. Both of those speeches assumed there would be a next generation to receive the inheritance. Pericles could tell grieving parents to have more children. Lincoln could speak of a nation's future without wondering whether there would be enough young people to carry it.

We cannot assume that so easily any more.

[PAUSE]

I am not saying the next generation will vanish. I have already told you it will not. I am saying it will be smaller than the one before it, in country after country, and in some places much smaller. The relay is still running, but in many places fewer hands are reaching for the baton.

So this is a strange kind of funeral oration. It is not for the dead. It is for the people who are not yet born, and for some who, as things stand, never will be.

We cannot dedicate a cemetery to them. We can do something better. We can dedicate ourselves to building a world they would be glad to be born into, and one that the people who want to bring them into it can actually afford to live in.

## III. Two Point One

So we begin with the first argument: the facts. Before we go around the world, we need one number. Everything else in this speech hangs on it.

The number is two point one.

Demographers use a measure called the total fertility rate. Strictly, it is the average number of children a woman would have over her life if she experienced, at each age, the birth rates of a given year. In plain terms, it is roughly how many children each woman is having, on average, right now.

Why is two point one the magic number? Because it takes two people to make a child. For a population to hold steady over the long run, without immigration, each woman needs on average about two children: one to replace herself, one to replace a partner. The extra tenth covers the fact that slightly more boys are born than girls, and that some children do not survive to adulthood.

In countries where child mortality is high, the replacement rate is higher than two point one. In rich countries with very low child mortality, it is very close to it.

Above two point one, a population grows over time. Below it, a population eventually shrinks, unless people move in from elsewhere.

That is the whole idea. It is simple, and it is relentless.

[SLOW]

Now here is how the world has moved.

In the nineteen-sixties, the average woman on Earth had more than five children. According to the United Nations' latest World Population Prospects, published in 2024, the global average is now about two point two five. In 1990 it was about three point three.

In one human lifetime, the average family on this planet has gone from five children to a little over two.

One lifetime. Five to two.

And the world average hides something. It mixes the regions where fertility is still high, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa, with the much larger regions where it is already far below replacement. The UN reports that in more than half of all countries and areas, fertility is now below two point one.

Europe, the Americas and Asia as a whole are now at or below about two children per woman. Africa, which averaged almost seven children per woman in the nineteen-seventies, is now at almost four, and the UN expects it to fall below three by mid-century.

[BUILD]

The other great forecast comes from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation, published in The Lancet in March 2024. Its authors looked at two hundred and four countries and territories.

In 2021, they found, ninety-four of those countries still had fertility above replacement. By 2050 they forecast forty-nine. By 2100, in their reference scenario, just six.

Six out of two hundred and four.

Their forecast for the world as a whole is a fertility rate of about one point eight three in 2050 and about one point six in 2100.

Now a word of fairness about these numbers, because forecasts are not prophecies.

The UN and the Lancet team use different methods, and they disagree about how fast and how far fertility will fall. The Lancet forecast is the more pessimistic of the two. Both come with wide ranges of uncertainty. Forecasting fertility eighty years ahead is hard. No one in 1950 predicted the world we have now, and no one today can be sure of the world of 2100.

But here is what matters. On the direction, they agree. On the basic fact that most of humanity now lives in societies with below-replacement fertility, they agree. And on the conclusion that world population will peak within this century and then begin to decline, they now agree too.

That last point is new. A decade ago it was still a minority view.

I want to stay a moment on why two point one is such an unforgiving line.

When fertility falls a little below replacement, say to one point eight or one point nine, the shrinking is gentle. Each generation is perhaps ten percent smaller than the one before. Immigration can easily make that up. Many countries have lived there for decades without much trouble.

But fertility does not fall in a straight line. When it falls to one point three, or one point one, or below one, the arithmetic turns harsh. At a fertility rate of one, each generation is roughly half the size of the one before. Half the parents. Then a quarter of the grandparents. Then an eighth.

That is the compounding of absence. It is interest paid in reverse.

There is one more feature of this arithmetic that makes the problem stubborn. Demographers call it population momentum, and in the shrinking direction some call it a low-fertility trap.

When a country has had very low fertility for a generation, it has fewer women of childbearing age in the next generation. So even if every one of them had more children than her mother did, the total number of births could still fall, simply because there are fewer mothers.

Japan's health ministry pointed to exactly this when it explained the country's 2025 record low. It named fewer young women first.

In other words, low fertility today does not only mean fewer children today. It means fewer parents tomorrow.

[BUILD]

Why did this happen? Why, across so many different cultures, did families shrink so far and so fast?

Demographers describe a pattern they call the demographic transition, and it begins with good news. For most of human history, many children died young. Families had many children partly because they expected to lose some of them. Then came clean water, vaccines, antibiotics and better nutrition. Children stopped dying in such numbers. A generation or two later, parents stopped having so many.

Then came the move from farm to city. On a farm, a child is an extra pair of hands from an early age. In a city apartment, a child is a long and expensive investment: years of school, years of dependence. What was once a source of labour became a source of cost.

Then came schooling for girls, and work for women outside the home, and reliable contraception. For the first time in history, women in large numbers could decide when and whether to have children, and they could build lives and careers of their own. That was a liberation, and I will not hear it called anything else.

Then came pensions and savings and the welfare state. In the old world, your children were your retirement plan. They would feed you when you could no longer work. In the modern world, the state and the market took over that role. A person could grow old safely without children of their own. That too was a great achievement. But it quietly removed one of the oldest reasons to have a family.

For a long time, demographers assumed the transition would end in balance. Fertility would fall to about two, and stay there, and population would level off at a new, stable plateau. That was the theory taught for decades.

The theory was wrong about the ending. In country after country, fertility did not stop at two. It went through two, and kept going. Nobody has a complete explanation for why. Some point to the cost of housing and education. Some point to the long years now spent in study and early career. Some point to changes in how young people meet, date and form couples at all. Some point to a deeper cultural shift, in which having children has become one choice among many, rather than the expected shape of a life.

I suspect all of these play a part. And I will offer you one observation of my own. The forces that drove the first half of the transition were mostly gains: fewer dead children, more educated women, safer old age. The forces that seem to be driving fertility below replacement look much more like obstacles: costs, delays, insecurity and exhaustion.

That distinction will matter a great deal for what I propose later. We should never try to reverse the gains. But we can work to remove the obstacles.

[LET IT LAND]

So when I say two point one, I am not reciting a technicality. I am describing a line, and on one side of it a society renews itself. On the other side, if it stays there long enough and far enough below, it slowly hollows out.

Most of the world has now crossed that line.

Let me take you to the places furthest across it.

## IV. The Edge of the Map: Korea and Taiwan

If you want to see where the rest of the developed world may be heading, look at South Korea and Taiwan. They are not outliers in kind. They are outliers in degree. They are what the general trend looks like when every pressure behind it is turned up to maximum.

Start with Korea.

In 2015, South Korea's total fertility rate was one point two four. That was already very low, lower than almost anywhere in Europe. Then it kept falling. By 2023 it had reached zero point seven two, the lowest figure recorded for any country in the world.

Zero point seven two. Not two point one. Not one point five. Under one.

At that rate, every hundred Korean adults of one generation would have, on average, about thirty-six children. Each generation would be roughly a third the size of its parents'.

[SLOW]

In Seoul, the capital, a city of extraordinary energy and wealth, the figure was lower still. Even in 2025, after a modest recovery, Seoul's fertility rate stood at just zero point six three.

Now I must tell you the latest news, because honesty requires it and because it matters.

Korea's numbers have turned up. In 2024 the fertility rate rose to zero point seven five. Preliminary figures for 2025 put it at about zero point eight. Births rose almost seven percent, to about two hundred and fifty-four thousand. That is the first sustained rise in years, and it came faster than even the government's optimistic projection.

That is good news, and I will not take it away from the Korean people. A recovery from zero point seven two to zero point eight is real.

But keep it in proportion. Zero point eight is still among the very lowest fertility rates reported anywhere on Earth. It is still well under half of replacement. Scholars in Korea have cautioned that part of the rise may reflect changes in the make-up of the population, such as how many people happen to be in their early thirties right now, rather than a change in the underlying pattern.

And look at who is having the children. The average Korean mother is now nearly thirty-four when she gives birth. More than a third of all babies are born to mothers aged thirty-five or older.

When the first child arrives at thirty-four, the second has very little time. The third almost none.

[BUILD]

There is an irony in Korea's story that I think we should sit with for a moment.

Within living memory, Korea's government worried that the country had too many children, not too few. From the nineteen-sixties into the nineteen-nineties, it ran family planning campaigns urging couples to have fewer children. Those campaigns began in a poor country, recovering from war, where large families were the norm.

They succeeded beyond anyone's imagination. And then the decline simply kept going, long after the campaigns had ended and long after the government had reversed course and begun pleading for more births.

The lesson is not that family planning was wrong. It gave millions of Korean women control over their lives. The lesson is that once a society's expectations about family size change, they do not easily change back. A government can push a birth rate down far more easily than it can pull it up again.

Why Korea? Here I must be careful to separate evidence from opinion.

The explanations most often given, by Korean researchers, by journalists and by Koreans themselves, are these. Housing in and around Seoul, where about half the country lives, is extremely expensive. Education is a fierce competition, and families spend heavily on private cram schools to keep their children in the race. Working hours are long. And many women report that marriage and motherhood still mean shouldering most of the housework and childcare, often at the cost of a career.

When the United Nations Population Fund surveyed people in fourteen countries in 2025, more than half of the Korean respondents said money worries had led them, or might lead them, to have fewer children than they wanted.

[PAUSE]

Put those together and you get a society that has done something remarkable. In two generations it went from poverty to the front rank of the world's economies. It built the schools and the hospitals and the factories and the culture that the world now admires.

And somewhere along the way, it made having a child feel, to many young Koreans, like a luxury they could not afford and a risk they could not take.

The empty classroom in North Gyeongsang is one result.

The pension system is another. In March 2025, after years of argument, Korea's National Assembly passed its first pension reform in eighteen years.

Contributions will rise from nine percent of income to thirteen. The rise comes in steps of half a point a year, from 2026 to 2033.

And even so, the fund is only expected to last until the mid-twenty-sixties, not the mid-twenty-fifties. All that effort buys about nine years.

Nine years is not nothing. But it is a bridge, not a destination.

Now cross the strait to Taiwan.

In August 2026, Taiwan's National Development Council reported that the island's fertility rate in 2025 had fallen to zero point six nine five. That is among the lowest figures ever reported for any country or territory, below even Korea's worst year. It was far below the council's own earlier estimate of zero point eight seven.

Taiwan recorded about one hundred and seven thousand, eight hundred births in 2025. That was the tenth straight year of decline and a record low.

[PAUSE]

Hear the council's own projections. Taiwan's population, about twenty-three million today, is projected to fall below twenty million by 2045, and to about twelve million by 2075. Nearly half the people gone within fifty years.

The working-age population, the people between fifteen and sixty-four who staff the hospitals, build the semiconductors and pay the taxes, is projected to shrink by two-thirds over the same period.

Two-thirds.

[LET IT LAND]

These numbers can make a speaker sound like a doom-monger, so hear the caveat. Projections fifty years out are uncertain. Fertility could recover. Migration could change. Taiwan and Korea are inventive, wealthy and determined societies, and they are not standing still.

But a projection is a mirror held up to the present. It tells you where today's behaviour leads if nothing changes. The Taiwanese government did not publish that figure to frighten its people. It published it so that they would change something.

That is what honest numbers are for.

Korea and Taiwan stand at the edge of the map. They show us that there is no natural floor, no point at which fertility automatically stops falling and bounces back. Rates below one were once almost unheard of for a whole nation in peacetime. They are now the measured reality for tens of millions of people.

If it can happen there, it can happen elsewhere. In fact it already is.

## V. The Long Afternoon of Japan

If Korea and Taiwan are the edge of the map, Japan is the place that has lived on the frontier longest.

Japan's population of its own citizens peaked in 2009. It has fallen every year since.

On the first of January 2026, there were about one hundred and nineteen point seven million Japanese nationals. In a single year the number had fallen by about three-quarters of one percent.

That was the seventeenth straight year of decline, and the sharpest drop on record.

In 2025, about six hundred and seventy-one thousand babies were born to Japanese citizens. That is the lowest number since the country began keeping these records in 1899. It was the tenth straight year in which the number of births set a new record low.

The fertility rate fell to one point one four, another record.

And here is the sentence from the health ministry I want you to remember. The ministry said the decline in births is running about fifteen years ahead of the official projections made by Japan's own national population research institute.

Fifteen years ahead.

[SLOW]

Think about what that means for a government. You plan schools, hospitals, pensions, military recruitment, regional railways and tax revenue on the basis of a forecast. And you discover that reality is arriving fifteen years earlier than your most careful experts expected.

That is not a failure of the experts. They were not careless. It is a measure of how fast this change is moving, and of how consistently, all over the world, forecasters have assumed that fertility would level off, and how consistently it has kept falling instead.

There is a lesson in this for every country, including our own. Official population forecasts are among the most consequential numbers a government produces. They determine how much a pension system says it can promise, how many teachers are trained, how many hospital beds are built.

When those forecasts assume a recovery in fertility that never comes, every institution built on them is quietly overcommitted. Japan is finding that out year by year. Others will too, unless they start building their plans on what is actually happening rather than on what they hope will happen.

Japan's experience also shows that the shrinking is not spread evenly. In 2025, the prefecture with the highest fertility was Okinawa, at about one point five. The lowest was Tokyo, at under one.

The great city draws young people in from the countryside with the promise of work and excitement. And then, in its small apartments, long commutes and long hours, they have very few children. The countryside loses its young, and the city does not replace them.

[BUILD]

Now let me show you what a shrinking country looks like on the ground.

In 2023, Japan's government counted the nation's housing. It found a record nine million vacant homes. That is about one in every seven homes in the country, thirteen point eight percent.

Some of those are flats for rent between tenants, or holiday homes. But close to four million of them, a number that has nearly doubled in twenty years, are what the survey calls homes with no designated use. Houses nobody is renting, nobody is selling, nobody is living in.

The ministry gave the main reason. These are, increasingly, homes left empty when an elderly owner dies or moves into care.

Picture one of those houses. A wooden house in a mountain village. A garden going to seed. A family altar still in the front room. The grandmother lived there until she could not. Her children live in Osaka or Tokyo or Nagoya, and they have their own small flats and their own one child, or none. Nobody wants to move back. Nobody can afford to tear it down. And so the house stands there, gently falling apart.

The Japanese have a word for these houses. They call them akiya.

[PAUSE]

Multiply that house by four million and you begin to see a country that is not collapsing, but slowly closing rooms. A village loses its school, then its clinic, then its bus service, then its shop. The last shopkeeper retires. The festival is held with fewer and fewer people, until one year it is not held at all.

None of this is a catastrophe in the way a war or an earthquake is a catastrophe. There is no single day you can point to. That is precisely what makes it so hard to respond to.

And yet Japan is also a lesson in resilience.

Japan has been living with low fertility and an aging population for longer than almost anyone. It has not fallen into chaos. Its streets are safe. Its life expectancy is among the highest on Earth. Its people continue to work longer, to adopt robots and automation in care homes and factories, to experiment with compact cities and new forms of regional life. It has opened its doors, more than many expected, to foreign workers.

In 2025 the number of marriages rose modestly, for the second year in a row. That is a small thing, but small things matter.

[PAUSE]

So Japan is not a horror story. It is a long afternoon. The light is still good. The work still gets done. But the shadows are getting longer, and everyone can see them.

The question for Japan, and for every country following it, is whether the afternoon can be made to last, and whether some new morning can be brought about, or whether we simply accept the evening.

[LET IT LAND]

The ministry said it plainly. The decline is running fifteen years ahead of schedule.

We do not have the time we thought we had.

## VI. The Old Continent

Now let us turn west, to Europe, the continent where the modern decline in fertility began more than a century and a half ago, and where it has now reached new depths.

Begin with Italy, a country whose very image, in the imagination of the world, is a big, loud, loving family around a long table.

In 2025, Italy recorded about three hundred and fifty-five thousand births. That is the lowest number since the Second World War. In 2008, the year the global financial crisis struck, Italy recorded about five hundred and seventy-six thousand births. Since then the number has fallen almost every year.

The country's fertility rate in 2025 was one point one four, down from one point one eight the year before.

[SLOW]

Meanwhile about six hundred and fifty-two thousand Italians died. More deaths than births, by nearly three hundred thousand in a single year.

Italy's population has held roughly steady, at about fifty-nine million, only because of immigration. Hundreds of thousands of people moved to Italy in 2025, even as many young Italians moved abroad.

Picture a maternity ward in a small Italian hospital. A generation ago it was crowded: mothers in every bed, fathers pacing in the corridor, grandmothers bringing food. Today there are a handful of births a week. The midwives are mostly in their fifties. When one retires, the position may not be filled. In time, the regional health service will decide that the unit is too small to be safe or efficient, and the mothers of the valley will drive an hour to give birth.

That is not a dramatic event. No headline marks it. But it is how a society's capacity to welcome children wears away: one ward, one school, one playground at a time. And once that capacity is gone, it is far harder to bring back than it was to keep.

[PAUSE]

And now here is the finding I most want you to hear about Italy. In July 2026, a foundation devoted to the birth rate published a study, produced with Italy's national statistics institute. Its survey found that the collapse in births is not happening because Italians have stopped wanting to be parents. People pointed instead to economic obstacles, to their working conditions, and to social barriers as the main reasons they did not have more children.

Hold on to that. We will come back to it again and again.

Now go north and west, to France.

For decades, France was Europe's success story. Its fertility rate hovered near two when most of its neighbours had fallen far below. It invested heavily in childcare, in family allowances, in preschool for every child. Demographers wrote papers about the French exception.

In 2025, France recorded about six hundred and forty-five thousand births and about six hundred and fifty-one thousand deaths.

For the first time since the end of the Second World War, more French people died than were born.

[PAUSE]

The fertility rate fell to one point five six, the lowest since the First World War. And here is a detail I think about. France's pension forecasts had assumed a fertility rate of one point eight. The reality is a third of a child per woman lower. Across tens of millions of people and decades of time, that third of a child is an enormous number.

France's population still grew, to about sixty-nine million, because of immigration. Life expectancy rose again. More than one in five French people are now sixty-five or older.

Now go to the far north, to the Nordic countries.

If any societies should have solved this problem, it is these. They have generous parental leave, including leave reserved for fathers. They have high-quality public childcare. They have gender equality that the rest of the world studies and envies. For years, people pointed to the Nordics and said: this is how you keep fertility up. Make it easy for women to work and have children at the same time.

And for a while, that seemed to work.

Then, after about 2010, Nordic fertility began to fall too. In 2024, Finland's fertility rate was one point two five, the lowest since Finnish records began in 1776. Since before the American Revolution.

[PAUSE]

In 2025 Finland's rate rose a little, to one point three. Again, I report the good news as faithfully as the bad. But one point three in a country with some of the most family-friendly policies on Earth tells us something sobering.

It tells us that good family policy is necessary, but not sufficient. It is the floor, not the ceiling. It may stop things being worse than they would be, but it has not, on its own, brought fertility back to replacement anywhere.

And then there is Hungary, which has tried something quite different.

Hungary's government made raising the birth rate a national priority. It built a battery of incentives for married couples: loans that are forgiven as children arrive, tax exemptions for mothers of several children, housing subsidies. Its spending on family support is often put at around five percent of national output, though estimates vary depending on what you count.

For a while, the results looked impressive. Hungary's fertility rate rose from about one point two three in 2011 to about one point six around 2020 and 2021.

Then it fell again. By 2024 it was one point three nine. Births dropped by more than nine percent in a single year.

There is a real debate among scholars about what happened. Some argue Hungary's policies did raise fertility. Others argue they mostly moved births earlier in people's lives, without changing how many children families had in the end. I do not think the evidence has settled that question, and I will not pretend it has.

But I will say this much. Even the most aggressive pro-natal spending in Europe has not lifted a country back to replacement.

And there is a cruel twist in Europe's south and east. Many of the young people who might have started families at home have left to find work elsewhere, in Germany, in Britain, in the Netherlands, in America. Their home regions lose not only their births but their future parents.

Picture a small town in the hills of southern Italy. The church bells still ring. The old men still sit in the piazza. But the school has merged with the one in the next valley, and the young doctor who grew up there now works in Munich. When she has children, they will speak German first.

There is nothing wrong with her choice. She went where the work was. But add up ten thousand such choices and a region slowly empties of the young.

[LET IT LAND]

So look at the old continent as a whole. Catholic Italy and secular France. Generous Finland and pro-natal Hungary. Different cultures, different politics, different policies.

The same direction.

Whatever is happening is bigger than any single culture or any single government. And that means the answer, too, will have to be bigger than any single policy.

## VII. The Great Turn: China, India, Chile

Everything I have described so far concerns rich countries. You might suppose that falling fertility is a disease of affluence, a luxury problem of nations that have grown comfortable.

That supposition is now out of date.

[PAUSE]

Consider China, the most dramatic demographic story on Earth.

For most of the last half-century, the world worried about China's numbers going up. The government imposed its notorious one-child policy to hold them down.

That policy was enforced, in many places and for many years, with brutal methods: heavy fines, lost jobs, and forced abortions and sterilisations. It is one of the great human rights abuses of the late twentieth century, and it should be remembered as such.

It also left behind a peculiar family shape. Chinese demographers speak of the four-two-one family: four grandparents, two parents, one child. One young person who, in time, may be the only support for six older people.

The policy is gone now. It was replaced first by a two-child limit, then by a three-child limit. Today the same state that once fined families for a second child is pleading with its citizens to have more.

So far, they are not listening.

In 2025, China registered about seven point nine two million births. That was a fall of about seventeen percent in a single year, and the lowest number since the People's Republic began keeping these records in 1949. In the same year, about eleven point three million people died.

China's population fell by about three point four million people. It was the fourth year in a row of decline, and the steepest of the four.

[PAUSE]

A country of about one point four billion people is now losing more than three million a year. That is a great city's worth of people, gone from the count every twelve months, and the pace is accelerating.

In July 2025, the Chinese government announced a national childcare subsidy: three thousand six hundred yuan, about five hundred dollars, per child, per year, until the child turns three. It was paid for by the central government, exempt from income tax, and aimed, officials said, at more than twenty million families a year.

It is a real policy and a real effort. But one economist at a respected research firm said what many quietly thought: the sums are too small to move the birth rate in the near term. Five hundred dollars a year does not buy an apartment in Shanghai. It does not buy back a young woman's fear that leaving work to have a baby will end her career.

Now look south, to India, the most populous country in the world.

For decades, India was synonymous, in the Western imagination, with the population explosion. In the nineteen-sixties, books warned of famine and mass starvation driven by too many people.

According to India's own official Sample Registration System, the country's total fertility rate in 2023 was one point nine. Below replacement.

[PAUSE]

India is a vast and varied place, and the national number conceals enormous differences. In Bihar, one of the poorest states, the rate was about two point eight. In Delhi, it was about one point two, as low as Italy or Japan. Eighteen of India's states and union territories are now below replacement.

In India's cities, the rate was about one point five. In the countryside it was about two point one, right at replacement.

And here is the pair of numbers I want you to keep. Among women who could not read, the rate was about three point three.

Among women who could read, it was about one point eight.

That pair tells you something profound about what drives this change. Education, especially the education of girls and women, is one of the most powerful forces behind falling fertility anywhere in the world. And that is overwhelmingly a good thing. Women who can read, who can work, who can choose, tend to have fewer children, and later.

Nothing in this speech argues against the education or the freedom of women. Those are among the great achievements of the modern age. The question is not whether women should have choices. It is whether the choices they make are truly free, or are being made under pressures that force them to accept fewer children than they wanted.

India's population will keep growing for decades, because of the momentum I described earlier: there are huge numbers of young people who will still become parents. But the fertility turn has already happened.

Now cross the Pacific to Chile.

In 1960, the average Chilean woman had about five point four children. In 2024, according to figures from the national statistics institute, the rate was about one. Some reports give one point zero three, others a little higher. Either way, it is among the lowest in the Americas, and lower than Japan.

From five to one in a single lifetime.

[PAUSE]

Chile's statistics institute projects that the country will record more deaths than births by around 2028.

Chile is not a rich country by the standards of Western Europe. It is a middle-income country that has grown quickly. And that is what makes it important. It shows that the fertility collapse is not waiting for countries to become fully rich. It is arriving while they are still on the way there.

Demographers have a phrase for this, and it is one of the sharpest in the whole field. They warn that some countries will grow old before they grow rich.

Japan grew rich and then grew old. It had decades of prosperity, savings and investment to cushion the change. China, Thailand, much of Latin America and others are growing old far faster, and with far less of a cushion. They will have to care for large elderly populations with smaller workforces and thinner public budgets than the rich countries had at the same stage.

[LET IT LAND]

So the great turn is global. It runs through Beijing and Delhi and Santiago as surely as through Rome and Tokyo.

The world did not get rich first and then stop having children. It stopped having children while still getting rich.

## VIII. America at Its Lowest

Now let me bring this home, to the United States.

For a long time, Americans told themselves they were different. American fertility stayed near replacement long after Europe and East Asia had fallen far below. A young, growing, immigrant-fed nation seemed immune to the demographic winter settling over the old world.

That story ended around 2007.

[PAUSE]

In April 2026, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention released provisional data for 2025. The general fertility rate, the number of births per thousand women of childbearing age, fell to fifty-three point one. That is the lowest ever recorded. It is about twenty-three percent below where it stood in 2007.

There were about three million, six hundred and six thousand births in America in 2025, down about one percent from the year before.

A calculation by the Wall Street Journal from the same provisional data put the total fertility rate at about one point five seven. Not as low as Italy or Japan. But well below replacement, and still falling.

Look at the details, because the details tell the story.

Births among teenagers fell again, to record lows. The teen birth rate is now under twelve per thousand. That is good news. The long fall in teen pregnancy over the past three decades is one of the great public health successes of our time, and nobody should want to reverse it.

Birth rates for women in their late twenties fell by more than four percent. Birth rates for women in their early thirties rose by almost three percent.

[PAUSE]

So American women are having children later. And when children come later, fewer of them come in total. The window narrows. The second child becomes a question. The third becomes rare.

Notice the year at which the decline began: 2007. That was the eve of the great financial crisis.

When the crisis came, millions of young Americans lost jobs, homes and savings. Birth rates fell, as they usually do in a recession. That much was expected. What was not expected was what happened next. When the economy recovered, the birth rate did not. Unemployment fell, wages eventually rose, the stock market climbed to record after record. And the birth rate kept drifting down, year after year, through boom and bust alike, through the pandemic and after it.

[PAUSE]

That is one of the great puzzles of American demography. For most of the twentieth century, births tracked the economy. Hard times meant fewer babies. Good times meant more. The baby boom itself followed the Depression and the war. But in the last two decades, that link seems to have weakened. Even good times have not brought the babies back.

Why? Here again, I must be honest about the limits of what we know.

Economists have searched hard for a single cause. A 2022 analysis in the Journal of Economic Perspectives looked at the obvious suspects, from the cost of childcare to student debt to housing, and could not identify one economic or policy factor that explained the decline. The economist Phillip Levine has argued that what has changed is something harder to measure: the priorities of younger generations, the way they weigh children against careers, leisure, freedom and other goals in life.

I take that seriously. I do not believe there is a single lever that, if pulled, would return America to two point one.

But I also want to point you to something. Shifting priorities do not form in a vacuum. Young Americans form their priorities in a world where housing in the cities with the best jobs costs many times what it cost their parents. Where full-time childcare can rival the rent. Where the United States remains one of very few rich nations with no national paid parental leave. Where a young couple can be in their thirties before they feel secure enough to start.

When people say that children are a lower priority, part of what they may mean is that children have been made very expensive, and other things have been made very easy.

[SLOW]

America still has advantages many countries do not. Its population is still growing, mainly because of immigration. It is younger than Europe or East Asia. Its fertility, while at record lows, is not yet in the territory of Korea or Taiwan.

That means America still has time. Time is the most precious thing in demography, because every policy takes a generation to bear fruit.

[LET IT LAND]

The question is whether we will use that time, or spend it.

Lost years do not come back. Neither do the children who were wanted and never born.

## IX. Not Extinction, a Shrinking

That completes the first argument. Korea and Taiwan, Japan, Europe, China, India, Chile and America: the facts.

Now the second argument: what those facts mean, and what they cost. It is time to step back and say clearly what all of this means, and also what it does not mean.

Begin with what it does not mean.

It does not mean humanity is about to die out. I have heard that said, sometimes by very prominent people, and I think it is wrong. It is wrong on the evidence, and it does damage, because it makes a serious problem sound like science fiction, and people stop listening.

According to the United Nations' 2024 projections, the world's population, now a little over eight billion, will continue to grow for about another sixty years. It will peak around 2084 at just under ten point three billion. By 2100 it is projected to have fallen slightly, to about ten point two billion.

[SLOW]

Ten billion people in 2100. That is not extinction. It is more people than there are today.

And there are still many parts of the world where families are large. The Lancet forecast expects that by 2100, more than half of all the babies born on Earth will be born in sub-Saharan Africa. That is a profound shift in the human story, and it will shape the century in ways we are only beginning to understand.

Put it another way. A child born in 2100 will more likely than not be born in Nigeria, Ethiopia, Congo, Tanzania or one of their neighbours. The young people of the late twenty-first century will be, to a degree never seen before, African.

That is a fact to meet with respect and with investment, not with fear. The countries of sub-Saharan Africa are themselves seeing fertility fall, as girls stay in school longer and cities grow. Their transition is happening too, only later and from a higher starting point. Whether their young populations become a great engine of prosperity or a source of frustration will depend on schools, jobs, health and good government, in Africa and in the partnerships the rest of the world chooses to build with it.

So let us retire the word extinction. It does not fit.

[BUILD]

Now, what does it mean?

First, it means that the long era of human population growth, which began with the agricultural and industrial revolutions and accelerated so dramatically in the twentieth century, is coming to an end. Within this century, for the first time in many centuries, the human population is expected to begin a sustained decline. Only this time it will not be caused by plague or war or famine. It will be caused by choice.

The UN now estimates that sixty-three countries and areas, home to about twenty-eight percent of the world's people, have already passed their peak population. China and Japan are among them.

And the projections keep moving in the same direction. The UN's 2024 forecast for the year 2100 is about seven hundred million people lower than its forecast of just over a decade earlier. Seven hundred million people, removed from the projected future, almost as many as live in all of Europe today.

Each new revision has, in general, brought the peak earlier and lower. Not every revision, and not by the same amount. But the direction is unmistakable.

It is worth pausing on how this decline will begin, because it will not feel like a decline at first.

A peak is a gentle thing. A population that reaches its highest point does not fall off a cliff. It levels off, hovers, and then begins, slowly, to slip. For years the change is barely visible in the national totals. It shows up first in the classrooms, then in the universities, then in the number of young workers entering each trade, and only much later in the headline population figure.

That is why the problem is so easy to ignore. By the time the total population is clearly falling, the decisions that caused it are a generation old, and the decisions that might reverse it will take another generation to work.

Second, it means aging on a scale never seen before.

By around 2080, the UN projects, the world will have more people aged sixty-five and over than children under eighteen.

Part of that comes from something to celebrate. Global life expectancy has risen from about sixty-four years in 1990 to over seventy-three today. Hundreds of millions of people are living lives that their grandparents never got to finish. An older world is, in part, a world that has conquered the diseases that once killed people young.

So when I speak of aging, I am not speaking of a disaster. I am speaking of a triumph that comes with a bill.

[PAUSE]

The bill is the balance between generations. When fertility is high and life is short, every older person is surrounded by many younger people. When fertility is low and life is long, the pyramid turns upside down. Fewer workers support more retirees. Fewer grandchildren visit more grandparents. Fewer young nurses care for more old patients.

Third, it means local depopulation long before global decline. The world as a whole will keep growing for decades. But towns, regions and whole countries are already shrinking, right now. The akiya in the Japanese mountains. The closed schools in rural Korea. The villages of southern Italy where houses are offered for a symbolic euro. The towns of eastern Europe that have lost so many of their young people to low fertility and emigration together.

That is what civilizational shrinking looks like in practice. Not an ending, but a thinning.

Now, fairness requires me to say that a smaller world is not only a story of loss.

Some economists argue that when workers become scarce, they gain bargaining power, and wages rise. Some planners see in emptying regions a chance to return land to forest and wetland. Some urbanists see in quieter cities a chance for more space, less crowding and cheaper homes, at least eventually. A world of ten billion that slowly becomes a world of nine, or eight, need not be a poorer or sadder world, if the transition is handled well.

I accept all of that as possible. I would only add two words of caution.

The first is that the benefits of a smaller population tend to arrive late, and the costs tend to arrive early. The pension bill comes due before the cheaper housing does.

The second is that it matters enormously how fast the shrinking happens. A population that declines by ten percent a generation can adapt gracefully. A population that shrinks by more than half every generation, as Korea and Taiwan would at today's rates, cannot adapt gracefully. It can only cope.

The difference between those two futures is, in large part, the difference between a fertility rate of one point eight and one of zero point eight. And that difference is something policy and society can still influence.

And there is one more thing I must say clearly, because this subject attracts ugly ideas the way a wound attracts infection.

There are people who talk about falling birth rates as if one group of people were being deliberately replaced by another. That is a conspiracy theory, and it is false. There is no plot. There is no secret plan. There are billions of ordinary people in every culture and on every continent, making private decisions about their own lives, under pressures that are very similar from Seoul to Santiago.

And the worth of a child does not depend on the country of her parents or the colour of her skin. A child born in Lagos is not a threat to a child born in Lyon. Every child is a beginning.

[PAUSE]

So let us describe the change accurately. It is not extinction. It is not replacement. It is not the end of the world.

It is the end of a world: the world of growth that every modern institution was built for.

[LET IT LAND]

Our pensions, our tax systems, our cities, our schools, our health services, our economic models, even our sense of progress, were all designed for a world in which every generation was larger than the one before.

That world is ending. And almost none of our institutions are ready for the one that replaces it.

## X. The Ledger of an Aging World

Every generation keeps a ledger, whether it knows it or not. On one side is what it receives from those who came before. On the other is what it leaves to those who come after.

Let me read you the entries that the empty cradle is writing into that ledger.

The first entry is pensions.

Almost every public pension system in the world works on a simple principle. Today's workers pay for today's retirees, with the promise that tomorrow's workers will pay for them. It is a chain of trust running across the generations.

That chain depends on there being enough people in each link. When each generation is smaller than the last, the chain is stretched. Either the workers must pay more, or the retirees must receive less, or the retirement age must rise, or the government must borrow. Usually it is some of all four.

[SLOW]

We have seen this already. Korea is raising pension contributions by nearly half, from nine percent to thirteen, and even so it has only pushed back the date the fund is expected to run dry by about nine years. France built its pension forecasts on a fertility rate of one point eight, and the reality is now one point five six.

These are not abstract numbers. Every gap between the forecast and the reality is a future argument about who pays: young workers through higher taxes, or old people through smaller pensions.

The second entry is care.

An aging society needs more nurses, more home carers, more doctors who understand old age, more people to help with the slow and patient work of looking after the frail. And it has fewer young people to do that work.

Remember Taiwan's projection. By 2075, the government's planners expect, there will be about one hundred and thirty-seven dependents, children and older people, for every hundred people of working age. Today, in most countries, there are far fewer than one hundred.

Think about what that means in a hospital ward at three in the morning. Think about what it means for the daughter who is the only child of two only children, and who will one day be responsible for four aging parents and grandparents, alone.

[BUILD]

The third entry is place.

A shrinking population does not shrink evenly. It drains from the countryside and the small towns first, toward the big cities, which themselves have the lowest fertility of all. We saw it in Tokyo, under one. We saw it in Seoul, at zero point six three.

So the small towns empty. The houses become akiya. The school closes, and then there is no reason for a young family to move there, and so the next school closes too. Roads, water pipes, power lines and railways built for a larger population must still be maintained by a smaller one. The cost per person goes up just as the number of people goes down.

And here is a paradox that I find almost unbearable. In the very same countries where millions of homes stand empty in the countryside, young people in the cities cannot afford a place big enough to raise a child. Japan has nine million vacant homes, and Tokyo, its most expensive city, has a fertility rate under one. Korea closes rural schools while young couples in Seoul postpone children because they cannot afford an apartment.

The homes are in the wrong places. The jobs are in the cities. And the young are caught between them.

The fourth entry is the economy itself.

Economic growth, at its simplest, comes from two things: more workers and more output per worker. When the workforce shrinks, growth must come entirely from productivity. That is possible. But it is harder. A country whose working-age population is falling by one percent a year needs productivity to rise by one percent just to keep its economy the same size.

And there is a subtler point, which I offer as an argument rather than a proven fact. New ideas, new businesses and new movements are disproportionately the work of the young. A society with fewer young people may find, over time, that it has fewer risk-takers, fewer founders, fewer rebels. Some economists have argued exactly this. Others disagree. I raise it because I think it deserves more attention than it gets.

The fifth entry is political.

I mentioned earlier that Pericles believed citizens with a stake in the future make better decisions. Consider what happens to a democracy as its median voter ages.

The share of voters who are retired rises. The share who have young children falls. Older voters are not selfish. Many of them care deeply about their grandchildren. But a democracy in which older voters vastly outnumber young families may find it harder to spend on schools than on pensions, harder to build housing than to protect the value of existing homes, harder to invest in the long term than to protect the short.

Nobody planned it. It is the simple arithmetic of who is in the room.

And there is a related entry that we rarely say out loud: debt.

Governments borrow against the future. They do it for wars and recessions and pandemics, and sometimes for good reason. Every bond issued is a promise that tomorrow's taxpayers will pay it back. When tomorrow's taxpayers are fewer than today's, each of them inherits a larger share of the bill.

A generation that borrows heavily and also has few children is, in effect, writing large cheques for a small number of people to cash. Those people did not agree to the debt. They were not yet born when it was signed. That is not an argument against all public borrowing. It is an argument for remembering, every time we borrow, who will be paying.

[PAUSE]

The sixth entry is the most personal.

It is the loneliness of a world with fewer children in it. Fewer siblings. Fewer cousins. Fewer nieces and nephews. Fewer children playing in the street. A growing number of people who will reach old age with no children and no grandchildren, not because they chose that, but because life never quite gave them the chance.

I do not say that childless lives are lesser lives. They are not. Many people without children live full, generous, loving lives, and give enormously to the young people around them. But I do say that a society in which children become rare is a society that has lost something it may not know how to name.

So read the ledger.

Pensions under strain. Care stretched thin. Towns emptying. Growth harder to come by. Politics tilting toward the present. And a quiet loneliness spreading through the generations.

None of these is a catastrophe on its own. Together, over decades, they will reshape every society on Earth.

[SLOW]

So let me take you to the bottom of the valley, because every honest speech on this subject has to go there once.

Imagine the year 2075. This is a composite, not a forecast for any one place. A woman of seventy sits in a flat in a large city. She is an only child, and so was her husband. Her parents are gone. She has one son, and he lives on another continent.

The building has more empty flats than full ones. The school at the end of her street became a care home years ago, and the care home is short of staff. The village her grandmother came from has no one left in it at all.

Nothing terrible has happened to her. There was no war, no famine, no plague. Only a long series of reasonable decisions, made by millions of reasonable people, none of whom wanted this.

That is the future the ledger is writing if we leave it alone.

[LET IT LAND]

But the ledger is not yet closed.

What is written in the next column depends on what we do now.

## XI. The Strongest Case Against Me

Now, I have made my case with as much force as I can. But a speech that only argues one side is not an argument. It is an advertisement. So let me give you the strongest case against what I have said, and answer it as honestly as I can.

[PAUSE]

The first objection is the oldest. It says: thank goodness. There are too many people already. The planet is warming. Forests are falling. Species are disappearing. A smaller human population will tread more lightly on the Earth. Why on earth would you mourn the empty cradle?

I take this seriously. For most of my lifetime, the dominant fear was overpopulation, not underpopulation. People wrote books predicting mass starvation. Some of that fear was justified. Pressure on land, water and wildlife is real.

But here is my answer.

The climate will be decided in the next few decades, by how we make energy, how we grow food and how we build our cities. The world population, as we have seen, will keep growing until the 2080s. By the time it starts to shrink, the decisive years for the climate will long be over. Falling birth rates cannot save the climate. Only clean technology and wise policy can do that.

And there is something deeper. I am not arguing that every family must have more children, or that the world should aim for some particular population. I am arguing for something much more modest: that people should be able to have the number of children they themselves say they want. If people freely want small families, so be it. My argument is about the gap between what people want and what they get.

An environmentalism that asks young couples to give up the children they long for, as a sacrifice to the planet, has lost touch with the very thing it says it is protecting: the future of human beings.

The second objection says: technology will solve it. Robots will do the work. Artificial intelligence will boost productivity. We do not need more workers. We need better machines.

There is something to this. Automation will help. Japan has already shown how robots and smart systems can ease labour shortages in factories and even in care homes. Productivity can and must rise.

But consider what machines cannot do. A robot cannot be a grandchild. A machine cannot sit with a dying father and hold his hand. Artificial intelligence cannot fill a classroom, sing in a choir, play in a football team, fall in love, or vote. A society is not just an economy. It is a web of relationships across generations. Productivity can pay the pensions. It cannot replace the people.

And even on the economics, it is a gamble. It asks us to bet the welfare of the next generation on a technological revolution arriving on time. I hope it does. I would not stake my children's future on it, and I do not think we should stake our nations' futures on it either.

The third objection is the most practical. It says: just let people in. There are billions of young people in Africa and South Asia who want to work in rich countries. Immigration can fill the gap.

Immigration is a great strength, and a good thing for countries that welcome it wisely. France and Italy are only holding their populations steady because of it. America is still growing because of it. Many of the nurses and carers in the aging societies of Europe and East Asia already come from abroad. I am glad they do, and I honour them.

But immigration cannot be the whole answer, for one simple reason. The countries that send migrants are also seeing their fertility fall. India is already below replacement. Latin America is far below it. The Lancet forecast expects only six countries in the world to be above replacement by 2100.

Immigration can redistribute the young people of the world. It cannot create them. When almost every country is aging at once, the pool of young people available to move will shrink too, and the competition for them will grow.

And there is a moral question here as well. Is it right for rich countries to solve their own demographic problems by drawing the nurses and doctors and engineers away from poorer countries that also need them? I do not think that question has an easy answer. But it should make us cautious about treating immigration as an endless reservoir.

So welcome immigrants, yes. Treat them well. But do not pretend that immigration relieves us of the work of making our own societies places where people can afford to have families.

[BUILD]

The fourth objection is the one I take most seriously of all. It says: be careful. History is full of governments that wanted more babies, and the results were often horrifying.

That is true. In Romania in the nineteen-sixties, the communist regime banned most abortions and contraception to raise the birth rate. Women died from illegal abortions. By the time the regime fell, something like a hundred thousand children were living in state orphanages. The birth rate jumped briefly and then fell again. Other regimes have used the language of national survival to control women's bodies, to rank some families above others, and to demonise outsiders.

So let me be absolutely clear about where I stand.

I reject coercion completely. No government should force anyone to have a child, or forbid anyone from preventing one. No government should reward some citizens' children above others because of their ethnicity, their religion or their origin. No government should tell women that their worth lies only in motherhood.

The goal I am arguing for is not more babies at any price. It is more freedom, the freedom to have the family you want, when you want it, without having to sacrifice your livelihood, your health or your dignity to do it.

That goal points in exactly the opposite direction from coercion. Coercion takes choices away. What I am arguing for gives them back.

[SLOW]

The fifth objection is the gentlest. It says: relax. Fertility has fallen before and recovered. The baby boom followed the Great Depression. Perhaps this too will pass on its own.

I hope it does. And I have reported to you, faithfully, the small recoveries in Korea and Finland in 2025.

But I would point out that for decades, forecasters have predicted that fertility would level off and rebound, and for decades it has kept falling. Japan's health ministry told us the decline is running fifteen years ahead of projections. Taiwan's planners expected zero point eight seven and got zero point seven. There is no law of nature that says it must bounce back.

There is a sixth objection, and it is often made with real feeling. It says: this is none of your business. Whether to have a child is the most private decision a person can make, and above all a woman's decision. When politicians and speakers start talking about the birth rate, women have learned to brace themselves, because what usually follows is pressure, blame or control.

I understand that fear. History has given women every reason for it.

[PAUSE]

So let me answer it directly. The decision to have a child belongs to the people who will have it, and no one else. Nothing I have said tonight changes that, and nothing ever should.

But the conditions under which that decision is made are not private. They are public. Housing law is public. Parental leave is public. The cost of childcare, the hours of school, the rules of the workplace, the price of fertility treatment: all of these are written by governments and employers, and they shape private decisions as surely as the weather shapes a harvest.

To talk about those conditions is not to intrude on anyone's choice. It is to ask whether the choice is being made freely, or whether it has already been made for people by the price of a two-bedroom flat.

Silence on this subject does not protect women. It leaves them alone with obstacles that were never theirs to carry by themselves.

[LET IT LAND]

So I have heard the case against me. And I find that each objection, taken seriously, does not refute my argument. It sharpens it.

The goal is not maximum population. It is not coercion. It is not panic. It is the closing of a gap between what people want and what they are able to have.

Let me show you that gap.

## XII. What People Say They Want

In 2025, the United Nations Population Fund published its annual State of World Population report. It gave the report a striking title: The Real Fertility Crisis.

Its argument was this. The real crisis is not that there are too many people or too few. The real crisis is that so many people cannot have the families they want.

To find out, the agency worked with the polling firm YouGov to survey about fourteen thousand adults in fourteen countries. Together those countries are home to more than a third of humanity. They ranged from rich to poor, from high fertility to very low.

Here is what they found.

The most common number of children that people said they wanted was two. Not zero. Not one. Two.

That is a remarkable finding, given everything I have told you tonight. In country after country where the actual fertility rate is one point five, or one point two, or under one, the most common ideal is still two.

[SLOW]

Almost one in five adults of reproductive age said they expected to end up with a different number of children than they wanted. Most of those expected to have fewer. And nearly a quarter said there had been a time in their lives when they wanted a child, but could not have one when they wanted to.

Nearly a quarter.

Think of the people in this room. Think of your friends, your brothers and sisters, your colleagues. Some of them are part of that quarter. Some of you are.

Why do people end up with fewer children than they want? The report asked them. In India, for example, people named financial limitations most often, then housing, then job insecurity. In Korea, as I mentioned, more than half said money worries had led them, or might lead them, to have fewer children than they wanted.

And recall the Italian study I described earlier. It found that the collapse in Italian births is not a collapse in the desire to be a parent. It is a collapse in the conditions that make it possible.

Let me try to describe those conditions, not as statistics, but as a life.

Imagine a young woman, let us say twenty-seven years old, living in any big city in the developed world. She is a composite. But you know her. She has spent her twenties studying and building a career, because she has been told, rightly, that her independence matters. She has student debt. She rents a small flat, and the rent takes a large share of her income. Buying a home seems a distant dream.

She has a partner. They talk about children. They both want them, someday. But not yet. Not until the career is more secure. Not until they have a bigger place. Not until they can afford childcare. Not until her employer stops treating pregnancy as a problem.

Someday turns into thirty-two. Then thirty-five. They have their first child. It is wonderful and exhausting and expensive. Childcare costs as much as a second rent. She notices that her career has slowed, while his has not. She notices that she does most of the night feeds and most of the doctor's appointments and most of the planning.

They talk about a second child. They want one. But she is thirty-eight now. They are tired. Money is tight. And then, perhaps, when they finally decide to try, it does not happen as easily as it did the first time.

They end up with one child. They wanted two.

[PAUSE]

Nobody forced that outcome. Nobody passed a law against the second child. Every decision along the way was reasonable. And yet the sum of those reasonable decisions is an outcome neither of them wanted.

And there is another story, told less often. It is the story of the young man who never gets as far as the conversation about children at all.

In many countries, young men are falling behind young women in education. Many are in insecure work, or out of work. Many say they feel they cannot offer a partner the stability that a family needs. In many places the number of young people in couples at all, married or not, has fallen. And without couples, there are few children.

So the empty cradle is not only a story about women weighing children against careers. It is also a story about men who feel they have nothing to offer, about couples who never form, and about a generation in which more and more people spend their twenties and thirties alone.

Any serious answer has to reach those young men too. Good jobs, good training and a sense that they are needed are, among other things, family policy.

Multiply those two stories by hundreds of millions, and you have the empty cradle.

[SLOW]

There is a biological fact underneath that story, and we do people no kindness by hiding it. Fertility does not wait for a housing market to calm down or a career to settle. For women it declines with age, and more steeply from the late thirties. Medicine can help, and it has helped millions of families. But it cannot fully turn back that clock, and treatment is expensive, demanding and far from certain.

So when a society pushes the start of family life later and later, it is not only changing the timing of children. It is changing how many children are possible at all.

Now let me be fair to the other side of this. Not everyone who has fewer children than the two they once imagined is a victim of circumstance. Preferences change over a life. Some people discover that one child is exactly right. Some people discover that a life without children suits them better than they expected. And it is possible that the ideal itself is slowly drifting down. Researchers argue about that, and I will not pretend to settle it.

That matters. Policy cannot and should not try to manufacture desires people do not have.

But even if ideals drift down, the gap between what people want and what they get remains. And that gap is the part we can do something about. That gap is not destiny. It is design. It is the result of how we have built our housing markets, our workplaces, our schools, our tax systems and our expectations of men and women.

[SLOW]

What about the policies that have been tried? I promised honesty, so here it is.

No country, anywhere, has used policy to bring fertility back to replacement after it fell far below. Not Hungary with its generous incentives. Not the Nordics with their excellent childcare. Not Korea, which has spent enormous sums over two decades. Not China with its new subsidy.

That is a sobering fact, and anyone who promises a quick fix is not telling you the truth.

But here is the other side of that fact. The countries that support families well generally have higher fertility than they otherwise would. France, with its long tradition of family policy, has stayed well above Italy for decades, and even after its recent fall, one point five six against one point one four. Policy may not restore replacement. But it can narrow the gap. It can mean the difference between one point five and one point two, which, over a few generations, is the difference between a gentle decline and a collapse.

And above all, good family policy is worth doing for its own sake. A child who grows up in a home that is not crushed by rent, with parents who are not exhausted by impossible hours, in a community with good schools and safe streets, is better off whether or not the national fertility rate moves at all.

[LET IT LAND]

So the evidence leads to a modest conclusion, and also a powerful one.

We cannot command people to have children. We should not try. But we can stop making it so hard for them to have the children they already want.

That is not social engineering. It is simply removing the obstacles we ourselves put in the way.

Remove them, and a society says to a child who does not yet exist the four words from that chalkboard: we were expecting you.

## XIII. What We Owe

That brings me to the third argument. We have heard the facts. We have weighed the stakes. Now the duty.

Near the end of his oration, Pericles made a promise to the families of the fallen. The children of the dead, he said, "will be brought up till manhood at the public expense."

[PAUSE]

Think about that. Two and a half thousand years ago, in a city at war, the Athenians understood that raising the next generation was not a purely private matter. It was something the whole city owed, a public debt paid to those who had given everything, and to the future they had died for.

That is the idea I want to leave with you. The raising of children is not only the private business of their parents. It is the shared work of a society that wants to have a future.

So let me tell you what I believe we owe.

We owe the next generation homes.

The single most common obstacle young people name, in country after country, is the cost of housing. You cannot raise a family in a studio flat. You cannot plan for children when rent takes half your income and owning a home seems impossible. We must build. We must build homes in the cities where the jobs are, homes with room for children, and we must stop allowing the rules of zoning and planning to be written for people who already own homes against those who do not.

A society that will not build homes for young families has decided, without saying so, that it does not want them.

[PAUSE]

We owe the next generation time.

Parenthood takes time, and our economies have been built as if it took none. We need paid parental leave that is long enough to matter, and that is shared by both parents. We need workplaces that allow flexible hours without punishing the people who use them. We need schools and childcare whose hours fit real working lives.

We owe mothers an end to the motherhood penalty.

In much of the world, a woman's earnings fall sharply after her first child and never fully recover, while a father's earnings do not. Women see that. They are not foolish. When the price of a child is a career, many will have fewer children, or none. Every employer and every government that closes that gap removes one of the most powerful reasons for the empty cradle.

We owe fathers a full share, and we owe children fathers who take it.

This is the part of the story that is too often left out. In Korea, in Japan, in much of Southern Europe, women describe a world in which they are expected to work like men and then come home to a second shift of housework and childcare. Researchers in several countries have found that where men share that work more equally, women are more willing to have a second child. A father who changes nappies, takes leave, does the school run and stays home when the child is sick is not doing his partner a favour. He is doing his part.

We owe people honest knowledge about their own fertility, and real access to care.

Fertility declines with age, for women and, more gradually, for men. Too many people discover too late that the someday they planned for has passed. That is not a reason to pressure anyone. It is a reason to tell the truth clearly and early, and to make fertility treatment affordable for those who need it. Nearly a quarter of people in the UN survey had wanted a child at a time when they could not have one. Some of them could have been helped.

We owe families money that is enough to matter.

Child allowances, tax credits and childcare subsidies will not, on their own, restore replacement fertility. We have seen that. But they ease the pressure on families who are already stretched, and they say, in the clearest language a government has, that children are valued. If a nation can find the money for its pensions, it can find the money for its children.

We owe the communities that are already shrinking an honest plan.

Even if every policy I have described succeeds, many places will keep losing people for decades. We owe them better than slow abandonment. That means reforming pension systems now, gradually and fairly, rather than in a crisis later. It means helping towns consolidate services gracefully, keeping clinics and schools open where they matter most, and turning empty houses into homes for new families. It means planning for a smaller future rather than pretending it is not coming.

We owe newcomers a welcome.

Immigration will not solve the empty cradle on its own, but it will be part of every answer. The nurse from the Philippines in a Tokyo care home. The engineer from India in a Berlin laboratory. The farm worker from Morocco in a village in southern Spain. Their children are our future too. A society that wants to renew itself must be a society that newcomers are glad to join.

And we owe our children, and theirs, a culture that honours the work of raising them.

For a long time, many of our societies have celebrated achievement, independence, travel, career, consumption, all good things, while treating the raising of children as a private hobby, a lifestyle choice, even an inconvenience to others. We have built cities without room for prams and restaurants without room for noise. We have spoken of children as costs.

I am not asking anyone to look down on those who choose not to have children. I am asking us to look up again at those who do. Parents are not doing something for themselves alone. They are raising the people who will one day care for all of us, pay for all of us, remember all of us.

[PAUSE]

And we owe the future patience.

Here is the hardest truth about everything I have proposed. None of it works quickly. A child born next year will not enter the workforce until the 2040s. A housing programme takes a decade to change a city. A culture changes over a generation. Politicians who face elections every four or five years are tempted to announce a bonus, watch the birth rate for two years, and then give up when it does not leap.

That is the pattern we have seen in too many countries. A burst of policy, a burst of headlines, and then drift. If we are serious, we must commit for a generation, across parties and across governments, the way the builders of cathedrals committed to work they knew they would never see finished.

And we owe the young a story about the future that is worth believing.

People do not bring children into a world they believe is ending. Many young people today have grown up on a steady diet of dread: about the climate, about war, about the economy, about technology taking their jobs. Some say openly that they hesitate to have children because they fear the world those children would inherit.

I do not dismiss those fears. Some of them are well founded. But a civilisation that tells its young people only that the future will be worse is a civilisation that should not be surprised when they hesitate to invest in it.

We owe them a different story, and not a false one. A true story: that the world has solved enormous problems before. Children in most of the world are far more likely to survive childhood than their great-grandparents were. Hundreds of millions have escaped extreme poverty. Life expectancy has climbed by nearly ten years since 1990. The future is not written. It is made. And the young are the ones who will make it.

[SLOW]

Homes. Time. Fairness at work. Fairness at home. Honest knowledge. Real support. An honest plan for shrinking places. A welcome for newcomers. A culture that honours the raising of children.

None of these is radical. Every one of them exists somewhere in the world today. What no country has yet done is to do all of them at once, consistently, for a generation.

Pericles said that the city would raise the children of the fallen. He understood that a society keeps faith with its past by investing in its future.

[LET IT LAND]

We are not asked to die for our country. We are asked something easier, and in its way harder. We are asked to make room in it for the children who would like to be born.

## XIV. The Unwritten Memorial

We began in an empty classroom, with a welcome sign on the board and no one to read it.

Let me end there too.

[PAUSE]

Pericles told the Athenians that "heroes have the whole earth for their tomb." He meant that the memory of the brave is not kept in stone alone. It is kept, as he put it, in a record unwritten, carried in every heart.

I want to turn that idea around.

The people I have spoken for tonight are not the dead. They are the unborn. They have no tomb and no name. They cannot thank us, or blame us. They cannot vote, or march, or write to their representatives. They exist only as a possibility, in the plans of young couples and in the arithmetic of demographers.

They too have an unwritten memorial. It is the world we leave them. Every home we build, every school we keep open, every workplace we make fair, every family we support, every newcomer we welcome, is a line written in that memorial. Each one says, across the years, the same four words as the chalkboard: we were expecting you.

Lincoln said that the world would little note nor long remember what he said at Gettysburg, but that it could never forget what the fallen had done there.

He was wrong about the first part. We remember every word.

But he was right about the deeper point. Words are not the measure. Deeds are.

[PAUSE]

So I will not ask you to remember what I have said tonight. Forget the numbers if you must: the zero point seven two and the one point one four, the nine million empty homes, the forty-nine closing schools. Numbers fade.

Remember instead one simple truth. The future is not something that happens to us. It is something we hand on, or fail to hand on, one generation at a time.

It is for us the living, then, to take up the unfinished work.

It is for us to tell the truth about the empty cradle: not extinction, but a shrinking; not the end of the world, but the end of a world; not a reason for panic, but a reason for purpose.

It is for us to refuse the false comforts: the comfort that says nothing is wrong, and the comfort that says nothing can be done.

It is for us to refuse the false cures as well: the cure that would coerce, the cure that would blame, the cure that would divide one people from another.

It is for us to close the gap between the families people want and the families they are able to have.

[SLOW]

It is for us to build homes where children can grow, and towns where they can stay.

It is for us to make work fit around life, and not life around work.

It is for us to share the labour of love between mothers and fathers, between families and the whole community.

It is for us to care for the old with dignity, and to plan with honesty for the places that will grow smaller.

It is for us to open our doors to those who come from far away, and to make them part of the story.

And it is for us to remember that every society, however great, is a relay and not a monument. Athens is gone. The Union Lincoln saved endures because each generation chose to renew it. Every nation on Earth lives only as long as it keeps making room for those who come next.

[SLOW]

Let it be said of our generation that we saw the change coming and did not look away.

Let it be said that we told the truth about it, without exaggeration and without denial.

Let it be said that when the cradles began to empty, we did not blame the young, or shame the childless, or turn on the stranger, or try to command what can only be invited.

Let it be said that we did the patient, unglamorous work: building the homes, changing the workplaces, sharing the care, reforming the pensions, keeping the schools open where children still needed them.

Let it be said that we kept faith with those who came before us by making room for those who come after.

We cannot dedicate a cemetery to the unborn. We cannot consecrate a classroom that no child will enter. We cannot hallow a cradle that stays empty.

But we can decide, here and now, that the cradle will not stay empty for lack of a home, for lack of time, for lack of fairness, for lack of care.

We can decide that the young couple who want two children will have the chance to have them.

We can decide that the welcome sign on the chalkboard will be read.

[SLOW]

The generations before us handed us a world they made, imperfect and unfinished, but alive. They did not know our names. They built for us anyway.

Now it is our turn.

[LET IT LAND]

Let us build for those whose names we do not know. Let us leave the chairs out and the lights on. And when they come, let the words on the board be true.

[SLOW]

We were expecting you.

Thank you.
