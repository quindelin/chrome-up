# Who Owns the Machines

## I. Two Deposits

On the first of October this year, a little after sunrise on the Kuskokwim River, money landed in bank accounts all across Alaska.

It landed in Anchorage and in Fairbanks. It landed in Bethel and in Utqiagvik, and in villages you cannot reach by road.

It landed for fishermen and for nurses. For retirees and for schoolchildren. For people who vote one way, people who vote the other, and people who have never voted at all.

Twelve hundred dollars each. A thousand of it dividend, two hundred of it energy relief.

Altogether, more than eight hundred million dollars. Paid by a state that most Americans would never call socialist, to every eligible resident, simply because they live there.

[PAUSE]

That same autumn, on the same continent, another kind of deposit was being made.

This one was poured in concrete and wrapped in steel. It arrived as transformers and cooling towers and racks of chips. It hums tonight in buildings the size of airports, in the deserts of Arizona, on the plains of Texas, in the farm country of Ohio and Indiana.

Four American companies, Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta, are on course to spend roughly seven hundred and twenty-five billion dollars this year on the machinery of artificial intelligence.

One year. Four companies. Seven hundred and twenty-five billion dollars.

And Alphabet has already told its investors that next year it will spend significantly more.

[LET IT LAND]

Two deposits.

The first went into the pockets of ordinary people, because fifty years ago a group of Alaskans decided that the oil under their feet belonged to all of them.

The second is going into the foundations of a new economy. And nobody has yet written the deed that says who that economy belongs to.

That is why I am here tonight. I did not come to ask whether the machines are coming. They are here. I did not come to ask whether they will be powerful. They already are.

[SLOW]

I came to ask one question. Who owns the machines?

Because whoever owns the machines will own the century.

If the answer is a few thousand shareholders and a few dozen founders, we will have built the most productive economy in history and handed its returns to a smaller circle than at any time since the Gilded Age.

If the answer is all of us, in some real and measurable way, this could be the most broadly shared prosperity human beings have ever known.

No law of physics decides between those two futures. Only laws of Congress, and of state legislatures, and of corporate charters. And the habits of a people who either insist on their share, or do not.

So for the next two hours I want to give you a program, not a mood.

I will tell you about the experiments that have been run, the ones that worked and the ones that did not. I will take you to Alaska and to Norway. I will tell you about workers who own their companies, about training programs that change lives, and about others that waste money and hope in equal measure. I will tell you how to tax a machine without killing the goose. And I will tell you what you, personally, can do.

I will also tell you where I might be wrong. A speech that asks for your trust should earn it.

But I will not pretend to be neutral. I believe that wealth built on the shared inheritance of a whole civilization should be shared by that civilization. Americans have a word for that. The word is not charity. The word is ownership.

[PAUSE]

So let us begin where good arguments about the future usually begin, which is in the past.

## II. A Letter from Newburgh

In June of seventeen eighty-three, the war was over in everything but paperwork. The British had not yet left New York. The treaty of peace had not yet been signed in Paris. And the commander of the Continental Army, sitting at his headquarters in Newburgh, New York, was preparing to go home.

George Washington could have done almost anything that summer. Some of his officers had flirted with the idea of marching on Congress. He had faced them down only months before. Now, instead of seizing power, he sat down and wrote a letter to the governors of the thirteen states.

We call it the Circular to the States. In many ways it was his first farewell. He told the governors he meant to retire to private life. And before he left, he told them what the new country needed in order to survive.

He listed four things. A lasting union under a single federal government. Honest payment of the public debts, so that the nation would keep faith with the soldiers and creditors who had trusted it. A sensible arrangement for the common defense. And a friendly and peaceful disposition among the people, a willingness to set aside local prejudices and make the compromises that a common life requires.

Then he asked that his words be received as the legacy of a man who wished to be useful to his country.

[PAUSE]

Notice what Washington did. He did not simply say "be good" or "be united." He named the specific institutions that would make goodness and unity possible. He offered a program. But he wrapped that program in a vision, a picture of the kind of people Americans could be if they built the right foundations.

A hundred and fifty-eight years later, in January of nineteen forty-one, another president stood before Congress and did the same thing.

Europe was on fire. France had fallen. Britain stood nearly alone. The United States was not yet at war, and a great many Americans wanted to keep it that way. Franklin Roosevelt came to the Capitol to argue for aiding the democracies. But in the middle of a speech about tanks and ships and planes, he stopped and talked about something that seemed, at first, to have nothing to do with war.

He talked about what the American people expected from their own economy. He gave Congress a plain list. Equal opportunity for the young and for others. Jobs for those who can work. Security for those who need it. An end to special privilege for the few. Civil liberties for all. And then the last item, which I want you to remember, because it is the subject of this whole speech.

[SLOW]

He said the people expected to enjoy the fruits of scientific progress in a wider and constantly rising standard of living.

The fruits of scientific progress. He did not say the fruits belonged to the owners of the patents. He did not say they belonged to whoever bought the right stock in the right year. He said a wider standard of living. Constantly rising.

And then Roosevelt lifted the whole thing up into a vision. He named four freedoms that he said should belong to people everywhere in the world. Freedom of speech. Freedom of worship. Freedom from want. Freedom from fear.

Two years later, Norman Rockwell painted those four freedoms for the Saturday Evening Post, and the Treasury Department sent the paintings around the country. If you have ever seen the family gathered around the Thanksgiving turkey, the grandmother setting down the platter, you have seen what Freedom from Want looked like to Americans in the middle of the twentieth century. It looked like plenty, shared at a common table.

[PAUSE]

I tell you about these two documents because tonight I want to do what Washington and Roosevelt did. I want to offer a program and a vision together.

The program has five parts. I will name them now so you can hold them in your mind as we go.

First, a floor. A cash dividend, paid to every citizen, that no one can fall beneath.

Second, a stake. A national wealth fund that owns a real share of the machines, so that the dividend grows as the machines grow.

Third, a share at work. A great expansion of employee ownership, so that the people who work beside the machines own part of the companies that deploy them.

Fourth, a fair ledger. A tax system that stops punishing human labor and starts asking capital, and especially the new capital of compute, to carry its weight.

Fifth, a ladder that holds. Retraining that is built on what the evidence says actually works, and not on what sounds good in a press release.

[SLOW]

A floor, a stake, a share, a ledger and a ladder. Five words. If you forget everything else tonight, keep those five.

And the vision is simple. It is Roosevelt's last item, carried into our own century. That the fruits of the greatest scientific achievement of our time be enjoyed in a wider and constantly rising standard of living. By everyone. And enjoyed the way an owner enjoys them, by right, and not the way a supplicant does, by favor.

## III. The Machines Are Being Built

Let me describe plainly what is happening, because it is easy to lose the shape of it in the noise.

For three years, Americans have been told two stories about artificial intelligence. One says it is a parlor trick, a chatbot that makes up citations. The other says it will replace every job on Earth by next Tuesday.

Neither story is true. And neither story helps you decide anything.

Here is what we can actually measure.

We can measure the money. You have already heard the figure for this year's spending. What I want you to notice is whose money it is. It is private money, building private assets, that will produce private returns.

We can measure the valuations. In April of this year, OpenAI closed a funding round that valued the company at about eight hundred and fifty-two billion dollars. In June, it confirmed it had filed to go public. Nobody pays that price for a parlor trick.

[PAUSE]

And here is something we can measure that I find genuinely interesting. Just a year ago, in October of twenty twenty-five, OpenAI finished converting itself from a nonprofit-controlled company into a public benefit corporation. Under that arrangement, the nonprofit, now called the OpenAI Foundation, held about twenty-six percent of the new company. Microsoft held about twenty-seven percent. Employees and other investors held the rest.

I am not here to praise or condemn that deal. But look at what it represents. The builders of one of the most important AI companies in the world faced, in miniature, the question I am asking tonight. Who should own this? And their answer, at least on paper, was that roughly a quarter of it should be held by an entity whose mission is the benefit of humanity.

If a single company can decide that a quarter of its value should be held in trust for the public good, then a nation of three hundred and forty million people can certainly ask how much of the whole AI economy should be held in trust for its citizens.

[LET IT LAND]

Now, what about jobs?

Here I want to be careful, because this is where the shouting is loudest and the evidence is thinnest.

The best early evidence I know of comes from economists at Stanford, Erik Brynjolfsson and his colleagues Bharat Chandar and Ruyu Chen. They looked at payroll records from ADP, the company that processes paychecks for millions of American workers. And they found something specific.

Young workers, ages twenty-two to twenty-five, in the occupations most exposed to AI, have seen a relative decline in employment since generative AI spread. In their first version of the paper, published in August of twenty twenty-five, that relative decline was about thirteen percent. In their November update, it was about sixteen percent. Meanwhile, older and more experienced workers in the very same occupations held steady or kept growing.

The authors call this early evidence. They do not claim it is settled. I will not claim more than they do.

But think about what that pattern means if it holds. It means the machine is not replacing the senior accountant. It is replacing the job the senior accountant had when she was twenty-three. It is not firing the experienced paralegal. It is quietly not hiring the next one. The ladder is not being knocked over. The bottom rungs are being sawed off.

Let me give you a face for that. She is imagined, but you will recognize her.

Call her Jasmine. She is twenty-four. She graduated from a state university with good grades and a mountain of loans. Her plan was the plan her parents' generation followed. Get a job as a paralegal at a mid-sized firm. Spend a few years doing the grinding work of document review and research memos. Learn the business from the inside. Maybe go to law school. Maybe become an office manager. Climb.

But the firm she wanted to work for did not hire a new class of paralegals this year. Nobody was fired. The senior paralegals are still there, and they are busier than ever, supervising software that now does the first pass of document review in an afternoon. The partners are pleased. The firm's profits are up. And Jasmine is working at a coffee shop, sending out applications, and wondering what she did wrong.

[PAUSE]

She did nothing wrong. She did exactly what she was told. The ladder simply moved while she was climbing toward it.

And here is why this wave is different from the ones that came before. The power loom replaced the hands of the weaver. The tractor replaced the back of the farmhand. The assembly robot replaced the arm of the welder. Each of those machines took over physical tasks, and each time, workers moved up into jobs that required more thinking, more judgment, more education. We told our children, go to college, work with your mind, and the machines will never catch you.

This time, the machine is coming for the mind. Not all of it. Not the deepest judgment, not yet, and perhaps not ever. But the routine intellectual work that served as the entry point into the professional middle class. The first draft. The first pass. The first rung.

That need not mean catastrophe. Jasmine may yet find a better job that does not exist today. But the old advice, get educated and you will be safe, is no longer enough on its own. Education still matters enormously. Yet in an economy where machines do more and more of the thinking, the people who prosper most will be the people who own the machines that think.

[PAUSE]

And there is one more number I want you to hold, because it is the one that ties all of this together.

Economists track something called the labor share. It is the portion of the income produced by the economy that goes to workers as wages and benefits, as opposed to the portion that goes to the owners of capital as profits, interest and rent.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes an index of the labor share in the nonfarm business sector. In the second quarter of twenty twenty-five, that index stood at about ninety-six point seven. In the second quarter of twenty twenty-six, it stood at about ninety-three point four. It fell in every one of those quarters.

A year is not a trend, and I will not tell you that one year of data proves the machines have arrived to take our paychecks. Labor share moves with the business cycle. Economists argue about how to measure it.

But I will tell you what a falling labor share means in plain English. It means that of every dollar our economy produces, a little less goes to the people who work and a little more goes to the people who own.

[SLOW]

And if you build an economy where the most productive new assets are machines, and the machines are owned by a few, then the labor share will fall, and keep falling, not because anyone is wicked, but because that is what the arithmetic of ownership does.

The answer to that arithmetic is not to smash the machines. It is to change who owns them.

You cannot win a race against capital by working harder. You can only win it by becoming an owner of capital yourself.

That is the whole speech, really. Everything else is how.

## IV. We Have Been Here Before

In March of eighteen eleven, in a village called Arnold, just outside Nottingham in the English Midlands, a group of men broke into workshops at night and smashed the knitting frames.

They called themselves followers of General Ludd, an imaginary commander. And for the next several years, across Nottinghamshire and Yorkshire and Lancashire, the Luddites broke machines. Stocking frames. Power looms. The shearing frames that put the skilled croppers of Yorkshire out of work.

Today we use their name as an insult. A Luddite is someone who is afraid of technology, someone too slow or too stubborn to understand progress.

[PAUSE]

But that is not who they were.

The historians who have studied them most closely say the Luddites were not opposed to machines as such. They were opposed to how the machines were used. They were skilled workers watching their wages cut and their trades undercut, by manufacturers who used the new frames to turn out cheap, shoddy goods with cheap, unskilled labor.

Their complaint was not "this machine exists." Their complaint was "this machine is being used against us, and we will get nothing from it."

And what did the British government do? It did not ask how the gains of the new machines might be shared. In eighteen twelve, Parliament made machine-breaking a crime punishable by death. The government sent something like twelve thousand troops into the manufacturing districts. The historian Eric Hobsbawm observed that this was a larger force than Wellington had taken into Portugal a few years earlier to fight Napoleon. At York in eighteen thirteen, men were hanged. Others were transported to Australia.

[LET IT LAND]

The machines won. In the long run, the machines always win. And in the very long run, the descendants of those Yorkshire croppers became far richer than their great-grandfathers could have imagined.

But the very long run is a cruel thing to say to a man whose children are hungry this winter. And it took decades, decades of child labor, of slums, of cholera, of the hungry forties, before the gains of the industrial revolution began to reach ordinary working families in any serious way. They did not reach them automatically. They reached them because people organized, and voted, and fought for factory laws and public schools and the right to form unions.

The lesson of the Luddites is not that technology is bad. The lesson is that the gains of technology are not distributed by technology. They are distributed by institutions. And if you do not build the institutions, the gains will flow to whoever already holds the deeds.

[PAUSE]

Now let me tell you about a man who saw that clearly, more than two hundred years ago.

In the winter of seventeen ninety-five, Thomas Paine, whose Common Sense had helped persuade a continent to declare its independence, sat down in Paris and wrote a smaller pamphlet called Agrarian Justice. It was published in seventeen ninety-seven.

Paine's argument went like this. The earth, in its natural state, was the common property of the whole human race. Nobody made the land. Then came agriculture, and cultivation, and with it private property, because you cannot separate the improvements a farmer makes from the land itself. Paine did not think that was wrong. Private property, he said, had made civilization possible.

But, he argued, the people who took the common land into private hands owed something to everyone else, to the people who had been dispossessed of their share of the natural inheritance. Not charity. A debt.

And so Paine proposed a fund. It would be paid for by a tax on inheritances. And out of that fund, every man and every woman, on reaching the age of twenty-one, would receive fifteen pounds sterling, to help them begin in the world. And every person over fifty would receive ten pounds a year for the rest of their lives.

[SLOW]

Fifteen pounds at twenty-one. A stake to start a life. Ten pounds a year after fifty. A floor beneath old age. Paid for by the wealth that had been taken out of the commons.

Paine wrote that in the seventeen nineties. Strip away the pounds and the powdered wigs and you have almost exactly the program I am going to propose to you tonight. A stake and a floor, paid for out of the returns on a shared inheritance.

Because here is what I want you to understand about artificial intelligence. It, too, was built out of a commons.

These machines learned to write by reading what we wrote. They learned to reason by studying the reasoning of millions of people, scholars and teachers and forum posters and novelists and engineers. They stand on top of decades of publicly funded research, of university laboratories paid for by taxpayers, of an internet that began as a government project. Nobody in this room is the sole author of the machines. But every one of us is, in some small way, a contributor to what they know.

If Paine was right that the people who enclosed the land owed a debt to those who lost their share of it, then we have every right to ask what is owed when the accumulated knowledge of a civilization is enclosed in a model.

A lifetime after Paine, another American asked the same question in a different form.

In eighteen seventy-nine, a self-taught printer and journalist in San Francisco named Henry George published a book called Progress and Poverty. George had watched California grow rich on railroads and telegraphs, and he noticed something that troubled him. Where the new wealth was greatest, poverty was deepest. Progress and poverty were advancing side by side.

How could that be? George's answer was ownership. The gains of progress, he argued, were being captured by those who owned the things that everyone needed and nobody could make more of, above all, land. As the city grew, the land under it soared in value, not because the landlord had done anything, but because everyone else had. And the landlord collected the rise.

George proposed to tax the part of land's value created by the community, and to use it for the common good. His book became one of the best-selling works of its century. You need not accept his whole program to feel the force of his question. When a whole society creates value together, who collects it?

Replace land with compute and data, and Henry George's question is the question of our age.

The value of an AI model comes partly from the brilliance and risk-taking of the people who built it, and they deserve to be richly rewarded. But it also comes from the words of millions, the research of thousands, and the grid and the permits and the schools of an entire nation. The question is whether that shared contribution earns a shared return.

And Americans have answered questions like this before.

In May of eighteen sixty-two, in the middle of the Civil War, Abraham Lincoln signed the Homestead Act. Any eligible person could claim a hundred and sixty acres of public land, and if they lived on it and worked it for five years, it was theirs. Over the following decades, about one point six million homesteads were granted. Something like a tenth of all the land in the United States passed into the hands of ordinary families.

The government owned a continent's worth of land. It could have sold it all to railroad barons and speculators, and a great deal of it did go that way. But a great deal of it was simply handed to working people, on the theory that a republic is stronger when its citizens own property.

And in June of nineteen forty-four, as American soldiers were fighting their way off the beaches of Normandy, Franklin Roosevelt signed the Servicemen's Readjustment Act, the GI Bill. It paid for veterans to go to college and trade school. It guaranteed low-interest, no-down-payment mortgages. It turned millions of renters into homeowners and built the American middle class.

[PAUSE]

But these programs were not perfect, and I promised to tell you where the story is uncomfortable.

The Homestead Act was written on land taken from Native nations, and it excluded many of them unless they gave up their tribal citizenship. The Southern Homestead Act, meant to give land to freed slaves, largely failed. And the GI Bill, administered through local and state institutions shaped by Jim Crow, delivered far less to Black veterans than to white ones. The historian Ira Katznelson called it, bitterly and accurately, affirmative action for whites.

So the lesson of our history is double. Yes, America has known how to turn a shared inheritance into broad ownership. And yes, when we have done it, we have too often drawn the circle of "everyone" too small.

This time, the circle has to be everyone. Not everyone who already has a brokerage account. Not everyone who happens to live near the data center. Everyone.

One more voice from the past, and then we will get to work.

In nineteen thirty, as the Great Depression was taking hold, the economist John Maynard Keynes wrote a short essay called Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren. Everyone around him was in despair. Keynes was not. He said the depression was a temporary sickness, and that the deeper trend of technological progress meant that within a century, the economic problem of scarcity might be solved. He imagined his grandchildren working perhaps fifteen hours a week.

He also gave a name to something people were already afraid of. He called it technological unemployment, unemployment caused by our discovering ways to economize on labor faster than we find new uses for it. And he said it was a phase of maladjustment, a growing pain.

Keynes was right about the wealth. We are vastly richer than the people of nineteen thirty. And he was wrong about the fifteen-hour week. The technology arrived. The gains simply did not flow, as he imagined, into leisure and security for everyone. They flowed unevenly, into more consumption for some and more insecurity for many.

That is the warning. The wealth will come. The question was never whether. The question is always to whom.

## V. The First Commitment: A Floor

The first commitment is a floor. It is the part of the program people argue about most, because it means cash.

The idea is very old. It is in Paine. People on the left and on the right have proposed it, in different forms, for most of the last century. The libertarian economist Milton Friedman proposed a negative income tax. The civil rights movement called for a guaranteed income. And in August of nineteen sixty-nine, a Republican president, Richard Nixon, went on national television and proposed something called the Family Assistance Plan, a guaranteed minimum income for families with children, sixteen hundred dollars a year for a family of four.

[PAUSE]

People forget this. The House of Representatives passed it. Not once, but twice. In April of nineteen seventy, and again, in a later version, in nineteen seventy-one. It died in the Senate, attacked from the right as too generous and from the left as too stingy. But for a moment, the United States came within a Senate committee of a guaranteed income, under a Republican president.

Serious people of every persuasion have kept coming back to this idea. And because they kept coming back, we have something we did not have in Nixon's day.

We have evidence.

[SLOW]

Let me walk you through it, the good and the inconvenient alike.

Start in the nineteen seventies, in a prairie town in Manitoba, Canada, called Dauphin. About ten thousand people. From nineteen seventy-four to nineteen seventy-nine, under a program called Mincome, any resident of Dauphin could apply for a guaranteed income that was reduced by fifty cents for every dollar they earned.

The experiment ended with a change of government, and the data sat in boxes for decades. Then an economist named Evelyn Forget went back and compared the health records of Dauphin with those of similar people elsewhere in Manitoba. She found that hospitalizations fell by about eight and a half percent during the program. Accidents and injuries went down. Hospitalizations for mental health went down.

And did people stop working? Other researchers found that hours did fall, but modestly. For men, by about one percent. For married women, about three percent. For unmarried women, about five.

And where did that time go? Much of it, researchers have argued, went to family and to education. Time with small children. Time in school.

[PAUSE]

Jump forward forty years, to Finland. In twenty seventeen and twenty eighteen, the Finnish government ran a true randomized experiment. Two thousand unemployed people, chosen at random and required to participate, received five hundred and sixty euros a month, tax-free, no conditions, whether they found a job or not. Their results were compared with everyone else on unemployment benefits.

The verdict, published in twenty twenty, was this. The effect on employment was small. The people receiving the basic income worked, on average, about six more days over the measurement period than the comparison group. Not a revolution. But not the collapse of the work ethic either.

And on wellbeing, the effect was clear. The recipients reported less mental strain, less depression, less loneliness. They were more satisfied with their lives. They were more confident about their futures.

Now come to California. Stockton, a city that went bankrupt in twenty twelve. Starting in twenty nineteen, the Stockton Economic Empowerment Demonstration gave a hundred and twenty-five residents five hundred dollars a month for two years.

Here is what happened in the first year. Among the people getting the money, the share with full-time jobs rose from twenty-eight percent to forty.

Among the people who got nothing, it rose too, but less. From thirty-two percent to thirty-seven.

And the share of recipients who could cover a surprise expense, a broken car, an emergency room bill, roughly doubled. From one in four to about one in two.

A small study. I would not build a nation on it alone. But it points in a hopeful direction. Security, it seems, can be a launching pad and not only a hammock. A person who is not panicking about this month's rent can afford to take the time to look for a better job.

[PAUSE]

Now go farther, to rural Kenya, where the largest basic income study in the world is still running.

The charity GiveDirectly, working with independent researchers led by Tavneet Suri of MIT and including the Nobel laureate Abhijit Banerjee, divided about two hundred villages into groups. In some villages, adults receive about twenty-two dollars and fifty cents a month, which is a great deal of money in rural Kenya, for twelve full years. In others, the same amount for only two years. In others, a single lump sum of five hundred dollars. And a set of control villages received nothing.

The early results came out in twenty twenty-three. People did not work less overall. Total household labor did not change. Some people shifted from agricultural wage work to running their own small businesses. People did not drink more. The lump sum and the long-term payment both did a great deal of good. The short-term payment did the least.

[SLOW]

I want you to notice that last finding, because it matters for policy. When people knew the money would keep coming for twelve years, they behaved differently than when they knew it would stop in two. They planned. They invested. They took risks. Permanence changes behavior. A floor that might vanish next year is not really a floor.

And that brings me to the most rigorous American study yet, and to the results that the advocates of basic income, including me, have to be honest about.

## VI. What the Trials Do Not Prove

From November of twenty twenty to October of twenty twenty-three, a research group called OpenResearch, with funding associated with Sam Altman, ran the largest randomized study of unconditional cash in American history. One thousand low-income adults in Texas and Illinois received one thousand dollars a month for three years. Two thousand others, the control group, received fifty dollars a month.

The researchers published their findings on employment through the National Bureau of Economic Research in twenty twenty-four. And they are not what the most enthusiastic advocates had hoped.

[PAUSE]

The people who received the thousand dollars worked somewhat less. Their participation in the labor force fell by about four percentage points relative to the control group. They worked about one to two fewer hours a week. Their partners also cut back by a similar amount. Their total income, not counting the transfer, fell by about nineteen hundred dollars a year relative to the controls. The largest single thing they did with their extra time was leisure.

And the hoped-for upgrade, the idea that freedom from desperation would let people find much better jobs, did not show up in the data. The researchers found no measurable improvement in job quality. And the recipients' sense of wellbeing, which rose in the first year, faded back toward the level of the control group after that.

[LET IT LAND]

I could skip over that study. Many advocates do. I am not going to.

Here is what I think it means. A guaranteed income is not a magic engine of productivity. It does not turn every recipient into an entrepreneur. When you give people money with no strings, some of them, quite reasonably, use some of it to work a little less. To rest. To care for someone. To breathe.

Is that a scandal? Let me ask it another way. When a wealthy person inherits money and works a little less, we do not usually call it a moral catastrophe. We call it financial security. A cut of an hour or two a week, in exchange for the knowledge that you will not be ruined by one broken transmission or one bad month, is a trade that most people with savings make without even thinking about it.

But I accept the honest point underneath the critics' argument. A pure cash program, on its own, is not a full answer to a changing economy. It does not, by itself, build skills. It does not, by itself, give people a stake in the future. It does not, by itself, give them the dignity that many people find in work.

That is why the floor is only the first of five commitments, and not the whole program.

[PAUSE]

And there is one more problem I have to put on the table. Cost.

Do the arithmetic with me. There are roughly two hundred and sixty million adults in the United States. If you gave every one of them a thousand dollars a month, twelve thousand dollars a year, that would cost more than three trillion dollars a year. That is more than everything the federal individual income tax brought in last year, before you have paid for a single soldier, road or hospital.

Nobody honest can tell you that a full universal basic income of that size can be paid for tomorrow morning by taxing a few billionaires. It cannot.

So what do I propose instead?

[SLOW]

I propose that we stop thinking about the floor as a welfare check that must be paid out of today's taxes, and start thinking about it as a dividend that must be earned by tomorrow's assets.

Start small and make it permanent. Pay it to everyone, the way Alaska does, so that nobody is stigmatized and nobody faces a cliff where earning one more dollar costs them their benefit. And tie its size not to the whims of each year's budget fight, but to the growth of a real fund that owns a real share of the new economy. As the machines grow more productive, the fund grows. As the fund grows, the dividend grows.

At first it may be a few hundred dollars a year. A modest thing. But it will be yours, by right, the way a share of stock is yours. And if the optimists about artificial intelligence are even half right about how much wealth these machines will generate, it will not stay modest for long.

And there is one more piece of evidence that makes me believe this can work politically and economically, and it comes from the place where it has already been working for more than forty years.

Two economists, Damon Jones of the University of Chicago and Ioana Marinescu of the University of Pennsylvania, studied the effect of Alaska's dividend on employment. They found no reduction in employment at all. None. The share of Alaskans working part-time rose by about one point eight percentage points, a seventeen percent increase in part-time work, but the overall share of people employed did not fall.

Their explanation is simple. When a whole community receives money at once, it spends it, and that spending creates jobs. Each person may work a little less. But more customers walk through every door in town.

A study that hands money to a thousand scattered individuals cannot capture that. A dividend paid to a whole society can.

[PAUSE]

So let me state the first commitment plainly. A floor beneath every citizen. Universal, so it carries no stigma. Permanent, so people can plan. Modest at the start and honest about what it costs. And growing, not by raising your taxes, but by owning a growing share of the machines.

Which brings us to Alaska, and the question of how a state of ranchers and oil workers and fishermen came to own a fortune together.

## VII. The Second Commitment: A Stake

In nineteen sixty-eight, oil was discovered at Prudhoe Bay, on the North Slope of Alaska, in a field so large that it changed the history of the state.

Alaska had been a state for less than a decade. It was poor, remote and dependent on federal money. And suddenly it was sitting on one of the largest oil fields in North America.

The state held a lease sale in nineteen sixty-nine and took in about nine hundred million dollars, an astonishing sum for a state of its size. And then, within a few years, a great deal of that money was gone. Spent. On roads and schools and salaries and programs, some of them worthwhile and some of them not. And Alaskans, who are not a sentimental people about government, noticed.

[PAUSE]

A Republican governor named Jay Hammond, a former bush pilot and wilderness guide, had been thinking about this problem for years. As a local official in Bristol Bay, he had argued that the fishing wealth of the region should benefit the people who lived there. Now, as governor, he argued that the oil belonged to all Alaskans, including Alaskans not yet born, and that it should not all be spent by whichever legislature happened to be in session when the money arrived.

In November of nineteen seventy-six, Alaskans voted on a constitutional amendment to create a permanent fund. At least a quarter of certain mineral revenues would be deposited into it, and the principal could not be spent. Seventy-five thousand, five hundred and eighty-eight Alaskans voted yes.

Thirty-eight thousand, five hundred and eighteen voted no.

Roughly two to one.

The fund was created. But what to do with its earnings? Hammond's answer was the most radical and the most conservative idea in the history of the state. Give it to the people. Directly. In cash. Every year. So that every Alaskan would have a personal reason to care whether the fund was managed well, and whether its principal was protected from politicians who wanted to raid it.

[SLOW]

In nineteen eighty-two, the first checks went out. One thousand dollars to every eligible resident.

Since then, by one recent estimate, Alaska has paid out about thirty-two billion dollars in dividends.

The check has risen and fallen with markets and with politics. In nineteen eighty-four it was about three hundred and thirty-one dollars. In twenty twenty-two it reached a record, three thousand, two hundred and eighty-four. This month, as you heard at the start, it was twelve hundred.

And here is what that money has done. A study published in twenty twenty-four estimated that the dividend reduced the number of Alaskans living below the federal poverty line by somewhere between twenty and forty percent. Among rural Alaska Natives, it lowered the poverty rate from about twenty-eight percent to below twenty-two percent.

Let me make that concrete. I want you to imagine a woman I will call Mary. She is not a real person I interviewed. She is a composite, the kind of person you would find in any of a hundred villages in western Alaska.

Mary lives with her husband and three children in a town on the Yukon-Kuskokwim Delta. Fuel there costs far more than it does in Anchorage, because it arrives by barge or by plane. So does nearly everything on the grocery shelf. Her husband fishes in summer and works construction when there is construction to work. In October, when the dividend arrives, five people in her household each receive twelve hundred dollars. Six thousand dollars, all at once.

That is heating oil for the winter. That is a new outboard motor so her husband can keep fishing. That is snow boots and a rifle and a plane ticket to Anchorage for a doctor's appointment. That is the difference between a hard winter and a desperate one.

[SLOW]

Now here is the most important thing about Mary's six thousand dollars. Nobody gave it to her out of pity. She does not fill out a form proving she is poor. She is not means-tested. The businessman in Anchorage gets the same check she does. She receives it because she is a part-owner of the wealth of Alaska.

And that is why the dividend is the most politically durable program in the state. Governors who have tried to cut it have paid a price. In twenty sixteen, when Governor Bill Walker vetoed half of that year's dividend to fill a budget hole, it became a political firestorm and a lawsuit that went all the way to the Alaska Supreme Court. The court upheld his power to do it, and the dividend has been fought over in every budget since. But notice what the fight is about. Nobody in Alaska seriously proposes abolishing it. They argue about how big it should be.

Today the fund's earnings supply more than sixty percent of the state's unrestricted general fund revenue. The fund that was created to protect the oil money from politicians has become the thing that pays for the state.

Alaska has a weakness, too. The dividend is not set by an automatic formula anymore. It is set by the legislature each year, and so it rises and falls with budget fights. That is a lesson for any American version. If you want the dividend to be permanent, you must put its rules beyond the reach of the annual raid.

[PAUSE]

Now cross the top of the world, to Norway.

In nineteen sixty-nine, the same year Alaska held its great lease sale, oil was found in the Norwegian sector of the North Sea, at a field called Ekofisk. Norway was a modest country of fishermen and shipbuilders. Within a generation, it was one of the wealthiest nations on Earth.

Many countries have found oil, and many have been cursed by it. The money pours in, other industries wither, the politicians spend the boom, and when the price falls, the country is left with debt. Economists call it the resource curse.

Norway did something different. In nineteen ninety, the Norwegian parliament passed a law creating a fund to hold the nation's oil revenues. The first money was deposited in nineteen ninety-six.

And then, as oil revenues grew and the pressure to spend them grew with them, Norway did something that very few democracies have ever managed. It tied its own hands. It adopted a fiscal rule. Over time, the government may spend only about the expected real return on the fund, currently estimated at about three percent a year. The principal is not touched. The oil is converted, barrel by barrel, into a permanent portfolio of stocks and bonds and real estate all over the world, and only the return on that portfolio is used.

[SLOW]

At the end of June this year, the Norwegian fund held twenty-two thousand, six hundred and eighty-three billion kroner.

That is nearly twenty-three trillion kroner. In dollars, about two point three trillion.

Divide that by Norway's population of roughly five point six million people, and it comes to about four hundred thousand dollars for every man, woman and child in the country.

The fund owns, through its investments, a small slice of thousands of companies around the world. Including, I should point out, many of the very American technology companies that are building the machines we are talking about tonight. When Microsoft or Alphabet or Amazon makes a profit, some part of that profit flows, through the fund, to the people of Norway.

[LET IT LAND]

Hold on to that. The citizens of Norway own a share of American artificial intelligence. Through their public fund, a family in Bergen owns a sliver of the companies building the machines in Ohio.

And the American cashier in Ohio? Some Americans own a share through their retirement accounts. But in the bottom half of our income ladder, according to the Federal Reserve's survey of family finances, about two families in three owned no stock at all. Not even through a retirement plan.

So ask yourself the question of this speech. Who owns the machines? Right now, a family in Bergen has a firmer claim on those machines than many of the families in Ohio who live down the road from them.

Norway does not send its people a check the way Alaska does. It uses the fund's returns to support the national budget, for schools and hospitals and pensions. In the budget it proposed this month, the Norwegian government plans to draw about six hundred and eight billion kroner from the fund for twenty twenty-seven, about two point seven percent of its value. Below the three percent ceiling. Patient. Disciplined. Thinking about grandchildren.

So we have two models. Alaska, which pays a dividend directly to the people. Norway, which funds the common life of the nation. Both of them took a resource that could have enriched a few, and turned it into the common inheritance of everyone.

And I want to say something to my friends who are suspicious of government, because I share some of that suspicion.

Neither Alaska nor Norway nationalized the oil companies. Private companies drilled the wells. Private companies took the risks and earned the profits. The public simply insisted on its share of a resource that belonged to everyone, and then invested that share in the private market, in the companies of the world, as a patient, passive, minority shareholder.

The state does not run the economy. The citizens own a piece of it.

## VIII. An American Commons Fund

So here is the second commitment. I propose that the United States create a national wealth fund. Call it the American Commons Fund, because that is what it is, the common inheritance of the American people, held in trust.

How would it be filled?

Oil was the resource of the twentieth century. It came out of the ground. Alaska and Norway could claim it because the ground belonged to the public.

The resource of this century does not come out of the ground. It is a combination of things. Electricity. Land. Water for cooling. Publicly funded research. The data that all of us have produced. And a scarce, physical thing called compute, the vast arrays of chips that turn electricity into intelligence.

[PAUSE]

Some of that, the public already owns or controls. The electric grid is regulated by the public. The airwaves are licensed by the public. Federal land is owned by the public. Water rights are granted by the public. The great data centers are built with public tax abatements, on public permits, connected to public power lines, and often with public subsidies attached.

So I propose four ways to fill the fund.

First, equity for subsidies. When a company receives tax breaks, cheap land or a place at the front of the line for grid power to build AI infrastructure, the public should receive a small equity stake in return. A share, the way any investor who puts money into a venture expects to own a piece of it. If taxpayers are going to underwrite the build-out, taxpayers should own part of what gets built.

This is not a new idea. When the federal government rescued the banks and the automakers in two thousand eight and two thousand nine, it took equity stakes in return, and it eventually sold them. The principle is simple. The public is not a charity. When it invests, it should own.

[SLOW]

Second, a compute dividend. Data centers draw enormous amounts of power from a grid that the public regulates and, in many places, has paid to expand. I propose a modest, transparent fee on large-scale commercial compute, measured by the electricity it consumes, with the proceeds deposited directly into the Commons Fund. Not into the general budget, where it would vanish into a thousand appropriations. Into the fund, where it would be invested, compounded, and paid back to every citizen as a dividend.

Think of it as the twenty-first century equivalent of the oil royalty. Alaska charges a royalty on every barrel that comes out of public land. We would charge a modest royalty on the industrial-scale use of the public grid to manufacture intelligence. And because it is tied to the physical use of power in this country, it is hard to move offshore. You cannot run an American data center from the Cayman Islands.

Third, voluntary commitments made binding. In twenty nineteen, a group of researchers at Oxford's Future of Humanity Institute, led by Cullen O'Keefe, proposed something they called the Windfall Clause. The idea was that AI companies would commit in advance to donate a significant share of any truly extraordinary profits, the kind they could only earn by transforming the world economy, for the common good.

I like the instinct behind that proposal. But charity is not ownership, and good intentions announced in a research paper are not a contract. I propose that we invite every major AI company to sign a windfall commitment, payable not to a foundation of their choosing, but into the American Commons Fund. And I propose that we make companies' eligibility for certain public contracts and subsidies depend on whether they sign it.

Fourth, and most important, a share of the public's own money, invested in the market from birth.

And here I want to give credit where it is due, because something has already started.

In the tax law passed last year, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, Congress created what are now called Trump accounts. For every American citizen child born from twenty twenty-five through twenty twenty-eight, the Treasury will deposit one thousand dollars into an investment account. Families and employers can add to it. The accounts opened for deposits on the Fourth of July of this year.

And then private citizens joined in. In December of last year, Michael and Susan Dell pledged six and a quarter billion dollars. Their gift puts two hundred and fifty dollars into the accounts of up to twenty-five million children who were too old for the federal deposit, in places where median family income is a hundred and fifty thousand dollars or less.

You may love the name or hate the name. I do not care what we call it. I care about the principle, and the principle is right. A child born in America should begin life as an owner.

And it is not only a federal idea, and not only a Republican one. In twenty twenty-one, Connecticut became the first state to create baby bonds, putting thirty-two hundred dollars aside for every child born into the state's Medicaid program, to be used in adulthood for education, a home or a business.

[SLOW]

Do you see what has happened? Without anyone quite noticing, Democrats in Hartford and Republicans in Washington have both laid down the first bricks of Thomas Paine's fund. Fifteen pounds at twenty-one. A thousand dollars at birth. Thirty-two hundred dollars for a child born poor. The idea that every citizen should start with a stake has quietly become bipartisan.

So let us finish building it. Let the Commons Fund grow these accounts. Let the compute royalty and the equity stakes flow into them. Let them be invested in a broad index of the whole American economy, including the companies that build the machines. And let every American, at eighteen or twenty-one, come into an inheritance that is not given to them by their parents, but by their country.

[PAUSE]

Now, how would such a fund be governed? This is where people rightly worry. A trillion-dollar pool of public money sounds like a politician's piggy bank, or worse, a way for the government to take control of private companies.

So we must learn from Norway, which has solved this problem better than anyone.

The fund must be passive. It invests in broad indexes, not in companies chosen by politicians. It holds minority stakes, never controlling ones. It does not tell companies whom to hire or what to build.

The fund must be independent. It is managed by professionals at arm's length from Congress and the White House, with a board appointed for long staggered terms, the way we appoint governors of the Federal Reserve.

The fund must be transparent. Every holding, every fee, every return, published for every citizen to see, as Norway already does.

And the fund must be protected by a rule that cannot be broken by a single Congress. A fixed share of its value paid out each year as a dividend, divided equally among all citizens, and the principal untouchable. If we must amend the Constitution to guarantee that, the way Alaska did, then let us amend it.

[PAUSE]

Now some arithmetic, because I will not promise you a fortune the numbers cannot deliver.

Start with a fund the size of Norway's today. About two point three trillion dollars.

Suppose it paid out four percent a year. A little more than Norway's rule allows, a little less than Alaska has paid.

That is about ninety-two billion dollars a year. Now share it among three hundred and forty million Americans.

Roughly two hundred and seventy dollars each. For a family of four, a little over a thousand dollars. Helpful. Not life-changing.

Now suppose the fund grew, over a generation, to ten trillion dollars.

That sounds enormous. But a single AI company was valued at more than eight hundred billion dollars this spring. And four companies are spending more than seven hundred billion in a single year. If the machines produce the wealth their builders expect, a public stake of ten trillion a generation from now is not fantasy.

Four percent of ten trillion is four hundred billion dollars a year.

Shared out, that is roughly eleven hundred and seventy-five dollars for every American. About what an Alaskan received this month.

For a family of four, about forty-seven hundred dollars. Every year. For as long as the republic lasts.

I give you these numbers for two reasons. First, so that you know I am not selling you a miracle. A wealth fund is a savings account for a nation, and it grows the way savings grow, slowly and then suddenly.

Second, so that you understand why we must start now. Every year we wait is a year of compounding we lose. Norway made its first deposit thirty years ago, and its people are reaping the rewards today. If we make our first deposit this decade, our children will reap them. If we wait until the machines have already been built and the shares have already been distributed, there will be nothing left to claim.

[LET IT LAND]

A floor that grows because the stake grows. That is the heart of the program. Everything else supports it.

## IX. The Third Commitment: A Share at Work

But a national fund, however well run, is a distant thing. It shows up once a year as a deposit. It does not change what happens on Monday morning when you walk into work.

And I believe that ownership should not only be something you receive. It should be something you exercise. Something you feel in the place where you spend most of your waking hours.

So the third commitment is about the workplace. It is about making it normal, not exceptional, for American workers to own a share of the companies they work for.

[PAUSE]

And it is already happening, on a scale most people do not realize.

According to the National Center for Employee Ownership, there are more than six thousand six hundred employee stock ownership plans in the United States.

They cover about fifteen million people. About eleven million of them are still on the job.

And together they hold more than two trillion dollars in assets.

The employee stock ownership plan, the ESOP, was the brainchild of a lawyer and economist named Louis Kelso, who believed in the nineteen fifties that the problem of the coming automated economy would be that capital would do more and more of the work, and so workers had to become owners of capital or be left behind. He was right, about seventy years early. And he found an unlikely champion in a conservative Democratic senator from Louisiana, Russell Long, who wrote the ESOP into federal law in the nineteen seventies.

[SLOW]

Here is how it works. A company sets up a trust. The trust buys shares of the company, often by borrowing money that the company then repays out of its profits. Those shares are allocated to the workers' accounts over time. The workers do not have to put up their own savings. When they retire or leave, they are paid the value of their shares.

And what does the evidence say?

I want to be careful here, because a great deal of the research on employee ownership is sponsored by organizations that promote it, and because companies that choose to become employee-owned may already have been better run than average. So take these numbers as encouraging, not as gospel.

Start with layoffs. Researchers at Rutgers looked at twenty years of the General Social Survey. Employee owners were laid off at a rate of about one point nine percent. Workers without ownership, about five point one. Fewer than half as often.

Then savings. An earlier analysis of federal pension filings found that people in ESOPs had about two point two times the retirement assets of comparable workers.

A study from twenty twenty-three put it in dollars. In one major category of ESOP companies, the typical worker's account held about eighty thousand five hundred dollars. For similar workers elsewhere, about thirty thousand.

And a meta-analysis of more than a hundred studies found a small but real positive link between employee ownership and company performance.

[PAUSE]

Let me give you a picture. Again, this is an imagined person, but you know him.

Call him Dale. Dale has been a machinist at a mid-sized manufacturer in Ohio for twenty-two years. The founder of the company is seventy-four and has no children who want to run it. A private equity firm has offered to buy it. Dale has seen what happens next in other towns. The firm loads the company with debt, cuts the workforce, sells off the pieces, and moves the work somewhere cheaper. Or it installs a fleet of new machines, and the people who used to run the old ones are shown the door.

Now imagine instead that the founder sells the company to an employee ownership trust. Dale and his coworkers become its owners, gradually, as the loan is paid off. When the company buys new automated equipment, as it must to stay competitive, the profits from that equipment do not flow out to a distant fund. They flow into Dale's account. The machine is not his competitor. It is his employee.

[LET IT LAND]

That is the whole difference. The same machine, the same factory, the same efficiency gains. But in one version, automation is something done to Dale. In the other, it is something done for him.

And this matters enormously right now, for a reason most people overlook. A huge number of American small and mid-sized businesses are owned by people of the baby boom generation who are approaching retirement. Those businesses are going to change hands in the coming years one way or another. The question is whether they are sold to people who will strip them, or to people who work in them.

Other countries have made a deliberate choice. In twenty fourteen, the United Kingdom created a new kind of legal structure called the employee ownership trust. A business owner who sells a controlling stake to a trust for the benefit of all employees pays no capital gains tax on the sale. And employees of those companies can receive tax-free bonuses of up to thirty-six hundred pounds a year. Employee ownership trusts have since become the dominant form of employee ownership in Britain. Some American companies have even used the British model here.

And then there is the most famous example of all. In the Basque country of northern Spain, in nineteen fifty-six, a Catholic priest named José María Arizmendiarrieta and a small group of his former students founded a cooperative in the town of Mondragón. Today the Mondragon Corporation employs about seventy-one thousand people and takes in more than eleven billion euros a year. Its worker-owners vote on how much their executives can be paid relative to the lowest-paid workers, and the agreed ratios range from about three to one up to nine to one.

Mondragon is not a paradise. When one of its largest cooperatives, the appliance maker Fagor, went bankrupt in twenty thirteen, thousands of jobs were put at risk, and critics pointed out that the members fared far better than the non-member workers. Cooperatives can fail like any business. Ownership is not a guarantee. It is a stake.

But a stake is what we are asking for.

[SLOW]

So here is what I propose.

First, an American employee ownership trust, with the same simple tax treatment the British use, so that a retiring founder can sell to her workers as easily as to a private equity fund.

Second, a right of first offer. Before a retiring owner sells to an outside buyer, the workers get a chance at the table.

Third, have the Small Business Administration, and a lending window at the Commons Fund, guarantee the loans that make these buyouts possible.

And fourth, give companies that share ownership broadly a preference in federal contracts for AI and automation, the way we already favor small businesses and veteran-owned businesses.

A worker who owns a share of the company does not fear the machine in the same way. He wants the machine to succeed. He has a seat at the table when it is installed. He has a claim on what it produces. That is how you turn a threat into a dividend.

## X. The Fourth Commitment: A Fair Ledger

The fourth commitment is a fair ledger, which means taxes, which is the part of every speech where the audience checks its phone. Stay with me. This is where the future is quietly being decided.

Here is a fact about the American tax system that most people never notice. We tax work much more heavily than we tax machines.

When a company hires a human being, it pays payroll taxes on that person's wages. Social Security. Medicare. Unemployment insurance. The worker pays her share too. On top of that comes income tax. Every hour of human labor carries a heavy tax load.

When a company buys a machine, the tax code often does the opposite. It lets the company deduct the cost of the equipment, sometimes all at once in the first year, through what accountants call accelerated depreciation or expensing. The machine pays no payroll tax. The machine does not pay into Social Security. The machine will never collect a pension.

[PAUSE]

Now picture the managing partner at Jasmine's law firm, deciding between hiring a new paralegal and licensing software that does roughly the same first pass.

The paralegal comes with a tax bill. The software comes with a tax break.

[SLOW]

We tax the worker and we subsidize the machine, and then we wonder which one the boss chooses.

I am not saying any single one of those rules is foolish. Many of them were written for good reasons, to encourage investment, to modernize factories, to compete with other countries. But added together, in an age when the machine can increasingly do intellectual as well as physical labor, they amount to a thumb on the scale. And the thumb is pressing down on the worker.

So what about a robot tax?

The idea has been around for years. In twenty seventeen, Bill Gates suggested in an interview that if a robot replaces a worker, the robot should be taxed at something like the level the worker was. That same year, South Korea, a nation with one of the highest densities of industrial robots in the world, did something more modest. It reduced the tax incentives it had been giving businesses for investments in automation. Some called it the first robot tax, though it was really the removal of a robot subsidy. Also that year, a committee of the European Parliament considered a robot tax, and the full Parliament rejected it.

I do not think a literal robot tax is the right answer. The critics have a point, and you deserve to hear it.

First, nobody can define a robot. Is a spreadsheet a robot? Is a dishwasher? Is an AI model that helps one lawyer do the work of two a robot, or a tool? Any tax that depends on drawing that line will be gamed by armies of accountants.

Second, a tax that punishes productivity is a tax on the very thing that makes nations rich. The World Bank warned in twenty nineteen that such a tax could reduce productivity and invite avoidance. If we make it more expensive to use the machines here, the machines will simply be used somewhere else, and we will have the job losses without the profits.

[PAUSE]

So instead of taxing the robot, I propose that we fix the ledger. Four changes.

First, stop subsidizing the substitution of machines for people beyond what sound investment policy requires. If we are going to give generous tax treatment to equipment, let us give at least equally generous treatment to the investment a company makes in training and retaining its people. Let a company deduct the cost of an apprenticeship as quickly as it deducts the cost of a server rack.

Second, lighten the tax on work, especially at the bottom. The payroll tax is one of the heaviest taxes most working Americans pay. As the Commons Fund grows and the compute royalty comes in, we should use part of that new revenue to cut payroll taxes on the first dollars that every worker earns. Make hiring a human cheaper, not by cutting wages, but by cutting the tax wedge between what an employer pays and what a worker takes home.

Third, tax capital income in a way that is harder to escape. Today, the largest fortunes in America can grow for decades without ever being taxed, because unrealized gains on stock are not taxed until they are sold, and if they are held until death, the gains can escape income tax altogether through what is called the step-up in basis. That is the way an enormous share of the wealth created by AI companies will be held, in stock that rises and is never sold. If the returns to the machines are going to be the main source of new wealth in this century, then the tax system must be able to reach those returns, at least when they pass from one generation to the next. Paine wanted to fund his stake with a tax on inheritance. He was onto something.

Fourth, tax what cannot move. Much of the profit of the digital economy can be shifted around the world on paper, booked in whatever country offers the lowest rate. But some things cannot be shifted. A data center in Ohio is in Ohio. The power it draws from the grid is drawn in America. The American customers who pay for an AI service are in America. That is why I proposed a compute royalty measured by power use, and why I believe we should tax digital revenue where the customer is, and not where the lawyers say the profit is.

[PAUSE]

A fair ledger does not mean a punitive one. I want these companies to succeed. I want them to build here, hire here and grow here. I want America to lead the world in this technology.

But leadership is not the same as exemption. The railroads paid. The oil companies pay royalties. The broadcasters pay for their spectrum. Every great industry in American history that was built on a public resource has been asked to give something back to the public. The industry that is built on the public's knowledge, the public's grid and the public's research should be no exception.

If we ask nothing of the machines, we will end up asking everything of the people they replace.

## XI. The Fifth Commitment: A Ladder That Holds

Now I come to the commitment that every politician makes and almost none of them keep. Retraining.

Whenever a factory closes or an industry collapses, someone stands at a podium and promises that the displaced workers will be retrained for the jobs of the future. Coal miners will learn to code. Truck drivers will become solar installers.

It sounds wonderful. It costs nothing to say. And too often, for the people who believed it, it has been a cruel joke.

[PAUSE]

Consider the largest American experiment in retraining displaced workers. It is called Trade Adjustment Assistance. It was created in nineteen sixty-two, under President Kennedy, on a simple and decent principle. When the government makes trade deals that benefit the country as a whole but cost some workers their jobs, the government owes those workers help.

For sixty years, Trade Adjustment Assistance paid for training, income support, and job search help for workers whose jobs were lost to imports. Hundreds of thousands of workers went through it.

And then the government did something it should do with every program, and too rarely does. It measured whether it worked.

In twenty twelve, a careful evaluation by Mathematica Policy Research and Social Policy Research Associates followed workers who participated in the program and compared them to similar workers who did not. The finding was devastating. The program had no measurable positive effect on earnings. Participants, years later, were earning less than comparable workers who had simply gone back into the job market, partly because they had spent time in training instead of working, and the training did not pay off enough to make up for it.

In July of twenty twenty-two, the program's authority lapsed, and it was allowed to expire.

[SLOW]

Sit with that for a moment. Sixty years. Large sums of public money. Hundreds of thousands of people who were told that if they did the responsible thing and went back to school, they would land on their feet. And on average, the evidence could not find that it helped their earnings.

Why did it fail? There are many reasons, and economists still debate them. The training was often generic, disconnected from actual employers who were actually hiring. Workers chose courses from a list without good information about which ones led to jobs. The income support often ran out before the training was finished. And the program was designed as a consolation prize for losers of globalization, not as a serious ladder into the middle class.

[PAUSE]

But here is the good news, and it is genuinely good. We also know what works. Not in theory. In randomized trials.

There is a family of programs that researchers call sectoral training. They work differently. Instead of offering general courses and hoping for the best, they start with employers. They find specific industries in a specific region that are actually hiring for specific jobs with decent pay, health care, information technology, advanced manufacturing. They design the training with those employers. They screen applicants carefully. They provide wraparound support, help with child care and transportation and the hundred small crises that knock people out of training. And they stay with graduates after they are hired.

Consider Year Up, a national program that trains young adults without college degrees for jobs in technology and finance, with an internship at a real company built into the program. The federal government sponsored a randomized trial. Six years later, according to the government's own evaluators, participants were earning about thirty percent more than the members of the control group.

Thirty percent. Six years out. In a randomized trial.

Consider Project QUEST in San Antonio, Texas, which trains low-income adults, mostly for health care careers, through community colleges. An independent evaluation found that six years after enrolling, participants were earning more than five thousand dollars a year more than the control group. Over fourteen years, they earned about fifty-four thousand dollars more in total. Those are among the longest-lasting earnings gains ever measured for an American workforce program.

Consider WorkAdvance, a demonstration studied by the research organization MDRC, which tested this sector-based approach in New York, Tulsa and northeast Ohio, including with the technology training nonprofit Per Scholas. The same pattern appears again and again. When training is built around real employers and real jobs, it can change the trajectory of a life.

[SLOW]

So the lesson is not that retraining is hopeless. The lesson is that bad retraining is hopeless, and good retraining is one of the best investments a nation can make.

Here is what I propose. First, fund sectoral training at the scale of the problem. Not pilot programs serving a few hundred people. Hundreds of thousands. Make it available to any worker whose job is displaced by automation, not only by trade, and to any young person whose entry-level rung has been sawed off.

Second, pay people while they train. Most working adults cannot afford to stop earning for six months. A training stipend, on top of the citizen's dividend, is what turns a brochure into a real option.

Third, make the money follow results. Pay training providers in part based on what their graduates earn two and three years later. Publish those results for every program, so that a worker choosing a course can see, as plainly as a price tag, which ones lead somewhere.

Fourth, ask the AI companies to help build the ladder. They know better than anyone which tasks their tools will take over and which new roles will emerge to supervise, check, and work alongside them. Let them co-design programs and guarantee interviews for graduates, the way the best sectoral programs already do with hospitals and banks.

[PAUSE]

And let me say a word to the young people, because they are the ones the Stanford researchers found are being hit first.

Remember Jasmine, behind the counter at the coffee shop with her law-firm applications in a folder. Under this program, the next time she searches for work, she finds a sectoral course built with the firms that are actually hiring, a stipend to live on while she takes it, and a dividend that means one bad month will not sink her.

If you are twenty-three and you cannot find the entry-level job your older brother found five years ago, it is not because you are lazy, and it is not because you are not good enough. It is because the first rung of the ladder is being automated, and nobody has built you a new one yet. That is not your failure. It is ours. And this program is how we fix it.

Retraining alone cannot carry the weight of an economy in transition. Nothing in the evidence suggests it can. That is exactly why it is the fifth commitment and not the only one. A ladder works best when there is a floor beneath it, so that you can afford to climb. And it works best when the thing at the top of the ladder is not just a job, but a share.

## XII. The Strongest Case Against Us

We are past the halfway mark of the evening, and you have heard all five commitments. A floor, a stake, a share, a ledger, a ladder.

Now I owe you something. I promised to take on the strongest arguments against this program, and to state them the way their best defenders would.

The first and most serious objection goes like this. Every great technology has come with predictions of mass unemployment, and every time, the predictions were wrong. The Luddites were wrong. The people who feared the tractor were wrong. The people who feared the computer were wrong.

Machines destroyed jobs, yes, but they created more than they destroyed. In nineteen hundred, a huge share of Americans worked on farms. Today it is a tiny fraction, and we did not get mass unemployment. We got web designers and physical therapists. Why should this time be different?

That is a powerful argument, and it may well be right about the long run. I do not know that artificial intelligence will cause mass unemployment. Nobody does.

But notice that my program does not depend on mass unemployment. Look at it again. A dividend that grows with the economy. A public stake in a booming industry. Workers who own part of their companies. A tax code that stops penalizing hiring. Training that works. Every one of those is a good idea even if every displaced worker finds a new job within a year.

Because the question I am asking is not "will there be jobs?" It is "who will own the gains?" And even in the most optimistic scenario, where AI creates more jobs than it destroys, the profits from the machines themselves will flow to whoever owns them. If the labor share keeps falling, then even full employment will mean a shrinking slice of the pie for the people who work. The farmhands of nineteen hundred found new work. The people who prospered most from the tractor owned the land, and the factories that made the tractors.

[SLOW]

And the transition matters. Even when the long run turned out well, the short run, for the people who lived through it, was often brutal. The handloom weavers of England did not become web designers. Their grandchildren's grandchildren might have. A program like this is how you make sure the transition does not crush a generation on the way to someone else's prosperity.

[PAUSE]

The second objection. You will kill innovation. If you tax these companies, take equity from them, and impose conditions on them, they will build in other countries, and America will lose the most important technological race of the century.

I take this seriously. America's lead in this field is a strategic asset, and I would not trade it away.

But look closely at what I have proposed. I have not proposed a punitive tax on profits. I have proposed a modest royalty on the use of the public grid, which every data center in America uses no matter where its owners live. I have proposed that public subsidies come with a public stake, which is how every private investor in the world behaves. I have proposed that windfall commitments be voluntary, with public contracts as the incentive. And I have proposed passive, minority ownership that gives the government no control over what any company builds.

Norway's fund owns slices of the biggest technology companies on Earth, and none of them has fled Norway's ownership. Alaska has collected royalties on oil for half a century, and the oil companies are still drilling there. Shared ownership has not killed innovation anywhere it has been tried with discipline. What kills innovation is the kind of popular backlash that comes when people feel that a new technology is being done to them. Shared prosperity is not the enemy of the AI industry. It is its insurance policy.

The third objection. Free money makes people idle. You saw it yourself in the OpenResearch study. People worked less.

I did see it, and I told you about it. People worked one to two hours a week less. They did not quit en masse. And where the money was universal and permanent, in Alaska, employment did not fall at all. In Kenya, household work did not fall. In Finland, employment rose slightly.

And we do not, as a society, actually believe that all unpaid time is idle. The mother who spends more time with her newborn is not idle. The son who cuts back his hours to care for his father with dementia is not idle. The volunteer firefighter, the youth coach, the woman who finally goes back to finish her degree. A small dividend that buys some of that time is not a subsidy for laziness. It is a recognition that much of the most important work in our society has never been paid.

[PAUSE]

The fourth objection. Government ownership of the economy is dangerous. A trillion-dollar fund controlled by Washington would become a political weapon.

This is the objection I share most deeply, and it is why I spent so long on governance. Passive, independent, transparent, rule-bound. Small slices of everything, control of nothing. A dividend paid equally to every citizen, so no politician can reward friends or punish enemies. Those are design requirements, and Norway has met them for thirty years.

And consider the alternative. If we do nothing, the ownership of the machines will still be concentrated, only in private hands. I would rather three hundred and forty million Americans each own a sliver of the AI economy than have a few thousand people own nearly all of it. That is not big government. That is a broad distribution of property, the oldest conservative idea in the American tradition.

[PAUSE]

The fifth objection. It costs too much.

I told you the truth about cost. A full basic income of a thousand dollars a month, paid out of current taxes, would cost more than three trillion dollars a year, and we cannot pay for it tomorrow.

That is exactly why I am not proposing to pay for it out of current taxes. I am proposing to build it, year by year, out of new ownership of new wealth. Alaska did not begin with a three-thousand-dollar dividend. It began with a fund and a principle. Norway did not begin with two trillion dollars. It began with a first deposit in nineteen ninety-six. The time to plant the tree is before the harvest. And if the AI boom is as large as its builders believe, the harvest is coming.

If it is not, if the boom proves smaller than promised, then the program costs less, because the royalties and stakes it depends on will be smaller too. It scales with the very thing it is designed to share.

The sixth objection. Why universal? Why should the hedge fund manager get the same dividend as the cashier? Would it not be cheaper and fairer to target the money only to those who need it?

It would be cheaper on paper. I have come to believe it would be neither fairer nor longer-lasting.

Targeted programs create cliffs. A family earns one dollar too much and loses its benefit, and so every raise becomes a trap. Targeted programs require paperwork, and paperwork falls hardest on the people with the least time and the most chaos in their lives. Targeted programs carry stigma, and stigma makes them easy to cut, because the people who pay for them do not receive them.

And here is the deepest reason. A dividend is not welfare. It is a return on ownership. When you own a share of stock, the company does not ask whether you need the dividend before it pays you. You receive it because you are an owner. The whole point of the Commons Fund is that every American is an owner. The billionaire receives the same check, and then, under a fair ledger, pays far more in taxes than the check is worth. The arithmetic still works out in the cashier's favor. But the principle is preserved. We are all in it, and we all own it.

That is why Alaska's dividend has survived for more than forty years in one of the most conservative states in the union. Everybody gets it, so everybody defends it.

[PAUSE]

The seventh objection. People can already buy stock. If Americans want to own a piece of the AI economy, they can open a brokerage account tomorrow and buy an index fund. Why does the government need to do it for them?

In a sense, that is exactly what I want. I want every American to own an index fund. But remember who actually does. You heard the Federal Reserve's finding earlier: in the bottom half, about two families in three owned no stock at all.

And among the bottom half who did own some, the typical holding was about twelve thousand six hundred dollars.

Among the top tenth, about six hundred and eight thousand.

You cannot buy a share of the future with money you do not have. A family living paycheck to paycheck cannot set aside a hundred dollars a month for an index fund, however good the advice. The Commons Fund, the accounts at birth, and employee ownership are simply ways of doing for everyone what the comfortable already do for themselves, so that the returns on the machines do not flow only to those who were already rich enough to buy in before the boom.

[PAUSE]

The last objection is the one I lose the most sleep over. What if it is a bubble?

If the Commons Fund owns a share of the AI economy, and the AI economy crashes, then you have tied every citizen's dividend to a falling market.

That is true, and I will not pretend otherwise. Alaska's check has been as low as about three hundred and thirty-one dollars and as high as three thousand, two hundred and eighty-four. A dividend tied to real assets will rise and fall with them.

But look at how the design answers it. The fund holds a broad index of the whole economy, not a bet on five companies. The principal is never spent, so a bad year shrinks the check without emptying the account. And the floor starts modest, so no family is asked to stake its rent on next quarter's earnings.

A bubble is a reason to own carefully. It is not a reason to own nothing while others own everything.

## XIII. The Other Country

Before I ask anything of you, I want to show you the country we get if we do none of this.

Nothing I am about to describe is a forecast. It is a picture, an imagined one, of where the arithmetic of ownership leads if nobody interrupts it.

[SLOW]

It is twenty forty-five. Jasmine is forty-three.

She never did get the paralegal job. She has worked a dozen jobs since, most of them through an app, most of them supervising software she will never own. She is good at what she does. She is paid by the task. She has no pension, no stake, and no reason to think next year will be better than this one.

The data center at the edge of her county is the largest building anyone there has ever seen. It drew a tax abatement, a new substation and the water rights to a whole valley. It employs a few dozen people. Its profits are booked somewhere else, and its shares are held by people who have never been to her county and never will.

Dale's factory was sold to the private equity firm after all. The new machines arrived on schedule. The profits from them flow, every quarter, to a fund in another state. Dale retired early. Not by choice.

And Mary's grandchildren, out on the Yukon-Kuskokwim Delta, still get their October check, because Alaskans defended it. They are the lucky ones. They are among the few Americans who hold any share of their country's common wealth at all.

[PAUSE]

In that country, nobody smashed the machines and nobody was wicked. The economy grew. The stock market did splendidly. The averages looked fine.

But the deed was written, and most American names were not on it.

A nation of owners became a nation of tenants. Tenants in their jobs, tenants in their homes, tenants in their own economy, paying rent to the machines that were built with their words and their grid and their taxes.

And tenants, history tells us, do not stay patient forever. The Luddites owned nothing of the frames that replaced them. The hungry forties were what that felt like. The backlash that would come, in that country, would not be against the few who owned. It would be against the machines themselves, and against the very idea of progress.

[LET IT LAND]

That is the low road. It does not require a villain. It only requires that we keep doing exactly what we are doing now.

[PAUSE]

But I did not come here to leave you there.

Because every part of that picture is a choice. Not one piece of it is fate. The abatement could have come with a stake. The factory could have been sold to the people who worked in it. The young woman with the folder of applications could have had a floor beneath her and a ladder in front of her.

We know how to build the other country. We have seen it built, in pieces, in Juneau and in Oslo, in Hartford and in Mondragón. What we have not yet done is decide to build it here, at scale, for everyone.

That decision does not belong to the companies. It belongs to you.

## XIV. What You Can Do

Now I want to talk to you directly. Not to Congress. Not to the companies. To you.

Washington did not ask the governors to admire his letter. He asked them to act. Roosevelt did not describe the four freedoms for a poster. He described them so that Americans would build them.

So here is what I ask of you.

But first, let me tell you why I believe ordinary people can move something this large.

Every institution I have described tonight began as an idea that serious people called impractical. A permanent fund owned by the citizens of a frontier state was a strange idea in nineteen seventy-six, until two Alaskans in three voted for it. Employee ownership was the eccentric notion of one lawyer in San Francisco, until a senator from Louisiana wrote it into law. Accounts that give every newborn a thousand dollars were a think-tank proposal, until last year, when they became federal law.

Ideas become institutions when enough people decide to stop treating them as impossible. That is the only magic there is in democratic politics. And it has worked before, again and again, when people insisted.

[PAUSE]

If you are a voter, and I hope every one of you is, then make this a question in every race you vote in. Ask your candidates, at every level, one simple question. Who should own the machines? Ask them whether they support a national wealth fund. Ask them whether they will vote for employee ownership trusts. Ask them whether public subsidies for data centers should come with a public stake. Do not accept vague answers about "the future of work." Ask about ownership.

If you live in a state or a town where a data center is being proposed, and many of you do, go to the public meeting. You have more power there than you think. Communities are being asked to give up land, water, power capacity and tax revenue. Ask what the community gets in return. Ask for a community stake, a local fund, a dividend for residents, a training pipeline for local workers. Alaska started as a state-level idea. The American Commons Fund can start at the county level.

If you own a business, and you are thinking about what happens to it when you retire, I ask you to talk to your employees before you talk to a private equity firm. Find out whether an employee buyout could work. There are organizations and advisers who do nothing but help owners make that transition. You built something. Consider leaving it to the people who helped you build it.

If you have a child born in the last two years, open the account. Put in what you can, even if it is twenty dollars. And if you are lucky enough to have money to spare, put some in the account of a child who is not your own. The Dells did it with billions. You can do it with a birthday check.

[SLOW]

If you work at one of the companies building these machines, and some of you do, I ask you to remember that you are not only an engineer or a researcher. You are a citizen, and you have more influence over how this technology is shared than almost anyone. Push your company to sign a windfall commitment. Push it to support training programs that work. Push it to share equity with the people who label its data and clean its offices, not only with the people who write its code. Some of the founders of this industry have said, in public, that the gains must be widely shared. Hold them to it.

If you are a young person who cannot find the job you were promised, do not let anyone tell you it is your fault. Organize. Vote. Find the training programs with real results, and demand that your state fund more of them. Your generation will live longest with whatever ownership structure we build now. You should have the loudest voice in building it.

And if you are none of those things, if you are simply a person who believes that a country should take care of its own, then talk about it. At the dinner table. At church. At work. On the bus. The reason Alaska's dividend survives is that every Alaskan has an opinion about it. Every Alaskan argues about the size of the check. Not one of them has forgotten whose money it is.

[PAUSE]

None of this will happen because a speech was given. It will happen if enough people decide it must.

## XV. Freedom from Want, Freedom to Own

Let me close where I began, with two deposits.

One went into the bank accounts of Alaskans this month, because fifty years ago, a bush pilot governor and a two-to-one majority of voters decided that the riches of the land belonged to all of them. The other is being poured into concrete across the American heartland, building the most powerful machines in human history, and the deed to those machines has not yet been written.

[SLOW]

We are the generation that writes it.

Washington, preparing to leave public life, told his countrymen that this was the moment that would decide whether the revolution was a blessing or a curse. He said the choice was theirs. Roosevelt, with the world on fire, told his countrymen that they were fighting not only for survival but for a world in which every person could be free from want and free from fear.

I believe we stand at a moment like theirs. We are not at war, thank God. But we are at a turning. A moment when the institutions we build, or fail to build, will shape the lives of our children and grandchildren for a century.

So let me state the program one last time, plainly, as Washington would have.

A floor. A dividend paid to every citizen, universal and permanent, that no one can fall beneath.

A stake. An American Commons Fund that owns a real share of the new economy, so that the dividend grows as the machines grow, governed with Norway's discipline and protected from every raid.

A share. Employee ownership made as easy as a sale to a stranger, so that the people who work beside the machines own part of what they produce.

A fair ledger. A tax code that stops paying employers to replace people, lightens the burden on work, and asks the new wealth to carry its weight.

A ladder. Training built on what works, paid for at the scale of the need, judged by results, and open to every worker and every young person whose first rung has been taken away.

[BUILD]

And now let me tell you the vision, as Roosevelt would have.

I see a country where the child born tonight in a hospital in Gary, Indiana, or on a reservation in South Dakota, or in a farmhouse in Iowa, begins life not as a debtor but as an owner, with a stake in the wealth of the nation already working for her.

I see a country where Dale, the machinist in Ohio, watches the new robot arrive on the factory floor and feels anticipation instead of dread, because part of what it earns will be his.

I see a country where Jasmine, and every young graduate like her, is never again told to pull herself up by bootstraps the machines have already taken. Where she is offered a real ladder, and a floor to stand on while she climbs.

I see a country where every October, in every state, and not just in Alaska, a deposit arrives in every account. Where the family in Gary and the family in Bethel open the same notice on the same morning. And where every citizen knows what that deposit is. A dividend. Their share. Their inheritance. Their part of the greatest wealth humanity has ever produced.

Mary already lives in that country. I want the rest of America to join her.

[BUILD]

That is freedom from want, brought into our century.

But I want to add one more freedom to Roosevelt's four. Not to replace them. To complete them.

Freedom to own.

The freedom to own a share of the machines that do the work of the world. The freedom to stand in your own country not as a tenant but as a proprietor. The freedom that Paine imagined, that Lincoln signed into law on the frontier, that the GI Bill brought to millions of veterans, and that we must now extend, at last, to every single American, with no one left outside the circle.

[PAUSE]

The machines will be built. That is settled. The money is already in the concrete.

What is not settled is whether we will look back on this century as the age when a few people came to own the minds of the world, or as the age when a free people decided that the greatest inheritance of their civilization belonged to all of them.

I know which country I want to live in.

I believe you do too.

So let us go and build it. Let us write the deed with every name on it. And let it be said of us, a hundred years from now, that when the machines came, we did not smash them, and we did not kneel to them.

[LET IT LAND]

We owned them. Together.

[SLOW]

So when your children ask you who owns the machines, I want you to be able to give them the answer Alaskans give every October.

We do.

Thank you.
