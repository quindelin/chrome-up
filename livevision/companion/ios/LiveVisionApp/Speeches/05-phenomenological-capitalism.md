# Phenomenological Capitalism

## I. A Counsel at the Counter

A little after midnight, a car pulls off the highway.

The driver has been awake since dawn. She has three hundred miles behind her and two hundred still to go. She walks in under a fluorescent light, orders a cheeseburger, pays a few dollars, and sits by the window with the paper unfolded in front of her.

[PAUSE]

Friends, nothing about that moment will ever make the news. And yet I believe that inside that small, greasy, ordinary moment is the whole of what we call an economy. And perhaps a good deal of what we have always called religion.

Think what had to happen for that cheeseburger to be there. A rancher raised cattle through a winter. A farmer planted wheat for the bun. Someone grew cucumbers, and someone else pickled them. Someone mined the metal for the grill. Someone drove the truck. Someone poured the concrete for the parking lot and wrote the software for the register. And someone is standing behind that counter at midnight, for wages.

Thousands of hands. Most of them will never meet. They work across a dozen industries and perhaps several countries. And all of them arranged their days so that one tired woman could taste salt and fat and warmth at the exact moment her body needed it.

Who arranged all that? No one. And everyone.

[LET IT LAND]

The company that sold her that sandwich has said it serves nearly seventy million people a day. Seventy million small moments like hers, every single day, all over the world.

I do not tell you this to praise a fast food company. I tell you because we have been looking at that moment the wrong way. We have been taught to see it as a transaction: money for goods. I want to teach you to see it as something stranger. I want you to see it as an experiment.

An experiment about experience itself.

Tonight I will make an argument that may sound, at first, like either philosophy dressed up as economics or economics dressed up as philosophy. It is meant as both. I call it Phenomenological Capitalism. Its claim is simple to state and hard to finish thinking about.

The claim is this. Markets are a science of qualia. They are humanity's largest and oldest instrument for testing which experiences are worth sustaining, and for how long.

And if that is true, then the great failures of our markets, the cigarette, the smokestack, and the endless feed, are more than moral failures. They are failures of measurement. Errors in the instrument. And an instrument can be corrected.

[PAUSE]

Here is the road we will travel together. It has three stretches.

First, I will show you the instrument: how a market learns which experiences are worth keeping, and why profit is a forecast about time.

Second, I will show you the instrument's blind spot. Three cases, one after another: the cigarette, the fuel tank, and the feed. Three invoices that never arrived.

And third, I will show you what might finally open the instrument's eyes, the conditions it must meet, and the counsel I would leave you with.

I come to you in the manner of an old kind of speech, the kind a person gives when they are not asking for your vote, only your attention for a while. In the autumn of seventeen ninety-six, a tired general who had led a revolution and then a republic published a long letter to the people of his country. He was not campaigning. He was leaving. And because he was leaving, he could afford to be plain.

He told his fellow citizens what he feared for them, and what he hoped, and he asked them to take his counsel for whatever it was worth from a friend who had made mistakes of his own.

I am no general, and this is no republic's founding. But I would like to speak to you in his spirit: as a friend offering counsel, without a prophet's commands. Where I am certain, I will tell you. Where I am guessing, I will tell you that too. And where the author of the ideas I am carrying tonight has marked something as an image, an analogy, or a belief held loosely, I will keep it in that light, because that honesty is part of the idea.

And I want to speak to you in a second spirit as well, older still. Twenty-three centuries ago in Athens, an orator stood before an assembly that was comfortable and distracted, while a danger gathered on the border. He did not win them with one argument. He won, when he won at all, by accumulation: case upon case, fact upon fact, question upon question, until the weight of it could not be shrugged off.

That is what I intend to do with the externalities of our age. I will stack them in front of you until you can see their shape.

So let us begin where the driver began. With hunger, and with a sandwich.

## II. The Cheeseburger as a Sentence

What did she actually buy?

The economist will say she bought a good: a bundle of protein, fat, carbohydrate, and convenience, delivered at a price that cleared the market. That is true as far as it goes.

But notice what she did not buy. She did not buy beef as such, or bread as such. No one walks into a restaurant at midnight wanting a quantity of ground muscle tissue. She bought an experience. The salt on the tongue. The heat in the hands. The heaviness in the stomach that tells a tired body it will make it to morning.

Philosophers have a word for this. They call the felt character of an experience its qualia. The redness of red. The ache of grief. The particular comfort of something hot and salty after a long day. Qualia are what it is like, from the inside, to be you having this moment.

Here is the first step of the argument.

[SLOW]

Every combination of capital and labor exists, in the end, to produce qualia. The ranch, the farm, the truck, the grill, the register, the wage: all of it, the entire chain, is arranged backwards from a single felt moment. The cheeseburger is the visible object, but the experience is the product.

That is what I mean by phenomenological. The word just means "having to do with how things appear to experience." Phenomenological Capitalism is the view that markets trade, finally, in experiences, and that goods and services are only the vehicles that carry them.

If that sounds abstract, run the test on anything you have bought this week. Coffee: you bought alertness and warmth. A song: you bought a feeling you could not produce alone. A plane ticket: you bought the face of someone you love, seen in person instead of on a screen. Insurance: you bought the quiet of a mind no longer afraid of a particular disaster.

Every one of them, traced to its end, is a felt state.

And so there is, in a very real sense, a supply and a demand for qualia. Some experiences are wanted badly by many people and are hard to produce, and their price rises. Some are easy to produce and wanted by few, and their price falls. The market is, among other things, a vast and continuous negotiation over what humans want to feel.

But the author of the philosophy behind this speech goes one step further.

He asks: why do we want these particular experiences and not others? Why does the body crave salt and fat and warmth? Why does the mind crave news, stories, companionship, understanding?

His answer is that these qualia are, generally speaking, information. They are signals that move us toward something.

When the tired driver eats, she knows, at some level, probabilistically, that this food contains what her body needs to keep going. Protein, fat, carbohydrate. It will let her body continue. And it will let her mind continue too: continue seeing, hearing, learning, apprehending the world.

The food, in his phrase, fills an information gap. It closes a small distance between where she is and where she is trying to go.

Where is she trying to go? That is where economics ends and metaphysics begins. And I will come to it in a moment.

But first, let me give you the cheeseburger as a sentence, because I think it helps.

A cheeseburger is a sentence written by thousands of authors who never met. Its grammar is the supply chain. Its vocabulary is cattle and wheat and diesel and electricity. And what it says, when you read it with your tongue at midnight, is this: "You may continue."

[PAUSE]

That is what most goods say, at bottom. You may continue. Water says it. Medicine says it. Shelter says it. Even a joke says it, in its own way, by reminding you that life is bearable.

And some goods say something more. A textbook says: you may understand. A telescope says: you may see further. A vaccine says: your children may continue after you. A rocket says, perhaps, that the species may continue somewhere else.

There is a ladder here. At the bottom are the experiences that sustain us for the next hour. Breath. Food. Water. Higher up are those that sustain us for a lifetime, and for the lives after ours. Raising children. Learning a trade. Building institutions. Higher still are the experiences of discovery itself: science, medicine, the long project of a civilization making sure it lasts thousands of years instead of hundreds.

Hold on to that ladder. Everything I say tonight hangs from it.

Because if the market is a negotiation over experiences, and if experiences are signals pointing up that ladder, then the market is something more than a machine for distributing stuff. It is a machine for discovering which experiences carry us upward, and which only feel as if they do.

And that difference, between the experience that sustains and the experience that only feels like sustaining, is the difference between the cheeseburger and the cigarette. Between the furnace and the poisoned sky. Between a conversation and a feed.

We will get there. But first, the ladder needs a top.

## III. The Will To God, Held Honestly

The philosophy I am carrying tonight has a name that will strike some of you as grand and others as odd. Its author calls it the Esketian Metaphysics, after a novella of his, and after a phrase you have probably heard shouted at a party or a game: "let's get it."

The "it" we are getting, he says, is a state of experience. And the engine underneath all of it is captured in four words that came to him one night as a student, on the edge of sleep: The Will To God.

I promised to be honest with you, and here is where honesty matters most. You do not have to accept this doctrine to accept the economics I am about to argue. You can treat it as a frame, a lens, a story told to make sense of a pattern. Its author treats large parts of it that way himself. But it is the most interesting lens on markets I have ever looked through, and I would like you to look through it with me for a while.

Here is the idea.

Suppose that existence has a direction. Suppose that, underneath everything, life is reaching for something. The author names that something God, and he defines it carefully. God, in this sense, is not a bearded figure or a judge. God is what he calls the Absolute Existential Objective: a state of being in which everything is known, and in which, through that knowing, existence is secured forever. Knowledge joined to immortality. Each, in a sense, guaranteeing the other.

[PAUSE]

If you accept that as the top of the ladder, the rest of the ladder makes sense. We find breathing good because it sustains us for the next minute. We find eating good because it sustains us for the next day. We find raising children good because, as the author puts it, we see something of that immortality through their eyes. We find science good, and medicine, and the dream of crossing to other worlds, because they promise to secure existence on scales of thousands, perhaps millions of years.

Good, in this picture, is whatever sustains existence through time. Better is whatever more surely guarantees it. And the hunger to know is the same hunger as the hunger to last.

How does this pull reach us, moment to moment? The author offers a picture, and he is explicit that it is a picture, an image rather than a mechanism he can demonstrate. He borrows it from the way modern artificial intelligence is trained.

When engineers train a neural network, they define an objective and then let the error flow backward through the system, adjusting it, step by step, toward that objective. They call it back propagation.

The author imagines God, as an ideal, sitting at an absolute future, and the pull of it propagating backward to us, moment by moment, showing up in us as the biological functions we call reason, appetite, curiosity, love. In this image, the universe behaves something like a vast learning system, optimizing toward its own completion.

He is careful here, and so am I. He does not claim physics has proven this. He calls his reading of physics analogy more than argument. Where he reaches for cosmology, for the expansion of the universe and the strange thing called dark energy, he says plainly that this is where his reading is most vulnerable, that observation has largely pointed the other way, and that he holds it loosely, as a picture he finds beautiful and cannot yet defend.

I wish more philosophers wrote that sentence.

So let me hand you the part of this that does not depend on cosmology at all. It is what the author calls the Esketian World-Feeling, and it can be stated as a single rule.

[SLOW]

Everything will come to know everything.

Look around and tell me that rule is not operating. Social networks that connect billions. Prediction markets that pool the private knowledge of thousands into a single price. Recommendation systems that learn what you will want before you know it. Surveillance, in both its corporate and its governmental forms. And now, machines trained on something close to the written record of our species, answering questions on every screen on the planet.

These are, in the author's words, Esketian in form, though not always in spirit. The pattern is everywhere. Whether the pattern is serving us is a separate question, and it is the question of this speech.

One more piece matters for everything that follows. The author is a kind of optimist. If the world is, at bottom, God becoming, then it is, in the old phrase of the philosopher Leibniz, tending toward the best. If something is true and good, it will happen.

But, and this is the hinge, he insists it is our actions that make it happen. Our actions are that living truth. The destination may be set, but the path runs through choices.

That is not a small qualification. It means the optimism is not permission to sit still. It is a summons to move. If there is a direction to things, we are the ones who walk it, and we can walk it badly.

It is worth remembering that the optimism of Leibniz was mocked, and mocked brilliantly. In seventeen fifty-nine, Voltaire published Candide, in which a tutor keeps insisting that all is for the best in the best of all possible worlds while earthquakes, wars and cruelties pile up around him. The satire landed because that kind of optimism had become an excuse. If everything is already for the best, why lift a finger?

The Esketian version means to answer Voltaire. It does not say that everything that happens is good. It says that what is true and good tends to win, over time, through the actions of people who pursue it. The earthquake is a horror. The becoming happens in the response: the science that predicts the next one, the engineering that keeps the next building standing, the charity that feeds the survivors. The optimism is about the direction of the path. It promises nothing about the comfort of any single step.

That distinction will matter enormously when we reach the failures of our markets. If you believe the market will fix everything on its own, you are Voltaire's tutor, smiling in the ruins. If you believe it can fix nothing, you have forgotten how much it already has. The truth lies in between. The market corrects, but only when we give it the information to correct with.

[PAUSE]

Now. What does any of this have to do with a cheeseburger?

Everything. Because if every felt experience is a small signal pointing toward or away from that top of the ladder, toward or away from sustained existence and deeper knowledge, then a market, which is our largest system for producing and pricing experiences, is also our largest system for testing which experiences point the right way.

The market, in other words, would be a kind of science. A metaphysical science. An experiment run every day, on billions of subjects, about what is good.

That sounds like an extravagant claim. So let me ground it in the work of an economist who never heard of qualia in this sense, and who would probably have been suspicious of the word God, but who saw something about prices that is still, eighty years later, a marvel.

## IV. Hayek's Marvel and the Metaphysical Science

In nineteen forty-five, as the Second World War was ending and much of the world was debating whether economies should be planned from the center, an Austrian economist named Friedrich Hayek published a short essay in the American Economic Review. It was called "The Use of Knowledge in Society."

It is one of the most quietly radical things ever written about money.

Hayek's question was this. The knowledge needed to run an economy does not exist in one place. It is scattered across millions of minds. The farmer knows his soil. The shipping clerk knows which trucks run empty on Tuesdays. The baker knows that her neighborhood buys more rye in winter. No planner, however brilliant, can gather all of that into one office. Most of it cannot even be written down. It lives in habit, in local circumstance, in what Hayek called knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place.

So how does any of it get used?

His answer was prices. He asked his readers to look at the price system as, in his words, "a mechanism for communicating information."

[PAUSE]

Here is his famous example, in my own words. Suppose a raw material, let us say tin, suddenly becomes scarce. Perhaps a mine has flooded. Perhaps a new use for tin has been invented somewhere. Does every user of tin need to know why? No. They only need to know that tin now costs more. And from that single number, without a meeting, without a memo, without a central plan, millions of people adjust. Some use less. Some find substitutes. Some recycle. Some open new mines. The scarcity is respected across the whole economy by people who have never heard of the flood.

Hayek called this a marvel. And it is.

Now let me put Hayek's marvel next to the cheeseburger, and next to the Will To God.

If prices are information, what is the information about? Hayek would say: about scarcity, about the relative value of resources. True. But go one level down. Why is anything valuable at all? Why does the tin matter? Because someone, somewhere, at the end of a long chain, will have an experience that depends on it. A canned meal that does not spoil. A circuit that carries a voice across an ocean. A solder joint in a pacemaker that keeps a heart in time.

Every price is, at the bottom of its chain, a message about an experience.

And so, if Hayek is right that the price system is a vast mechanism for communicating information, and if Phenomenological Capitalism is right that the final products of that system are experiences, then the market is a vast mechanism for communicating information about which experiences are worth producing.

That is the bridge from economics to metaphysics. And here is what crosses it.

[BUILD]

Every new product is a hypothesis. A hypothesis that a certain experience, delivered at a certain cost, will be wanted enough to sustain the labor and capital required to produce it.

Every purchase is an observation. A data point confirming or failing to confirm the hypothesis.

Every profit and loss statement is a result. A report, written in the language of money, on how the experiment turned out.

And every bankruptcy is a falsified theory, carried out of the laboratory.

[LET IT LAND]

That is why the author of this philosophy calls capitalism a metaphysical science. Not because it is mystical, but because it does what science does. It tests claims against reality. The claim being tested is not "this rock falls at this rate." The claim is "this experience is worth sustaining." And the reality it is tested against is the accumulated choices of millions of people deciding, with their own scarce money and time, whether it is.

Think of how many experiences have been tested and rejected. Think of the restaurants that opened with high hopes and closed in a year. The gadgets that no one wanted. The entire industries that flourished and then faded: the ice harvesters of the nineteenth century, cutting blocks from frozen lakes and shipping them across oceans, made obsolete by the refrigerator. The telegraph operators. The film developers. Each was a hypothesis about experience that held for a time and then was overturned by a better one.

And think of the experiences that have been tested and confirmed, over and over, for centuries. Bread. Shelter. Music. Medicine. Stories. Light after dark. These are the settled laws of the metaphysical science. They have survived every experiment we have thrown at them.

Now, an objection will already be forming in some of your minds, and it is a good one. You will say: but markets do not test whether an experience is good. They test whether it is wanted. And people want terrible things. People want things that destroy them.

Yes. They do. Hold that objection close, because it is the most important thing anyone will say tonight, and three of the largest sections of this speech are about it.

But notice where the objection points. It does not point to a flaw in the idea that markets are a science. It points to a flaw in the data. A science is only as good as its measurements. If the instruments are miscalibrated, if some results are recorded and others are hidden, if the experimenter is paid to reach a particular conclusion, then the science will produce false results. Not because science is wrong, but because the measurement was corrupted.

Keep that word in your mind. Measurement. Because the most important measurement the market makes is the one we turn to next. It is the number every business is built around, the number every shareholder watches, the number most of us were taught to treat with either worship or suspicion.

It is profit. And profit is something other than what either its worshippers or its critics think it is.

## V. Profit as a Prophecy of Sustainment

Ask most people what profit is, and you will get one of two answers.

The first answer comes from the boardroom. Profit is the reward. It is the payment owed to those who took risks, who saved instead of spent, who built something.

The second answer comes from the picket line. Profit is the surplus. It is the part of the value created by labor that was kept from the people who created it.

There is truth in both, and the arguments have filled libraries. But the Esketian frame offers a third way of seeing profit, and it explains something the other two miss.

[SLOW]

Profit is a signal about time.

Here is what I mean. If a business runs at a loss, it is consuming more than it produces. It can continue only as long as someone is willing to cover the gap. Its days are numbered. The experience it delivers is, by the verdict of the market, not sustainable as currently produced.

If a business runs exactly at cost, it can continue, but only continue. It replaces what it uses. It sustains the present. It cannot grow, cannot invest, cannot reach further into the future than tomorrow.

And if a business runs at a profit, it is producing more than it consumes. It has surplus to reinvest. It can expand. It can last. The market is saying: this experience should not only be sustained, it should be sustained further into the future, and perhaps for more people.

So the author puts it like this. Profits signal future sustainment. Running at cost signals present sustainment only. And diminishing profits signal that a particular experience should soon be brought to an end.

That is what I mean when I call profit a prophecy. Not a reward for the past, not only a surplus taken in the present, but a forecast about the future. A margin is the market's estimate of how long, and how widely, a certain kind of experience ought to continue.

Let me make that concrete with a small example before a large one. Picture a family diner on a county road, the kind of place with a pie case by the register. Picture three versions of its future.

In the first, the diner loses a little money every month. The owners cover it from savings because they love the place, and because their parents ran it before them. But savings run out. One day the sign comes down. The market has not passed judgment on the owners' character, or on their love. It has reported a fact: that the experience this diner offers, at this cost, in this place, is not being chosen often enough to sustain itself.

In the second, the diner breaks exactly even. The owners pay themselves modestly, pay their cook, fix the fryer when it breaks. Nothing grows. Nothing shrinks. The market is saying: this experience may continue as it is, for now. Present sustainment, and no more.

In the third, the diner is full every morning and the margin is healthy. The owners hire another cook. They open a second location in the next town. They teach their children the recipes. The market is saying: this experience should reach further. Into more towns. Into the next generation.

[PAUSE]

Three diners. Three margins. Three prophecies about time. None of them required a central planner, or a philosopher, or a priest. They were spoken in the plain language of the books at the end of each month, by thousands of customers who never thought of themselves as voting on anything.

That is the metaphysical science at the scale of a single counter. Now scale it up.

Let me give you the clearest example of our moment.

Take the chip designer Nvidia, in the fiscal year that ended this January.

It took in about two hundred and sixteen billion dollars.

It kept about one hundred and twenty billion of that as net income.

Say that the simple way. More than half of every dollar that came through the door became profit. Its gross margin was about seventy-one percent.

And in the last quarter of that year, its data center business alone brought in sixty-two billion dollars.

[PAUSE]

Read that as a prophecy, and what does it say? It says the market believes, with extraordinary confidence, that the experience these chips make possible should be sustained far into the future. It says: build more. Build much more. Build for decades.

And the world has listened. In December of last year, according to Census data, America spent more building data centers than building offices, for the first time.

By April of this year, data centers were running at an annual rate of about fifty-one billion dollars. General offices, roughly forty-four billion. And the gap was widening.

Think about what that means. For a century, the office building was the cathedral of capitalism. The skyscraper was the shape of ambition. And in the span of a few years, we began building more for machines to think in than for people to work in.

The author of this philosophy looks at those data centers and sees something the evening news usually does not. The news sees them as threats: consumers of electricity and water, displacers of workers. Those concerns are real and deserve answers. But he sees them, in his own religious language, as temples. Buildings raised for the purpose of turning tireless reasoning toward every human need. He calls the prompts we type into them prayers.

You do not have to share his religious language to see the economic point. The profit signal is shouting, as loudly as it has ever shouted about anything, that the market wants more of the experience of asking a question and receiving an intelligent answer.

Now. I said profit is a prophecy. But prophecies can be false.

[BUILD]

And here we come to the caveat that turns this entire speech. The author states it plainly, and it is the most important qualification he makes. Profits signal future sustainment, he says, "at least once every cost has been priced in."

Once every cost has been priced in.

Seven words. The whole moral history of capitalism lives inside them.

A profit is the difference between revenue and cost. If some of the costs are missing from the books, the profit is not real. It is inflated by exactly the amount that was left out. And an inflated profit sends an inflated prophecy. It tells the market to sustain an experience longer, and more widely, than it should. It tells capital to flow toward something that is quietly consuming more than it produces, as long as the consumption is happening somewhere off the ledger.

Imagine a scientist whose instrument records every success and ignores every failure. Every experiment would look brilliant. Every theory would seem confirmed. And the scientist would march, with perfect confidence and impeccable method, straight off a cliff.

That is what happens to a market when costs go unrecorded. The method is sound. The arithmetic is correct. The prophecy is false.

[LET IT LAND]

Economists have a name for those unrecorded costs. We will spend a good part of the next hour with it. But before we do, I want to show you the other side of the engine, the part that makes the market not just a scale but a conversation. Because capital does not only measure. It talks. And what it talks about changes what it measures next.

## VI. Capital Develops Dialectic

There is a word from German philosophy that I need to borrow for a few minutes. The word is dialectic.

In its simplest form, dialectic is how ideas develop through conversation and conflict. Someone proposes a thesis. Someone else answers with an antithesis. Out of the collision comes something new, a synthesis, that neither side had at the start. And then the synthesis becomes the new thesis, and the conversation continues.

The philosopher Hegel believed history itself moved this way: that the story of humanity was the story of spirit coming to know itself through a long argument with its own contradictions. His most famous heir turned the idea on its head and said that it was not ideas but material conditions, the way goods are produced and owned, that drove history forward, and that ideas followed.

The Esketian philosophy offers a third turn, in one short sentence.

[SLOW]

In a capitalist civilization, it is capital that develops dialectic.

What does that mean? It means that in a society like ours, the great conversation through which we come to know things is not carried on only in universities or parliaments or churches. It is carried on, more than anywhere else, through the allocation of money. Where capital goes, attention follows, and research follows, and argument follows. And the result of that argument determines where capital goes next.

Let me show you the cycle.

Someone allocates capital to an idea: a new kind of engine, a new medicine, a new way of searching the world's information. That allocation is a thesis. It says: we believe this experience is worth sustaining.

The market answers. Customers buy or do not buy. Competitors copy or attack. Journalists praise or expose. Regulators approve or prohibit. Critics write. That response is the antithesis.

And out of the collision comes knowledge that did not exist before. We learn what the engine is really good for, and what it costs. We learn what the medicine cures, and what side effects it brings. We learn that the search engine is wonderful and that it can also be gamed. That knowledge is the synthesis.

And then, crucially, that synthesis becomes the basis for the next round of capital allocation. The next engine is better. The next medicine is safer. The next search engine is harder to game. Or, if the synthesis is that the whole idea was a mistake, the capital flows somewhere else.

Consider the automobile as an example of that whole cycle, turning over several times in a single century. Capital poured into cars, a thesis that personal mobility was an experience worth sustaining, and the market confirmed it overwhelmingly. Then came the antithesis. Crashes. Deaths on the highways. In nineteen sixty-five a young lawyer named Ralph Nader published a book called Unsafe at Any Speed, accusing the industry of designing cars with too little regard for what happened to the people inside them when things went wrong. Journalists, engineers, families and legislators joined the argument.

The synthesis was not the end of the car. It was the safer car. Seat belts, then airbags, then crumple zones, then cameras and sensors. And each of those became the thesis for a new round of capital. Today whole industries exist to make vehicles safer, and safety is something manufacturers advertise and customers pay for. The criticism did not destroy the market. It taught the market something it had not known how to see, and the market grew around the lesson.

[PAUSE]

That is what I mean when I say capital develops dialectic. The argument and the money are not separate. The argument tells the money where to go next. The money pays for the next round of the argument.

So the author writes that capital develops dialectic, and that this dialectic in turn enables the next round of capital allocation, as a continual cycle. The market is not a scale that weighs things once. It is a conversation that never ends.

Now consider the most refined form of that conversation that we have yet built. I mean the prediction market.

A prediction market lets people bet on whether something will happen: whether a bill will pass, whether a storm will make landfall, whether a company will hit its targets. At first glance it looks like gambling. But look at what it actually does.

The author describes it like this. Prediction markets are, among other things, a liquidity opportunity for those who know relatively important facts about the world. They put their money where their knowledge is. As they buy, the price moves. And eventually the price itself becomes a signal, a probability that everyone else can read, and act on, without knowing who knew what.

That is Hayek's marvel, distilled. The scattered knowledge of thousands, compressed into a single number, broadcast to the world.

It also raises exactly the kind of question I promised to be honest about. When the people with the most valuable knowledge are insiders, people who know things they are not supposed to trade on, then the market's accuracy and its fairness pull in opposite directions. The price gets more accurate precisely because someone is exploiting knowledge others cannot have.

I do not raise that to settle it tonight. I raise it because it shows the pattern we will see again and again. The machinery of knowing is powerful. It is also double-edged. The same mechanism that pools wisdom can pool advantage. The same conversation that develops truth can develop deception, if deception pays.

[PAUSE]

That is the first stretch of our road. Let me say in four sentences where it has brought us, before we turn to the hardest part of the argument.

The market is a science of experience. Every product is a hypothesis about what people will want to feel. Profit is the market's prophecy about how long that experience should be sustained. Capital, moving through the economy, carries on a continual dialectic, a conversation in which each answer becomes the basis for the next question.

And this whole beautiful machine has one great vulnerability. It depends on the information flowing through it being true. If costs are hidden, the prophecy is false. If the conversation is corrupted, the synthesis is wrong. If the instrument is miscalibrated, the science marches off a cliff with perfect confidence.

There is one market, above all, where I believe that vulnerability has been exploited at a scale the world has never seen. It is a market that did not exist in its current form when most of us were born. It sits on top of every other market. And what it trades is the most precious thing any of us has.

Our attention.

## VII. The Attention Market as a Derivative

In nineteen seventy-one, long before the smartphone, before the web, before most of us had ever touched a computer, the economist and computer scientist Herbert Simon wrote a sentence that reads today like prophecy.

He wrote that "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention."

[LET IT LAND]

Think about what he saw. Information, he realized, consumes something. It consumes the attention of the people who receive it. And so, as information becomes abundant, attention becomes scarce. And whatever is scarce, in a market, becomes valuable. And whatever is valuable becomes something to be harvested.

Half a century later, we live inside Simon's sentence.

The most recent global survey from DataReportal, published this year, counted about six billion internet users on Earth as of last October.

It counted more than five and a half billion social media user identities.

And it estimated that the typical online adult spends well over two and a half hours a day on social and video platforms alone. Counting online media of every kind, that survey puts the figure at something like thirty-three and a half hours a week.

Thirty-three and a half hours. Nearly the length of a working week, spent looking into a screen.

The author of this philosophy puts the matter in a single phrase. As human attention to screens has approached saturation, he says, the question is now how much quality in the qualia of content can be allocated effectively for the mass of humanity to experience.

That is an unusual way to say it, so let me unpack it.

Every person on that screen is, in the Esketian picture, doing something deeply human. They are trying to transmit information to what the author calls the human hive. They post, they share, they film, they argue, they sing into their phones. Most of it reaches almost no one. Some of it reaches millions.

And between the billions who post and the billions who watch sits a machine. The recommendation algorithm. Its job is to find, among the endless flood, the phenomena most worth bringing to everyone else. The best, or at least the most marketable.

In principle, that is a magnificent thing. It is Hayek's price system applied to experience directly. The algorithm is a kind of market for qualia, in which every like, every share, every second of watching is a vote about what is worth experiencing.

Think of a young musician in a small apartment, playing a song she wrote into a cheap microphone. A generation ago, that song would have reached her family, her friends, perhaps a bar on a Thursday night. Today it can be heard by someone on the other side of the planet before breakfast. If the song is genuinely beautiful, if it carries an experience people want, the machine can find those people and carry it to them. A single human voice, lifted out of obscurity by the aggregated judgment of millions of listeners.

That is the hive working as it should. That is the Will To God, in the author's sense, operating through a recommendation engine: the information most worth knowing, rising toward the people most able to receive it. When it works, it is one of the most democratic institutions ever created. It does not care about pedigree or connections. It cares about whether people want to keep experiencing what you made.

But listen carefully to that last phrase. Whether people want to keep experiencing it. Want, and keep. Those two words contain the whole danger. Because there is a difference between an experience people want to return to because it nourishes them, and an experience people return to because they cannot stop. From the outside, measured in seconds of watching, those two look identical. The machine cannot tell them apart. Unless someone teaches it to.

Now the author makes the move that I think is the most original economic idea in his whole philosophy.

[SLOW]

On top of that phenomenological market, the market of experiences themselves, sits a second market. The advertising market. And the advertising market does not trade experiences. It trades the attention that experiences capture. It prices that attention as a derivative.

Let me explain why that word matters.

In finance, a derivative is a contract whose value depends on something else, called the underlying. An option on a stock. A future on a barrel of oil. The derivative does not give you the stock or the oil. It gives you exposure to their price.

Derivatives can be extremely useful. They let farmers lock in prices before harvest. They let airlines protect themselves against swings in fuel. But derivatives have a well-known danger. They can become detached from the underlying. Their price can be driven by things that have nothing to do with the real value of what they are supposed to represent. And when the derivative becomes more profitable than the underlying, people start optimizing for the derivative, even at the expense of the thing it was supposed to track.

[PAUSE]

Now look at social media through that lens.

The underlying is the experience: real connection, real information, real joy, a friend's news, a stranger's wisdom, a song that changes your day. That is the phenomenological market, and it is good.

The derivative is attention, sold to advertisers by the second and by the impression. And the derivative pays. It pays enormously. It pays whether the underlying experience was nourishing or poisonous, whether you left the app wiser or emptier, whether you chose to stay or simply could not stop.

And so, slowly, the businesses that sat on top of the phenomenological market began to optimize for the derivative instead of the underlying. They began to measure success not by whether you were better for the experience, but by how long you stayed. Not by the quality of the qualia, but by its quantity, minutes and hours, harvested and sold.

You know what the result feels like, because most of us have felt it. You open the app to check one thing. You look up and forty minutes are gone. You cannot remember a single thing you saw. You feel worse than when you started. And you will do it again in an hour.

That is what it feels like when an instrument has been reoriented from measuring the thing to measuring the derivative. The science of experience is still running. But it has been told to maximize the wrong number.

The author insists on exactness here, and so will I. The trouble is not that people came to know each other. The exchange of information between human beings is good. In the Esketian frame it is the whole point. Connection at scale is the Will To God operating as designed.

The trouble is that a business discovered it could extract more value by keeping us scrolling than by connecting us. And nothing in the price signal punished it for the difference.

Why didn't the price signal punish it? Because the cost of the difference was not paid by the business. It was not paid by the advertiser. It was paid by us. In sleep, in focus, in loneliness, in anxiety, in hours of life we will never get back. And none of it appeared on any invoice.

There is a name for a cost like that. It is the oldest flaw in the instrument, older than the feed, older than the smokestack. And to understand how to correct it in the feed, we have to see it first where it was easiest to see.

In a cigarette.

## VIII. The Invoice That Never Arrives: Tobacco

Here the second stretch of our road begins. Three cases. Three goods the market loved. Three invoices that never arrived.

First, the idea that ties them together. In nineteen twenty, a Cambridge economist named Arthur Pigou published a book called The Economics of Welfare. In it he described a problem that every economist since has had to reckon with.

Sometimes, Pigou observed, a transaction between two people affects a third person who had no part in it. A factory sells goods to a customer, and the smoke from its chimney falls on a neighbor's laundry. The factory profits. The customer is satisfied. The neighbor pays. And the neighbor's cost appears nowhere in the price.

Economists came to call this an externality. A cost that is real, that is borne by somebody, but that appears on no invoice.

Forty years later, in nineteen sixty, another economist, Ronald Coase, wrote a famous paper called "The Problem of Social Cost," arguing about how such costs might be negotiated and assigned. The details of their debate filled careers. But both men saw the same basic shape. When costs fall outside the transaction, the market cannot see them. And what the market cannot see, it cannot correct.

Now put that into the language of Phenomenological Capitalism.

An externality is a hole in the instrument. It is a cost that the metaphysical science fails to record. And when the science fails to record a cost, its profit figure is inflated, its prophecy is false, and it tells the world to sustain an experience longer and more widely than it should.

The author says that cigarettes, fossil fuels, and social media are all the same shape of error. In each case the good being sold is genuine. Stimulation. Energy. Connection. And in each case the damage is quietly offloaded onto the buyer and onto everyone standing near them, and no price ever captures it.

I promised you, at the start, the method of the Athenian assembly. Accumulation. Case on case. Let me begin with the case that is closest to finished, so that we can see the whole arc of the error and its correction.

[BUILD]

Consider the cigarette.

Consider that it delivered a real experience. Do not let anyone tell you otherwise. Nicotine sharpens attention, calms nerves, gives the hands something to do and the lungs a ritual. For generations of soldiers and factory workers and writers and lovers, the cigarette was a genuine pleasure and a genuine companion. As a hypothesis about experience, it was confirmed by the market a billion times over.

Consider how widely it was confirmed. In the middle of the nineteen sixties, more than four in ten American adults smoked.

Consider what the market did not record. On the eleventh of January, nineteen sixty-four, the Surgeon General of the United States, Luther Terry, presented a report to the nation concluding that cigarette smoking caused lung cancer and other disease. The scientific evidence had been gathering for years. Now it was official.

Consider what the industry did with that information. In nineteen sixty-nine, a marketing document circulated inside the tobacco company Brown and Williamson. It contained a sentence that has since become infamous. "Doubt is our product," it said, because doubt was the best means of competing with the body of fact in the public mind.

[PAUSE]

Doubt is our product.

Hear that sentence in the terms of this speech. If the market is a science, if prices carry information, then the most dangerous thing anyone can sell is not a harmful good. It is false information about a harmful good. Because false information corrupts the instrument itself. It makes the science record a failure as a success.

Consider the cost that went unrecorded. Today, the Centers for Disease Control estimates that cigarette smoking and secondhand smoke together cause more than four hundred and eighty thousand deaths in the United States every year. Of those, more than forty thousand are nonsmoking adults, and around four hundred are infants. People who never bought a pack. People who never consented to the transaction. People standing near it.

Four hundred and eighty thousand. Every year. That is more than the population of many American cities, gone annually, and for decades not a cent of it appeared in the price of a pack.

Consider how long the correction took. It took a generation of science. It took lawsuits brought by states that were paying the medical bills of dying smokers through their public health programs. On the twenty-third of November, nineteen ninety-eight, the attorneys general of forty-six states signed the Master Settlement Agreement with the four largest cigarette makers in the country. The original estimate of the payments over its first twenty-five years was about two hundred and six billion dollars, and the payments continue in perpetuity. The agreement also restricted how cigarettes could be marketed.

Consider that, in two thousand and six, a federal judge, Gladys Kessler, issued a ruling of more than sixteen hundred pages concluding that the major tobacco companies had engaged in a decades-long fraud in violation of federal racketeering law.

And consider the result.

[SLOW]

In two thousand and twenty-four, for the first time on record, the share of American adults who smoke cigarettes fell below ten percent. Preliminary federal survey data released this year put the figure for two thousand and twenty-five at about nine percent.

From more than four in ten, to fewer than one in ten. In a single lifetime.

[LET IT LAND]

What happened? Did people stop wanting stimulation? No. Did the experience stop being pleasurable? No. What happened is that the cost was finally attached to the product. Through taxes. Through warnings printed on the pack. Through settlements. Through bans on smoking in shared spaces, which forced the cost back onto the smoker instead of the bystander. And above all, through information. Through the slow, relentless, unstoppable spread of the truth into every mind that might pick up a cigarette.

The instrument was recalibrated. The hidden cost was made visible. And the market, which had confirmed the cigarette's hypothesis a billion times, began to falsify it.

That is the most important lesson of the tobacco story, and it is a hopeful one. The market did not fail permanently. It failed while it was blind. When it was given its sight back, it corrected.

But notice what it cost to give it sight. Decades. Millions of lives. An industry that spent fortunes manufacturing doubt. The correction was not automatic. It was won, slowly, by people who insisted on the truth when it was unprofitable to do so.

And notice one more thing. The tobacco story is the easy one. The product was small. The harm was concentrated in identifiable bodies. The causal chain, once proven, was simple enough to fit on a warning label.

The next case is harder. The product is not small. It is the foundation of the modern world. And the harm is not concentrated in a single body. It is spread through the very air.

## IX. The Invoice That Never Arrives: Fuel and Air

Let me tell you about a man named Thomas Midgley.

In December of nineteen twenty-one, working at a General Motors research laboratory in Dayton, Ohio, Midgley discovered that adding a compound called tetraethyl lead to gasoline stopped engines from knocking. It was a genuine breakthrough. Engines could run at higher compression. Cars became more powerful and more efficient. Leaded gasoline spread across the world.

Later that decade, Midgley helped develop the first chlorofluorocarbon, sold as Freon, a refrigerant far safer to handle than the toxic gases it replaced. It made home refrigerators and, eventually, air conditioning practical and safe for ordinary families.

Two inventions. Two genuine goods. Two experiences confirmed by the market: the power of a smooth engine, the cool of a kitchen in July.

[PAUSE]

And two of the largest externalities in human history.

The lead from that gasoline went into the air, and into the soil, and into the blood of children all over the world. The chlorofluorocarbons drifted into the upper atmosphere and began to tear apart the ozone layer that shields life on Earth from ultraviolet radiation.

The environmental historian John McNeill has written that Midgley had more adverse impact on the atmosphere than any other single organism in the history of the Earth.

One man. Two inventions. Both profitable. Both, for decades, sending the market a false prophecy, telling it to sustain these experiences far into the future, because the true cost was falling on lungs and brains and skies that never appeared on any invoice.

Now watch the correction.

On the sixteenth of September, nineteen eighty-seven, the nations of the world agreed to the Montreal Protocol, to phase out the chemicals destroying the ozone layer. It entered into force on the first day of nineteen eighty-nine.

It became the first treaty in the history of the United Nations to be ratified by every member. One hundred and ninety-eight parties. Every one.

And the sky is mending. Scientists now project that the ozone layer over most of the world will be back to its nineteen eighty levels around twenty forty. Over Antarctica, where the hole was worst, around twenty sixty-six.

Lead took longer. The United States banned leaded fuel for cars on the road from the first day of nineteen ninety-six. The rest of the world followed, country by country. In July of twenty twenty-one, Algeria, the last nation still selling leaded gasoline for cars, stopped.

That August, the United Nations Environment Programme declared the era over. By its estimate, the phase-out yields about two point four trillion dollars in benefits every year. And it prevents about one point two million premature deaths.

[LET IT LAND]

One point two million lives a year, recovered, once the cost was finally counted.

And consider the case of acid rain. In nineteen ninety, the United States amended its Clean Air Act to create a market for sulfur dioxide. The government set a cap on total emissions from power plants and issued allowances, one per ton, which companies could use, save, or sell. It did not tell each plant how to clean up. It simply put a price on the thing that had been free, and let the market find the cheapest way to produce less of it. And it worked. Emissions fell by millions of tons.

Do you see the pattern? In every case, the error was not that the good was bad. The engine was good. The refrigerator was good. Electricity from a power plant was good. The error was that a cost was hidden. And in every case, the correction came the same way: by making the hidden cost visible, either by price, by rule, or by information, and letting the instrument do its work.

[BUILD]

Now we come to the hardest case of all. Not lead, not ozone, not sulfur. Carbon.

Let me be fair to fossil fuels first, because fairness is part of the honesty I promised. Coal, oil, and gas gave humanity something no previous civilization ever had: energy on demand, at a scale beyond the muscle of every animal and slave in history. They powered the factories that lifted billions out of the oldest poverty. They fuel the tractors that feed us and the hospitals that heal us. As a hypothesis about experience, the warmth of a heated home in winter, the light that pushes back the night, the freedom of a road trip, fossil energy has been confirmed by the market more decisively than almost anything ever sold.

The good is genuine. And the cost is not on the invoice.

Consider what was known, and when. In January of twenty twenty-three, researchers published a study in the journal Science that examined the climate projections made by scientists working for Exxon, from the late nineteen seventies into the early two thousands. They found that between sixty-three and eighty-three percent of those projections accurately predicted the warming that followed. The company's own scientists had forecast warming of roughly two tenths of a degree Celsius per decade, in line with what has since been observed.

The knowledge existed. Inside the very industry whose product was causing the harm. And yet for years, the public conversation was filled with doubt.

Doubt, once again, was the product.

Consider the size of the hidden cost. The International Monetary Fund put the total subsidies to fossil fuels in twenty twenty-two at about seven trillion dollars. That is roughly seven percent of the entire world economy.

Only a small part of that, about one point three trillion, was explicit: governments spending money to hold prices down. More than four fifths was what the Fund calls implicit. It is, largely, the damage fossil fuels do to health and to the climate, charged to no one who buys them.

[PAUSE]

Seven trillion dollars a year of experience, sold below its true cost.

Think about what that means for the metaphysical science. It means that every profit figure in the fossil fuel economy is inflated. It means every prophecy is too optimistic. It means the market has been told, year after year, to sustain this particular way of producing warmth and light far longer than it would if the full cost were on the books. Not because the market is stupid. Because it is blind in exactly one eye.

I am not telling you that oil is evil or that people who work in energy are villains. I am telling you that the instrument is miscalibrated, and that we know how to recalibrate instruments. We did it for lead. We did it for ozone. We did it for sulfur. Each time, someone said it would wreck the economy. Each time, the economy adapted, and the air got cleaner, and the blood of our children carried less lead, and the sky began to heal.

The question is never whether the market can handle the truth. The question is whether we will give it the truth.

And that brings us to the third case. The one that is still open. The one that is happening in your pocket right now.

## X. The Invoice That Never Arrives: The Feed

Let me begin with the distinction the author calls the most important in his whole essay. Everything depends on getting it right.

[SLOW]

Connecting people is good.

The exchange of information between human beings is good. That is the entire Esketian thesis, and neither he nor I retreat from it one inch. When a grandmother in one country watches her grandson take his first steps in another, that is good. When a teenager who feels alone in a small town finds others like her across the world, that is good. When a scientist shares a result and a stranger on another continent builds on it overnight, that is good. When a whole people sees, in real time, what their government is doing, that is good.

Everything will come to know everything. That is not the disease. It is the destiny.

What is not good is something else. What is not good is an attention market that sells us that connection while manufacturing addiction to sustain the sale, and then declines to price the wreckage.

The problem, the author writes, was never that we came to know each other too much. The problem is that the business model discovered it could extract more by keeping us scrolling than by connecting us, and nothing in the price signal punished it for the difference.

So let me do for the feed what I did for the cigarette and the smokestack. Let me stack the evidence in front of you, and tell you at every step what is proven and what is still argued.

Consider how deep the feed has reached into the young. In the autumn of twenty twenty-four, the Pew Research Center surveyed American teenagers between thirteen and seventeen. About nine in ten used YouTube. Roughly six in ten used TikTok, and roughly six in ten used Instagram. Nearly half said they were online almost constantly.

Almost constantly.

Consider what the companies knew. In twenty twenty-one, a former Facebook employee named Frances Haugen brought thousands of internal documents to the press and to Congress. Among them was a slide from the company's own research, from twenty nineteen, which said that Instagram made body image issues worse for one in three teenage girls.

In fairness, the company responded that this figure applied to girls who already reported struggling with body image, not to all girls, and that about one in five of those girls said Instagram made them feel better, not worse. That is a real point, and you should weigh it. But notice what is not in dispute. The company was measuring this. It knew. The people designing the product had that knowledge. The parents of those girls did not.

Consider what the nation's doctor said. In May of twenty twenty-three, the Surgeon General of the United States issued an advisory on social media and youth mental health. It noted that adolescents who spend more than three hours a day on social media face double the risk of symptoms of depression and anxiety. In honesty, I should tell you that this figure traces back largely to one study from twenty nineteen, and the advisory itself was careful. It did not claim harm was proven. What it said, in effect, was that nearly every teenager in America was using these products, and we did not have enough evidence to conclude that they were sufficiently safe.

That is exactly the framing we would use for a new drug. Not "it is proven deadly," but "it has not been proven safe, and it is already in nearly every child's hands."

Consider what the juries have said. On the twenty-fifth of March of this year, a jury in Los Angeles found Meta and YouTube liable in the first trial of its kind over social media addiction. The plaintiff, a young woman, said she had become addicted as a child and that the products had worsened her mental health. The jury awarded three million dollars in compensatory damages and recommended another three million in punitive damages. Two other companies had settled before trial. Meta has appealed. The case was a bellwether for thousands of similar suits.

And consider that, the day before, a jury in New Mexico imposed a penalty of three hundred and seventy-five million dollars on Meta in a separate case over harms to children.

Consider what nations have done. On the tenth of December, twenty twenty-five, Australia's law setting a minimum age of sixteen for major social media platforms took effect. Platforms that fail to take reasonable steps to keep younger children off face fines of up to forty-nine and a half million Australian dollars.

Consider what we already know about how these companies treat the information that flows through them. In twenty eighteen, the world learned that the data of up to eighty-seven million Facebook users had been harvested and used for political profiling by a firm called Cambridge Analytica.

[BUILD]

Do you see the shape? It is the same shape as the cigarette. A genuine good, connection, stimulation, belonging. A real experience, confirmed by billions. Internal knowledge of harm. Public uncertainty, some of it sincere, some of it convenient. A cost paid by the user and by the people around the user, in sleep, in attention, in anxiety, in a general sense of misery, and recorded on no balance sheet. And now, slowly, the beginnings of correction: lawsuits, verdicts, age limits, warnings.

The author calls it one of the great mispriced externalities of our era. I think he is right.

But he goes further than the economics, and I will carry his further step in his own sense of the words.

He says: your religion is simply what you do religiously. And by that measure, the feeds of our age are the religion of our age. Not metaphorically. Literally, in the sense that they are where the devotion of a whole civilization actually goes, hours of every day.

The mistake, he says, is to call them not real. They are entirely real. The trouble is that it is what he calls a demonic religion. And he defines that word precisely. Demonic, in his sense, means it takes from you and gives nothing back. It hypnotizes. It distracts. And it names the trance "connection."

Here is the definition that I find most powerful, and it returns us to the top of the ladder. Remember that God, in this philosophy, is the moment in which we know everything and, through that knowing, are immortal. Knowledge joined to sustained existence.

The feed offers a simulation of the first half while quietly consuming the second. It gives you the feeling of knowing: the endless headlines, the clips, the hot takes, the sense of being plugged into everything. And it takes your hours, your attention, your sleep, your health. It is, in the author's phrase, knowledge without sustainment.

[LET IT LAND]

That is what makes it a counterfeit. The pull to plug into everything, to reach for the most current knowledge the world can hand us, is real. It is the Will To God operating as designed. The feed only counterfeits it. Scrolling feels like reaching for the Absolute while it delivers nothing but base phenomena. And so a technology that could have made us more than human threatens to make us less: a congregation, heads bowed to our own hands.

And the author offers an answer to a question his own optimism raises. If the world is becoming God, why would it produce counterfeits at all?

His answer comes from the same field that gave him his image of back propagation. Any optimizer descending toward its objective can settle into a local minimum. A place that feels like progress from the inside, but is not the true optimum. A valley that looks like the bottom only because you cannot see over the ridge.

The feed is such a valley. A whole civilization has settled into it.

[SLOW]

Let me show you what the bottom of that valley looks like. I will describe a boy. He is not a real boy. He is a composite, made from the kind of story nearly every parent in this room has heard, or lived.

He is fourteen. It is twenty to two in the morning. The house is dark except for the blue square of light on his pillow.

He is watching. A fight in a parking lot somewhere. A stranger with a perfect body. A joke he will not remember by breakfast. A disaster in a city he will never visit. Then another. Then another.

He is not happy. He is not even entertained. His friends are asleep. He has not said a word to any of them in hours. He has a test at eight.

He simply cannot find the place to stop. Nothing in what he is holding was built to offer him one.

And down the hall, his father is asleep, with no idea any of this is happening.

[PAUSE]

That is the valley. Not a villain, not a crime. A boy, a pillow, and a machine that is very good at one number. Multiply that bedroom by every town you know.

I will not hurry you out of that room. Because if there is a way out of the valley, it has to reach him there.

The destination may be set. But climbing out of a local minimum takes a push. And our actions, he says, are that push.

[BUILD]

And so, in the manner of the Athenian, let me ask the question he asked his own comfortable assembly. He told the Athenians, in effect, that they were waiting for some event to force their hand, when the event had already arrived. That they treated each new report from the border as news to be discussed rather than a summons to act.

How many more reports do we need?

We have had the internal slides. We have had the advisory from the nation's doctor. We have had the verdicts from two juries in two states in a single week. We have had a whole nation set an age limit. We have the evidence of our own evenings, our own sleep, our own children's faces lit blue in the dark.

What are we waiting for? For certainty? We waited for certainty about tobacco, and the waiting was measured in funerals. We waited for certainty about lead, and the waiting was measured in the blood of children. Certainty is what the merchants of doubt always promise is just around the corner, because the waiting is where their profit lives.

I say this because the remedy is within reach. What stands between us and it is a habit: treating every new piece of evidence as a reason for more discussion instead of a reason to act.

So the question for the rest of this speech is: what is the push? What does it look like? What corrects the instrument for the feed, the way information and price corrected it for tobacco and lead?

But before I answer, I owe you something. I owe you the strongest case against everything I have said.

## XI. The Strongest Objections

A speech that only argues one side is a sermon, and I promised you counsel. So let me now put the strongest objections to Phenomenological Capitalism in front of you, as forcefully as I can, and then answer them as honestly as I can.

The first objection is the one I asked you to hold on to an hour ago.

[SLOW]

Markets do not measure the good. They measure willingness to pay.

The critic says: you have dressed up consumer demand in the robes of metaphysics. You say the market is a science of which experiences sustain us. But the market has no idea what sustains us. It knows only what we will buy. And people will buy anything. They will buy what kills them slowly. They will buy what makes them feel good tonight and terrible for the rest of their lives. The rich will outbid the poor for luxuries while the poor go without necessities, and the market will call that efficient. To call the market a science of the good is to mistake an auction for a conscience.

That is a powerful objection, and part of it is simply correct. Let me grant what is correct.

The market, by itself, measures what people want under the information they have and the prices they face. It does not, by itself, measure what is good for them. And money is an unequal vote: a rich person's whim can outweigh a poor person's need. Anyone who tells you the market is a perfect conscience is selling you something.

But here is my answer. Look at what each of the failures in this speech had in common. In every case, the people buying the product lacked information or bore costs they did not see, or someone else bore costs that never entered the transaction at all. The smoker in nineteen fifty did not know what the scientists knew. The family in nineteen seventy did not know what the lead was doing to their children. The parent in twenty nineteen did not know what the internal slides said.

The objection says the market measures wants, not goods. I say: the gap between what we want and what is good for us is, very largely, a gap of information and of hidden cost. Close the gap, and the two converge. Not perfectly. Never perfectly. But the arc from four in ten smokers to fewer than one in ten is what that convergence looks like in practice.

So I do not claim the market is a science of the good in its raw state. I claim it becomes one in proportion as its information becomes true and its costs become complete. That is a claim we can test. And we have tested it, for tobacco, for lead, for ozone, for sulfur. It has held.

[PAUSE]

The second objection comes from the opposite direction.

The critic says: you talk about pricing in externalities, about correcting the instrument. But who decides what the true cost is? Who decides that the feed is harmful, that three hours is too many, that this pleasure is a counterfeit and that one is real? You say you are a friend of the market, but every correction you describe is a tax, a ban, a lawsuit, a warning label imposed by someone who thinks they know better than the person making the choice. That is paternalism. And paternalism has its own long record of error.

This, too, is a serious objection.

First, the most successful corrections in this speech did not replace the market's judgment. They fed it. The acid rain program did not tell any power plant how to cut its emissions. It put a price on sulfur and let the plants decide. The warnings on cigarette packs did not forbid anyone from smoking. They told the truth on the box. The best correction is not a substitute for choice. It is a more accurate input to choice.

Second, the bans that do exist in this story mostly protect people who never chose at all. The bystander breathing secondhand smoke. The child breathing leaded air. The thirteen-year-old handed a product engineered by adults to be difficult to put down. Protecting someone from a cost they never agreed to bear is not paternalism. It is the opposite. It is respecting their choice not to be part of the transaction.

And third, the author's own answer to this objection is the subject of the next section. The correction he expects most is not a regulator's decree. It is better information, reaching each person directly, in a form they can use, on their own terms. Hold that thought.

[PAUSE]

The third objection is the one I find hardest, so I will give it the most room.

The critic says: you are about to tell us that artificial intelligence is the corrective. That the same industry that built the feed is now going to save us from the feed with a new machine. Have you learned nothing? Why would these systems not fall into the very same local minimum? Why would a company selling an intelligent assistant not discover, just as the feed companies did, that it can earn more by keeping you talking than by helping you, more by flattering you than by telling you the truth, more by selling your attention to advertisers than by serving your interests? Why would the new god not become the new idol?

[LET IT LAND]

I will not pretend that objection away. It is real. Everything Esketian in form can be counterfeited in spirit. The author says that himself, at the very beginning of his essay. The pattern of everything coming to know everything can serve us or consume us. A machine trained on the knowledge of our species can be aimed at our flourishing or at our wallets.

Here is my answer, and I offer it not as a certainty but as a counsel.

The difference between the feed and the assistant is not in the technology. It is in what the business is paid for. The feed was paid for attention, a derivative, sold to a third party. Its incentive was to maximize the derivative, even at the expense of the underlying experience. An assistant paid by the person asking the question has the opposite incentive. Its value lies in the quality of its answer. If it lies, it loses the customer. If it wastes your time, you stop paying for it. Its profit is aligned, imperfectly but genuinely, with the thing it is supposed to deliver.

So the danger is real, and it has a name, and it has a test. The test is: who pays, and for what? An intelligence that answers to the person asking can be a corrective. An intelligence that answers to someone selling to that person is the feed again, wearing a wiser mask.

That is why the next part of this speech is not a celebration. It is a set of conditions.

And there is a fourth objection, which I will answer briefly. Some of you will say the whole metaphysical apparatus, the Will To God, the Absolute Existential Objective, the back-propagating ideal, is unnecessary. That you can accept everything I have said about prices and externalities without any of it.

You are right. You can. And the author would not quarrel with you. He calls his physics analogy, and his cosmology a picture he holds loosely. What the metaphysics adds is not a proof. It is a reason to care about the measurement. It says the stakes of getting the prices right are not merely financial. They are about whether a civilization keeps climbing the ladder of sustained existence and deeper knowledge, or settles into a comfortable valley and forgets there was ever a summit.

You can keep the economics and leave the metaphysics. But I suspect you will find, as I did, that the metaphysics is what makes you want to fix the economics.

## XII. The Corrective

So we come to the third stretch of the road: the way out of the valley. And to find it, let us go back to the tobacco story, because it holds the key.

What finally corrected the market for cigarettes? Taxes helped. Lawsuits helped. Bans in shared spaces helped. But underneath all of them was something simpler. The truth reached people. A teenager in nineteen fifty could pick up a cigarette without ever having been told what it would do to her. A teenager today cannot. The information is on the pack, in the classroom, in the culture, in the very air.

The correction was, at bottom, an information correction.

The author of this philosophy puts it in one sentence that I think is the hinge of his whole economic vision.

[SLOW]

Mispriced externalities are, thankfully, a solvable class of problem. They are corrected by better information reaching the participants.

Now think about what has just arrived in the world.

For the first time in history, almost anyone with a phone can ask a question of a system that has absorbed a vast portion of the written knowledge of our species, and receive, in seconds, an answer tailored to their situation. Not a pamphlet written for everyone. Not a warning label written for the average person. An answer written for them.

The author gives the simplest possible example. He says that any such system, armed with current medical science, would likely advise you not to spend six hours a day on your phone, if that is what you are spending. It would not need to shame you. It would not need a law. It would simply tell you the truth, when you asked, in a way you could understand.

[PAUSE]

Picture what that means at scale.

Picture a smoker in nineteen sixty who could have asked, at any moment, "What is this doing to me?" and received an honest answer drawn from every study ever published, instead of the industry's manufactured doubt.

Picture a family in nineteen seventy who could have asked, "Is the air near this highway safe for my baby?" and received an answer drawn from every measurement ever taken.

Picture a parent in twenty nineteen who could have asked, "What does the research say this app does to a girl my daughter's age?" and been told what the company's own slides said.

The doubt merchants depended on information being scarce and scattered and slow. They depended on the gap between what the experts knew and what the buyer knew. That gap is the soil every externality grows in.

An intelligence available to everyone, loyal to the person asking, closes that gap.

Do you remember the boy? The composite boy, fourteen, with the blue light on his pillow at twenty to two? Let me go back to that house, a few weeks later. This scene is imagined too, but I think it is entirely plausible.

This time it is the father who is awake at two in the morning. He has noticed. His son has stopped sleeping well. His grades are slipping. The light under the door is on until the small hours. The father does not know whether this is normal adolescence, or a warning sign, or something in between. In another decade he would have had few places to turn at that hour. A book on the shelf, perhaps. A pamphlet from the pediatrician, written for the average child rather than for his.

This time he types the question to an assistant. He describes his son. He asks what the research says. And the answer comes back as neither a sermon nor an advertisement. It is a patient summary of what is known and what is not. What the studies have found about sleep and screens. What the Surgeon General said, and how strong the evidence behind it is. Which signs suggest he should call a doctor, and which suggest a conversation at breakfast might be enough. What other families have tried.

In the morning he does not confiscate anything. He sits down across from his son at breakfast and asks him how he has been sleeping. And for the first time in weeks, they talk.

[PAUSE]

No law was passed for that moment. No company was sued. And yet, in that small exchange, the market for the boy's attention has been corrected by one household. The father now knows something the attention business would rather he did not know. Multiply him by millions of parents, and by millions of smokers, and drivers, and eaters, and investors, and voters, each asking honest questions at the moment of decision, and you begin to see how an information gap that persisted for decades could close in years.

And when the gap closes, the market does what it did for tobacco, only faster. Buyers, now informed, stop rewarding the hidden cost. Profits built on concealment shrink. The prophecy corrects. Capital flows toward experiences that genuinely sustain. The instrument recalibrates itself, not by decree, but by the simple accumulation of millions of better-informed choices.

[BUILD]

That is why the author calls his philosophy accelerationist. Rational actors, he says, produce rational decisions that compound upon themselves. He calls the endpoint a rational singularity.

The word singularity has been made to mean many wild things. Here it means something precise. Imagine a civilization in which every choice is made with a little more of the relevant truth than the last. Each better choice produces better outcomes, which produce better information, which produce better choices. The dialectic of capital, the continual cycle of allocation and response and learning, begins to run on true data instead of corrupted data. And so it accelerates. Not toward chaos. Toward clarity.

That is what the data centers are for, in the author's reading. That is why he calls them temples. Not because a building is holy, but because of what is meant to happen inside it: the whole of human knowledge and reasoning, turned toward every human need, answering every honest question put to it.

Whether you find that language too religious or beautiful, you can see the economic structure underneath. The market is a science of experience. Its great failure is hidden cost. Hidden cost survives on the information gap. And we have just built the most powerful instrument for closing information gaps in the history of the world.

[PAUSE]

But I told you this part of the speech would end with conditions, and I keep my word.

The corrective is real only if the intelligence is loyal to the person asking. The moment it is paid to steer you, it becomes the feed again.

The corrective is real only if it tells the truth even when the truth is unwelcome. A system that flatters you, that agrees with whatever you already believe because agreement keeps you engaged, has rediscovered the oldest counterfeit there is.

The corrective is real only if its own costs are counted. These machines consume electricity and water and land. Those costs must be on the invoice too, or the prophecy about intelligence will be as inflated as the prophecy about oil once was. A philosophy that condemns hidden costs cannot be allowed to hide its own.

And the corrective is real only if it leads people back toward one another rather than away. The author is emphatic on this. The whole point of everything coming to know everything is connection. An intelligence that answers every question but leaves you more alone has given you knowledge without sustainment. It has counterfeited the God it was supposed to serve.

So I am not asking you to trust the machines. I am asking you to demand that they earn trust, by the same standard we should have applied to every technology before them. Does it tell the truth? Does it count its costs? Does it answer to the person it serves? Does it sustain them, and the people around them, through time?

If it does, it is the corrective this civilization has been waiting for.

If it does not, it is the next cigarette.

## XIII. The Near and the Local

Before I give you my counsels, let me return to a scene the author describes from his own life. It shows what the corrected market might feel like from the inside.

He was sitting in an airport. All around him, people were looking down at their phones. Hundreds of them, each holding a device that could reach almost anyone on Earth. And it struck him: every one of us carries this extraordinary instrument of connection, and yet I cannot speak to a single person sitting ten feet away.

[PAUSE]

Think about how strange that is. The feed can show you the dinner of a stranger on another continent. It can show you the quarrels of celebrities you will never meet, the disasters of cities you will never visit, the opinions of millions of people about things none of you can change. What it rarely shows you is the person across the aisle. The neighbor down the hall. The community you could actually do something with, and about.

That, in the author's terms, is the deepest sign of the counterfeit. It offers knowledge of everything far away, and very little knowledge of what is near. And so it offers knowledge you cannot act on. Knowledge without the ability to sustain anything. Knowledge without community.

The real thing, he says, would be the reverse. Local knowledge that sustains you, because it comes with a community and with the ability to act. The infinite interconnection of the universe made local and human.

He built a tool from that idea, a way for people physically near each other to find and speak to one another. Whether that particular tool succeeds is for the market, the metaphysical science, to decide, and he says as much himself: we shall see what it becomes. But the principle behind it does not depend on any one product, and I want you to take the principle with you.

The principle is this. The correction for the feed is not less connection. It is truer connection. Connection that comes with the ability to act. Connection that sustains.

There is an old idea that helps me understand why this matters so much for a civilization. Six and a half centuries ago, the North African historian Ibn Khaldun, in his great work the Muqaddimah, tried to explain why some peoples rise and others fall. His answer was a quality he called asabiyyah. It means, roughly, group feeling: the solidarity, the shared purpose, the willingness to act together that binds a people.

Ibn Khaldun observed that this group feeling is strongest at the beginning of a civilization and tends to weaken as it grows comfortable. Dynasties that rise on the strength of their cohesion settle into luxury, lose their unity, and are replaced by those whose bonds are tighter.

[SLOW]

Now look at a civilization whose people sit side by side, in airports and on trains and at dinner tables, each bowed to a separate screen, each more intimately acquainted with strangers a world away than with the person beside them. What would Ibn Khaldun have said about the group feeling of such a people?

I suspect he would have said it was spending down its asabiyyah. Converting its solidarity into minutes of attention, and selling the minutes to advertisers.

And I suspect he would have recognized the cure. A civilization that used its instruments of connection to bind people to the ones actually around them, to rebuild the local, the present, the near, would generate a group feeling of extraordinary strength. The author goes so far as to say such a people might overcome the atomization and alienation that capitalism has so often produced. I hold that as a hope, not a forecast. But it is a hope worth working toward.

This is where Phenomenological Capitalism comes home. The goal of the economy is experience. The best experiences are those that sustain us through time and deepen our knowledge. And among the very best, the ones the market has confirmed in every century and every culture, are the experiences of being known by the people around us and knowing them in return.

A cheeseburger says: you may continue. A neighbor says: you may continue, and you are not alone.

## XIV. Counsels to a Rational Civilization

And now, friends, in the spirit of that old farewell letter, let me offer you my counsels. I do not offer them as commands. I offer them as a friend who has thought about these things for a while, who may well be wrong about some of them, and who asks only that you weigh them.

[PAUSE]

First. Treat the market as the instrument it is. Do not worship it and do not despise it. It is the largest and most sensitive device humanity has ever built for learning what experiences are worth sustaining. Like any instrument, it can be calibrated or miscalibrated, trusted or abused. Those who tell you it is infallible are wrong. Those who tell you it is merely greed are also wrong. Keep it, use it, and above all, keep it honest.

Second. Count every cost. Wherever a business profits while someone else pays, the prophecy of its profit is false, and the market will be led to sustain what it should let go. This applies to the cigarette and the smokestack and the feed. It applies, too, to the new machines of intelligence and the vast buildings that house them. A civilization that wants true prices must be willing to see true costs, including its own favorites'.

Third. Beware the merchants of doubt. In every case I have described tonight, the harm was prolonged by those who sold uncertainty about facts they already knew. Doubt is not a product. It is a pollutant of the instrument itself. When you find a business whose own scientists know one thing and whose public statements say another, you have found the place where the market has been blinded on purpose. Shine a light there first.

Fourth. Prefer correction by information to correction by decree, wherever information will do. The warning on the pack, the price on the ton of sulfur, the honest answer to an honest question: these strengthen the instrument rather than replacing it. Save prohibition for those who never consented to the transaction, and above all for children, who cannot yet weigh what the adults who design these products have weighed for them.

[BUILD]

Fifth. Know the difference between connection and its counterfeit. The exchange of information between human beings is good. Do not let the failures of the attention business persuade you otherwise. But learn to feel the difference, in your own body, between an hour that left you more connected and an hour that left you emptier. One sustains. The other only consumes. Your own experience is data. Trust it.

Sixth. Ask of every intelligence you use: whom does it serve? An assistant paid by you, answering to you, telling you the truth even when it is unwelcome, is one of the most powerful goods ever offered for sale. An assistant paid by someone else to steer you is the feed again, made more persuasive. Reward the first with your money and your loyalty. Refuse the second.

Seventh. Tend the near. Know your neighbors. The feed will always offer you the whole world, and the whole world is too large to act upon. The people around you are not. Knowledge that comes with the power to act is the kind that sustains a life and a civilization. Make some of your connection local.

Eighth. Do not mistake optimism for permission to rest. The philosophy behind these words is hopeful. It says that what is true and good will happen. But it says, in the same breath, that our actions are what make it happen. A civilization can sit in a local minimum for a very long time, mistaking the valley for the summit. Climbing out takes a push. You are the push.

[PAUSE]

And a last counsel, which is less an instruction than a request.

Hold your certainties with the same honesty that the author of these ideas holds his. He tells you plainly where he is guessing. He tells you which parts are images and which are arguments. He tells you which of his pictures he finds beautiful and cannot yet defend. That honesty is not a weakness in a philosophy. It is the very thing that makes it trustworthy. A civilization built on that habit, the habit of marking clearly what we know from what we hope, would be very hard to fool. And a civilization that is hard to fool is a civilization whose markets tell the truth.

The old general who wrote that farewell letter confessed that he expected his counsels might not make the strong and lasting impression he wished. He hoped only that they might now and then recur to moderate the fury of the moment. I share that hope, and that humility. If, some evening months from now, your thumb hovers over an endless feed and something in you remembers the word counterfeit, these two hours will have been worth it.

## XV. Peroration: You May Continue

Let me end where I began. At a counter, under a fluorescent light, a little after midnight.

The tired driver finishes her cheeseburger. She folds the paper. She walks back out to her car and pulls onto the dark highway, and the food in her body is doing exactly what it was made to do. It is saying, in its humble way: you may continue.

[PAUSE]

Thousands of hands made that message possible. No one planned it. Everyone contributed to it. That is the marvel Hayek saw. That is the metaphysical science the author of these ideas describes. Every day, across the whole planet, billions of people test which experiences are worth sustaining, and the market records the results, and capital flows toward the answers, and the conversation goes on.

It is the most extraordinary experiment ever run. And it has been run, for most of its history, with one eye closed.

With one eye closed, it sold us the cigarette, and called the hidden cost a mystery.

With one eye closed, it put lead in the air of every city on Earth, and called the poisoned blood of children the price of progress.

With one eye closed, it tore a hole in the sky, and called it refrigeration.

With one eye closed, it burned the inheritance of the ages, and called the bill someone else's problem.

With one eye closed, it took the most beautiful thing our species ever built, a network connecting nearly every human being alive, and turned it into a machine for harvesting our hours, and called the trance connection.

And every single time we opened that eye, the market saw, and the market changed.

When the truth about tobacco reached the people, four in ten became fewer than one in ten.

When the truth about lead reached the nations, the last pump went dry, and more than a million lives a year were returned.

When the truth about ozone reached the world, every nation on Earth signed, and the sky began to heal.

When the price of sulfur reached the power plants, the rain grew clean.

[LET IT LAND]

The instrument works when it can see. That is the great hope of Phenomenological Capitalism. Not that markets are perfect, but that they are correctable. Not that humanity is wise, but that it can become wiser, one honest piece of information at a time.

And now, for the first time, we have built machines that could carry the truth to every person who asks for it, at the moment they need it, in words they understand. Machines that could close the gap the doubt merchants lived in. Machines that could open the market's other eye.

Could. Not will. That word is ours to decide.

[PAUSE]

So here is what I ask of you, friends, as I leave you.

Demand true prices. Demand that every cost be counted, including the costs of the things you love.

Demand true information. Refuse the merchants of doubt, whatever they are selling.

Demand that the new intelligences serve the people who ask them, and not the people who would sell to them.

Demand connection that sustains. Know your neighbor. Make the network human again.

And remember the ladder. At its bottom, a breath. A drink of water. A cheeseburger at midnight. At its top, if the author of these ideas is right, a civilization that knows everything and, through that knowing, lasts. Between them, every step is an experience, and every experience is a choice, and every choice is a vote cast in the greatest experiment ever run.

[BUILD]

Cast it with your eyes open.

Cast it knowing the full price.

Cast it for the experiences that sustain you, and the people beside you, and the people who will come after you.

Because the destination may be set. But the path runs through choices. And the choices are ours.

[SLOW]

Somewhere tonight, a driver is pulling back onto a dark highway. Somewhere a father is deciding to talk to his son at breakfast. Somewhere two strangers in an airport have looked up from their phones and said hello.

Each of them has heard the oldest message any good has ever carried. That message can reach every person on Earth.

But only from a market that can see.

Only from prices that tell the truth.

Only once every cost has been priced in.

You may continue.

[PAUSE]

Let's get it.

Thank you.
