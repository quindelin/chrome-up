# The Last Entry-Level Job

## I. The Text We Agree Upon

Somewhere tonight, a young woman is sitting at a kitchen table with a laptop open in front of her.

I will call her Maya. She is not one person. She is a composite, drawn from the stories young graduates tell about this year. But every detail of her evening is happening, tonight, in kitchens across this country.

She is twenty-three. She has a degree that took four years and a sum of money she does not like to say out loud. She is filling out her one hundred and fortieth application.

The form asks for her name, her school, her major. Then it asks the question that every form asks now. It asks for three to five years of relevant experience.

[PAUSE]

She has none. She is neither lazy nor unqualified. She has none because experience is the one thing you cannot buy, cannot study for, and cannot get without first being given a chance to get it.

She clicks submit anyway. Software reads her application and scores it. And in a few days, if she hears anything at all, an automated email will tell her that the company has decided to move forward with other candidates.

I want to talk tonight about Maya, and about the hundreds of thousands like her. And about a question that is larger than any one of them: what happens to a society when the bottom rung of the ladder is quietly sawed off?

I am going to try to answer that question carefully. I am not going to shout at you. I am going to count.

There is a famous speech, given in a hall in New York on the twenty-seventh of February, eighteen sixty, by a lawyer from Illinois whom most of the audience had never seen. He did something unusual that night. He did not begin by attacking his opponents. He began by agreeing with them.

His opponent had said that the men who framed the government understood the question at hand better than anyone living. And the lawyer from Illinois said, in effect: fine. I accept that. Let us take it as our text. And then he went back, man by man and vote by vote, through the record of those framers, and he counted. He counted until the count made his argument for him.

I want to do the same thing tonight.

So let me begin by agreeing with the people who think I am wrong.

[PAUSE]

Here is their text. It is a good one. It goes like this. Every great technology in human history has destroyed jobs, and every great technology in human history has created more jobs than it destroyed. The loom put the hand weavers out of work, and the factory employed their grandchildren by the million. The tractor emptied the farms, and the farmers' children went to the cities and built a richer life. The automatic teller machine was supposed to end the bank teller, and instead the banks opened more branches and hired more tellers to sell more things.

That is their text. And I accept it. I accept it not as a trick but because I think, over the long run, over decades, it has mostly been true. Economists who have studied this have found that the majority of the jobs Americans hold today are in kinds of work that did not exist in nineteen forty. The future has a way of inventing work we cannot yet name.

So I will grant all of it. I will grant that the economy as a whole is not collapsing. I will grant that, as of tonight, there is no evidence of mass, economy-wide unemployment caused by artificial intelligence. I will grant that many of the loudest predictions have been wrong, and that some companies have blamed machines for layoffs that had other causes.

[LET IT LAND]

And having granted all of that, I am going to show you, from the record, that something is still badly wrong. Not with the economy as a total. With the entrance to it.

Because the optimists' text has a hidden assumption inside it. It assumes that when the new work arrives, there will be a way in. It assumes that the young will be trained, somehow, by someone, to do the new work and the old work both. It assumes the ladder.

And the ladder is exactly the thing that is now in question.

Over the next two hours I am going to call witnesses. First, the young graduates themselves, and what the Federal Reserve's own figures say about them. Second, the payroll records of millions of American workers. Third, the companies, in their own words. Then I will walk through four kinds of work, one at a time: the coder, the voice on the customer service line, the paralegal in the back room of the law firm, and the junior analyst at the accounting firm. Then I will give the other side its strongest case, at full strength, and answer it. And only then will I say what I think we owe the young, and what I think those who hire, and those who govern, ought to do about it.

That is the order. Evidence first. Conclusion after.

I should also tell you what I am not arguing. I am not arguing that we should slow down this technology, or ban it, or pretend it is not useful. I use it myself. I am not arguing that every lost entry-level task was a good job, or that the drudgery of the past was noble in itself. And I am not arguing that the young are owed a particular career, or a particular salary, or a life without struggle. Every generation has had to fight its way in.

What I am arguing is narrower, and I think harder to escape. I am arguing that a society owes its young a way in. That the way in has always been the routine work at the bottom of each trade. That this technology is best at exactly that work. And that unless we act on purpose, the way in will close, quietly, while the totals look fine.

If, at the end, the count does not support me, you should not believe me.

But I do not think that is how it will come out.

## II. What a First Rung Is

Before we look at the numbers, we need to be clear about what we are counting. Because the phrase "entry-level job" sounds small. It sounds like a starter apartment, a thing you pass through on the way to something real.

That is not what it is. An entry-level job is one of the oldest institutions in human civilization. It is how one generation hands its knowledge to the next.

[PAUSE]

In fifteen sixty-three, the Parliament of Queen Elizabeth the First passed a law that historians call the Statute of Artificers. Among many other things, it said that no one could practice a skilled trade in England without first serving a seven-year apprenticeship.

Seven years. Think about that. A boy would go to live in a master's household. He would sweep the floors. He would fetch and carry. He would do the dull, repetitive work that the master no longer wanted to do himself. And in exchange, slowly, by watching and by failing and by being corrected, he would learn the trade.

The law was harsh in many ways, and much of it deserves no defense. It fixed wages. It restricted who could move between jobs. It reserved the better trades for the children of the better-off. I am not here to praise it.

But it understood something that we are in danger of forgetting. It understood that skill is not transferred by a lecture. It is transferred by work. And that the work by which it is transferred is, very often, the boring work. The low work. The work nobody senior wants to do.

[SLOW]

And notice what came after the apprenticeship. In the old trades, a young person who finished his years of service did not become a master overnight. He became something in between, a journeyman. The word comes from the French word for a day, because the journeyman was paid by the day. He was a qualified worker, free to sell his labor, but not yet trusted to run a shop of his own. In some parts of Europe, journeymen traveled for years from town to town, working under different masters, learning different methods, before they could settle and open a workshop.

Think about how carefully that system was built. Apprentice, journeyman, master. Three rungs. Each one had its own work, its own pay, its own responsibilities. And the whole thing rested on a simple understanding: that the people at the top had a duty to bring up the people at the bottom, because one day the people at the bottom would be all that was left.

Cross the ocean and come forward a century and a half. A boy in Boston, the youngest son of a candle-maker, is twelve years old when he is bound as an apprentice to his older brother, a printer.

He sets type. He inks the presses. He runs errands through the streets of Boston. It is drudgery. And because he is around the work, because he is handling the words of other people letter by letter, he begins to write. He becomes a writer, then a printer in his own right, then a publisher, a scientist, a diplomat, and one of the men who signed the founding documents of this country.

His name was Benjamin Franklin. He ran away to Philadelphia at seventeen. But he ran away with a trade in his hands. He had been given the first rung.

Come forward again, to the young man from Illinois whose speech I mentioned. Before he was a lawyer, before he was anything, he clerked in a general store in a frontier village. He did the counting and the stocking and the talking to customers. He borrowed law books and read them by himself, and was admitted to the bar in eighteen thirty-six. His entry-level job was humble. It was also the place where he learned to talk to ordinary people, which turned out to be the most important skill he ever had.

[PAUSE]

Now come forward to our own century, and look at the most skilled profession most of us ever encounter: medicine.

We do not let a young doctor graduate from medical school and walk straight into an operating room alone. We make her serve a residency. For years, she works long, exhausting hours doing much of the routine labor of the hospital, under supervision, so that she can learn by doing what no textbook can teach. And here is the remarkable part. We, the public, pay for a great deal of that apprenticeship. Federal funding for graduate medical education has run to roughly fifteen billion dollars a year, most of it through Medicare.

Why do we do that? Because, as a society, we decided long ago that the training of the next generation of doctors was too important to leave to chance. We decided that the first rung was a public concern.

[BUILD]

So here is what an entry-level job really is. It is the apprentice's broom. It is Franklin's type case. It is the store counter in New Salem. It is the resident's night shift.

It is the place where a person who knows how to learn becomes a person who knows how to do.

And notice what all of those first rungs had in common. The work that was handed to the beginner was the work that was easiest to describe, easiest to check, and easiest to repeat. The first draft. The first pass. The routine case. The simple call. The cleanup.

That is not an accident. That is the design. You give the beginner the work that is codified, so that they can learn the judgment that is not.

[LET IT LAND]

And now we have built machines whose greatest strength is precisely the work that is codified. The first draft. The first pass. The routine case. The simple call. The cleanup.

We have built a technology that is best at exactly the tasks we used to give to beginners.

That is the whole argument of tonight in one sentence. Everything after this is the evidence.

So let us look at it.

## III. The First Witness: The Graduates

The first witness is the young graduate. And the record keeper is no pundit and no activist, but the Federal Reserve Bank of New York.

For years, the New York Fed has tracked what it calls the labor market for recent college graduates. It defines them plainly: people between twenty-two and twenty-seven years old who hold at least a bachelor's degree and are no longer in school. It measures how many of them cannot find work. And it measures how many of them have found work that does not require the degree they paid for.

[PAUSE]

In its release this past August, covering the second quarter of this year, the New York Fed put the unemployment rate for recent college graduates at about five point six percent. It used a word for that number that central bankers do not use lightly. It called it elevated.

Now, five point six percent may not sound like a catastrophe. And taken alone, it is not. In the depths of a real recession, recent graduates have seen far worse.

But the number means something different when you set it beside the others.

For most of modern American history, a college degree was a kind of shelter. If you had one, you were less likely to be out of work than the population as a whole. That was the point of going. That was what the parents who took out the loans were buying.

That shelter has a leak in it now. For several years, the unemployment rate for young graduates has run higher than the unemployment rate for the American workforce as a whole. Earlier this year, when the national rate was a little over four percent, young graduates stood at five and a half and more. People with a fresh degree, the people we told to do everything right, are now more likely to be looking for work than the average American worker of any age and any education.

[LET IT LAND]

Then there is the second number the Fed tracks, which I think is the more important one. Underemployment. That is the share of recent graduates who are working, but in jobs that typically do not require a college degree. The Fed's latest figure is about forty-two percent.

Forty-two percent. Four in ten young graduates who have a job are working in one that did not need their education. Some of them will climb out. Many always have. But every year a graduate spends in a job that does not use the degree is a year in which they are not building the experience the next employer will ask for.

And that is the trap. Remember the form at the kitchen table. Three to five years of relevant experience. If your first job is not relevant, your second application looks exactly like your first.

Go back to Maya for a moment. Suppose that by Christmas she gives up waiting and takes a job at the front desk of a dental office, because the rent is real and the callbacks are not. It is honest work, and she is good at it. She is also now one of the forty-two percent.

And next spring, when she sits down at the kitchen table again, the form will ask the same question. Three to five years of relevant experience. She will have one year of experience. It will not be relevant. She will be a little further from the door than she was this autumn, and she will have done nothing wrong.

Hold on to her. We will come back to her more than once tonight.

[PAUSE]

Now look at which graduates are struggling. If this were simply a story about soft degrees in a hard market, you would expect the engineers to be fine.

They are not.

In the table the New York Fed published last year, looking across majors, computer engineering graduates had an unemployment rate of about seven and a half percent. Computer science graduates were at about six point one percent. Computer engineering was the third worst of more than seventy majors on the list, worse than many fields that parents have been warning their children away from for a generation.

Think about what that means. For twenty years, we told young people: learn to code. We told them it was the safe path, the practical path, the path that could not be offshored or automated. Governors said it. Presidents said it. Commencement speakers said it.

And the young people listened. They filled the computer science departments. They took the hard classes. They graduated into a market where the people with exactly the skills we told them to get are now listed near the anthropology majors we used to make jokes about, in the part of the table nobody wants to be in. Not always. Not everywhere. But often enough to show up in the Federal Reserve's tables.

[SLOW]

This witness has limits, and you should hear them. A rate of unemployment does not tell you its cause. Graduates may be struggling for many reasons: higher interest rates, companies that overhired during the pandemic and then pulled back, the end of cheap money in technology, a glut of degrees in some fields. All of that is real, and I will come back to it.

But hold on to the pattern. The young are doing worse than the old. The educated young are doing worse than they used to relative to everyone else. And the young who trained for the most technical, most codified knowledge work are among those doing worst.

If that pattern were caused only by the business cycle, we would expect it to hit everyone. A recession is not choosy. It does not pick out twenty-three-year-olds with computer science degrees and leave forty-five-year-olds in the same field alone.

So the question becomes: is something choosing?

To answer that, we need a better witness than a survey. We need the payroll.

## IV. The Second Witness: The Payroll

Surveys ask people what happened to them. Payroll records show what happened.

The second witness comes from a team at Stanford University's Digital Economy Lab: the economist Erik Brynjolfsson and his colleagues Bharat Chandar and Ruyu Chen. They did something that had not been possible before. They got access to the administrative payroll data of ADP, the company that processes paychecks for a very large share of American employers. Millions of workers. Month by month. Real paychecks, not opinions.

They titled their paper with a question mark. "Canaries in the Coal Mine?" The miners of old carried a canary below ground because the bird would sicken from bad air before the men did. The question in the title is whether young workers are that canary for artificial intelligence.

[PAUSE]

Here is how they did it. They took every occupation and sorted it by how exposed it is to what today's AI systems can do. Then they split workers by age. And then they watched what happened to employment in each group from late twenty twenty-two, when the first widely used chatbot was released to the public, onward.

The most recent version of their paper came out this August, using payroll data through June of this year. Let me give you its findings in the order they give them, because the order matters.

First, they found no evidence of widespread, economy-wide job displacement. Notice that. The witness I am calling to support my case begins by saying that the sky is not falling. That is exactly why I trust it.

Second. Take workers aged twenty-two to twenty-five, in the occupations most exposed to AI. Between November of twenty twenty-two and June of this year, their employment fell by roughly eleven percent.

Now take the same age group, in the less exposed occupations. Over the same months, their employment grew by about ten percent.

[LET IT LAND]

Eleven down on one side. Ten up on the other. A gap of twenty-one points.

The authors measure it another way, too. By their measure, young workers in exposed jobs now stand about nineteen percent below where they would be if they had kept pace with their peers in other work. Nearly a fifth.

And for older, experienced workers in the very same exposed occupations? The authors report no comparable gap.

Same jobs. Same companies. Same technology. The experienced are holding. The beginners are falling behind.

Third. The gap has not closed. When the authors first published, in August of last year, the headline figure was a thirteen percent relative decline. By the autumn it was sixteen. This summer it was nineteen. Every time they have checked, it has been wider.

Fourth, and this is the finding I want you to remember above all the others: the gap is happening mainly through hiring, not firing.

[SLOW]

The young people in these jobs are, for the most part, not being laid off. They are simply not being hired in the first place.

No one is escorted out of a building. No one is handed a box. There is no news story. There is no plant closing, no picket line, no ribbon of yellow tape. There is just a door that used to open, and now does not.

That is why this is so easy to miss. A layoff is an event. A hire that never happens is a non-event. It leaves no trace except in the payroll records of people who do not exist.

Let me make that concrete with an illustration. No particular company is meant. What I am describing is the shape of a decision being made in many of them.

Picture a manager at a mid-sized firm in the spring. Every year for ten years, she has hired four new graduates onto her team. They arrive in the summer. She gives them the routine work. By the following spring, two of them are good, one is excellent, and one has moved on. That is how her team has always grown.

This year, the budget meeting is different. Her own senior people have been using AI tools for a year, and they are getting through the routine work much faster. Someone from finance asks a reasonable question. Do you really need four new graduates this year? Could you manage with two? Or one? Or could you wait and see?

She thinks about it. Her team is, in fact, keeping up. The work is getting done. She does not want to be the manager who asked for headcount she did not need. So she says: let us try it with one.

Nobody was fired. Nobody was hurt that anyone could see. The firm saved money. The manager made a sensible decision.

And three young people, somewhere, whose names she will never know, did not get the job that would have started their careers. They do not know that the job existed. They only know that they applied and heard nothing. One of them might have been Maya.

Now multiply that meeting by every team, in every firm, in every exposed occupation, in every city. That is what the payroll data is measuring. Not a wave of layoffs. Countless small, reasonable decisions, each of which closes a door that no one sees close.

Fifth. The declines are concentrated in occupations where AI is mostly used to substitute for human tasks. Where AI is mostly used to complement workers, to help them do more, employment is flat or rising, especially for experienced workers.

Sixth. The adjustment is coming through the number of jobs, not through pay. Firms are not paying young people less. They are hiring fewer of them.

[PAUSE]

Now, I promised to be honest with you, and Lincoln, in his famous count, corrected his own numbers as he went. He told his audience when a figure should be reduced, when a man had been counted twice. So let me do that now.

The authors themselves call these findings early, descriptive indicators, not causal proof. They say the pattern weakens somewhat when you control for education. They say some of the divergence was already starting before the chatbots arrived. They say the effect looks larger in the ADP data than in the national surveys, though they also find some consistent evidence in government records. In a note published this February, they acknowledged that when you control carefully for what is happening inside each firm, the drop for the most exposed young workers only becomes clearly visible in twenty twenty-four.

They also tested the most obvious alternative explanation, which is interest rates. When rates went up, borrowing-dependent industries pulled back. But they found that the most AI-exposed jobs are, on average, less sensitive to interest rates, not more, and that the gap persists when you account for that. They tested remote work. They excluded technology firms and computer occupations altogether. The gap persists.

They even looked by region. The decline for the most exposed young workers shows up in every region of the country. It is steepest in the West, where employment in the most exposed group fell about twenty-one percent from its late twenty twenty-two level, and in the Northeast, at about thirteen. It is smaller in the South and the Midwest. But it is everywhere.

[BUILD]

So here is where the second witness leaves us. Not with proof beyond all doubt. With a pattern that is large, that is growing, that is concentrated on the young, that is concentrated in exactly the jobs the machines can do, and that is happening through a door that closes silently instead of a door that slams.

The economy is not losing jobs overall. It is losing first jobs.

## V. The Third Witness: The Firms Themselves

The third witness matters most, because it has the least reason to exaggerate. It is the employers, speaking in their own words.

Start with the companies that build the technology, because they also hire for the jobs most exposed to it.

SignalFire is a venture capital firm that studies hiring data across the technology industry. Last year it published its State of Talent report. At the largest technology companies, it found, hiring of new graduates had fallen by more than half compared with twenty nineteen.

By the time of the report, new graduates were about seven percent of Big Tech's hires. At startups, they were under six percent, and more than thirty percent below their twenty nineteen level.

[PAUSE]

The same report named the problem in plain language. It called it the experience paradox: companies post jobs labeled junior and then fill them with experienced people. You need experience to get the job, and you need the job to get experience. Employers, it said, are no longer looking for potential. They are looking for proof.

Remember who is saying this. Not a union. Not a campus protest. A venture capital firm, describing the market it puts its own money into.

Maya has met the experience paradox a hundred and forty times. Now she knows it has a name.

Next, listen to the people who run the companies.

In May of last year, the chief executive of one of the leading AI laboratories, Dario Amodei of Anthropic, sat down with the news site Axios. He said that AI could eliminate up to half of all entry-level white-collar jobs, and could push unemployment to between ten and twenty percent, within one to five years. He named finance, law and consulting among the fields at risk.

[LET IT LAND]

Handle that witness with care. It is a forecast, not a measurement. Forecasts are often wrong, and this one has been criticized by serious people. But notice who made it. Not a critic of the industry. One of its leaders. Warning, not boasting.

A month later, in June of last year, the chief executive of Amazon, Andy Jassy, wrote to his employees about generative AI. He said that in the next few years, the company expected it would reduce its total corporate workforce. Amazon is one of the largest employers in the world. When its chief executive tells his staff, in writing, that the white-collar headcount will shrink, he is not speculating about the economy. He is describing his plan.

In September of last year, Marc Benioff, the chief executive of Salesforce, said on a podcast that he had taken his company's customer support staff from about nine thousand people to about five thousand, because, as he put it, he needed fewer heads. AI agents, he said, were now handling about half of the company's customer conversations. Salesforce said it had moved hundreds of people into other roles. I will take them at their word. But four thousand support positions did not move.

[PAUSE]

And then there is the broadest witness of all, the firm of Challenger, Gray and Christmas, which has tracked announced job cuts in America for decades.

In twenty twenty-three, Challenger began tracking artificial intelligence as its own separate reason for layoffs. Its report this August covered the first seven months of this year.

In those seven months, employers cited AI in one hundred and twelve thousand, seven hundred and thirteen announced job cuts.

That is about one in every four cuts announced in America this year. And for the fifth month in a row, AI was the single leading reason companies gave.

[SLOW]

Now let me do my correcting again. Challenger itself warns that what counts as an AI-related cut is ambiguous. Some companies cite AI because it sounds forward-looking to investors, when the real reason is cost or a bad quarter. Overall announced cuts this year are actually down sharply from last year, and hiring plans are up. Andy Challenger, speaking for the firm, put it this way: while AI is shifting the labor market, it is not dismantling it. I agree.

But read those numbers alongside the payroll data. The layoff numbers capture people who had jobs and lost them. They do not capture the jobs that were never posted. They do not capture the class of graduate trainees that was quietly made smaller. They do not capture the twenty-three-year-old at the kitchen table.

Put the three witnesses side by side.

The graduates say: we cannot get in. The payroll says: the young in exposed jobs are not being hired, while the experienced hold steady. And the firms say: yes. We are doing more with fewer people, we are hiring fewer beginners, and we are planning to do more of it.

[BUILD]

Ask the people outside the door. Ask the records of who came through it. Ask the people who hold the door. When all three tell you the same story, you are no longer dealing with a hunch.

Now let us go job by job, the way Lincoln went man by man.

## VI. The Coders

Begin with the people who wrote the software that is now writing software.

There is a bitter irony here, and I do not want to rush past it. For a generation, the young person who learned to program was the one we held up as having made the wise choice. Programming was the craft of the future. It was the field that would never run short of work, because the whole world was becoming software.

And it was the first field the new machines learned to do.

[PAUSE]

It is not hard to see why. Code is written down. Billions of lines of it sit in public on the internet, along with the questions people asked about it and the answers other people gave. Code can be checked: it runs or it does not, it passes the test or it fails. It is, in the language I used earlier, codified knowledge in the most literal sense. If you were designing a technology to learn from the written record, you could hardly find a better first subject.

The Stanford paper I described cites the annual AI Index, which found that on one widely used benchmark of real software engineering problems, AI systems went from solving about four percent of them in twenty twenty-three to about seventy-two percent in twenty twenty-four. One year. From almost nothing to most of the test.

Now, a benchmark is not a job. A real software engineer does far more than solve a well-specified problem from a test set. She talks to the people who will use the system. She figures out what they actually need, which is often not what they asked for. She decides what to build and what to leave out. She takes responsibility when it breaks at two in the morning.

[SLOW]

But think about what a junior engineer used to do in her first year. She fixed small bugs. She wrote tests. She made the minor changes that the senior people did not have time for. She wrote the first version of a function that someone more experienced would then review and correct. That is the type case. That is the apprentice's broom. That is how she learned the system, the codebase, the craft.

That is also precisely the work that the machines now do in seconds.

So what does a senior engineer do now when she needs a small bug fixed? Increasingly, she does not walk over to the desk of a junior colleague. She asks the machine. It is faster. It does not need to be taught. It does not need health insurance. And it does not, at the end of the year, become a mid-level engineer who can take on harder work.

[LET IT LAND]

Look at how the government's own forecasters are reading this. The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes projections for every occupation, ten years out. In its latest handbook, it projects that employment of computer programmers, the occupation most focused on writing code to specification, will shrink by about seven percent between twenty twenty-five and twenty thirty-five.

At the same time, it projects that employment of software developers, the broader occupation that designs systems and decides what to build, will grow by about ten percent.

Hold those two forecasts side by side, because they tell the whole story of tonight in miniature. The part of the work that is writing code to instructions is shrinking. The part of the work that requires judgment about what to build is growing. And the part that is shrinking is the part we used to give to beginners. The part that is growing is the part that requires years of experience to do.

The optimist looks at those numbers and says: see, net growth. And the optimist is right about the total.

But how does a person get from the first group to the second? How does a twenty-two-year-old become a software developer with judgment if the programming work through which judgment was learned is handed to a machine?

[PAUSE]

Recall the Stanford finding that, in exposed occupations, the gap is between the young and the experienced. Experienced workers in the same occupations are holding steady or growing. That fits exactly. An experienced engineer with an AI assistant is more productive than before. The tool complements her. She has the judgment to direct it and to catch its mistakes. A beginner with the same tool has no such judgment yet. For the firm, the cheapest way to get more output is to give the veteran a better tool, and leave the beginner unhired.

The authors put it in their own careful language: where AI substitutes for human work, the young lose ground. Where it complements human work, employment is flat or rising, especially for the experienced.

[BUILD]

So the coder is the first canary. And what we learn from the coder is this: the technology is not replacing the profession. It is replacing the apprenticeship to the profession.

The profession will survive. Whether there will be anyone to inherit it is a different question.

## VII. The Voice on the Line

Now leave the gleaming offices of the technology companies and go somewhere much more ordinary. Go to the call center.

Many of us have a slightly impatient relationship with customer service. We call when something has gone wrong. We wait on hold. We are not always at our best. And on the other end of the line, there is a person whose whole job is to absorb our frustration and solve our problem.

That person has, for decades, been doing one of the great entry-level jobs in the American economy.

[PAUSE]

According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, about two point six seven million Americans worked as customer service representatives in twenty twenty-five. The median pay was about forty-four thousand seven hundred and seventy dollars a year. And the typical education required was a high school diploma.

Hear that last fact clearly. This is not a story only about college graduates. The customer service job was one of the most important first rungs for Americans without a degree. A young person from a town with no university and no tech hub could get a job on the phones, learn how a business works, learn how to handle an angry person with patience, learn how to solve problems under pressure, and climb. Into supervision. Into training. Into sales, into operations, into management. Some of the most capable managers I have known started on a headset.

The same BLS handbook now projects that employment of customer service representatives will fall by about five percent over the next ten years. That is about one hundred and forty-two thousand fewer jobs.

[SLOW]

That projection may turn out to be too gentle. Listen again to what Marc Benioff said about Salesforce: from nine thousand support staff to five thousand, with AI handling about half the conversations. And listen to the story of a company called Klarna.

In February of twenty twenty-four, Klarna, the Swedish payments company, announced that its new AI assistant was doing the work of seven hundred customer service agents. It said the assistant had handled millions of customer conversations in its first month alone. It was hailed as the shape of things to come.

Then, a little over a year later, Klarna's chief executive, Sebastian Siemiatkowski, told Bloomberg that the company would make sure customers could always reach a human being. He said cost had been too predominant a factor in how the company organized its service, and that what you got in the end was lower quality. Klarna began hiring people again.

[PAUSE]

The optimist will hear that story and say: you see, the machines cannot do it, the humans came back. And there is real truth in that. Klarna learned that the hardest calls, the confused customer, the angry customer, the customer whose problem fits no category, still need a person.

But look at the shape of what came back. The machine kept the simple calls. The routine questions. The password resets and the where-is-my-refund and the how-do-I-change-my-address. The humans came back for the hard ones.

And the simple calls were exactly where a new hire used to start.

[LET IT LAND]

In a well-run call center, you do not hand a new employee the furious customer with the tangled account on her first morning. You give her the easy calls. You let her build confidence. You let her learn the systems and the policies on problems that are forgiving. Then, gradually, she gets the harder ones.

If the machine takes every easy call, then the only work left for humans is the hard work. And the only people who can do the hard work are the people who learned on the easy work. Who will that be, ten years from now?

This is the pattern we found with the coder, and it is the pattern we will find again and again. AI does not take the whole job. It takes the bottom of the job. And the bottom of the job was the training ground for the top.

[BUILD]

For the young woman with a high school diploma in a small town, the call center was not a dead end. It was a doorway. A doorway with a paycheck, with benefits, with a supervisor who could see her potential and promote her.

If that doorway narrows to the width of the hardest calls, she does not get through it. And unlike the computer science graduate, she does not have a degree to fall back on while she waits.

## VIII. The Back Room of the Law

Now go to the law.

The legal profession has one of the oldest apprenticeship traditions we have. For centuries, young lawyers learned their trade in the chambers of older lawyers, reading their files, drafting their papers, carrying their bags into court. The man from Illinois read law on his own, but he learned to practice it as the junior partner of older, established lawyers.

In the modern law firm, that tradition took a particular shape. Picture a pyramid. At the top, a handful of partners. Below them, a larger band of senior associates. Below them, a large base of junior associates and paralegals. And at the very bottom, an enormous amount of work: document review, legal research, first drafts of memos and contracts, the checking of citations.

[PAUSE]

Document review is a good example of what I mean. When two companies sue each other, there may be millions of emails and files that must be read to see which ones matter. For a generation, that reading was done by first-year associates and paralegals, sometimes by contract attorneys hired just for the purpose. It was tedious. It was also how a young lawyer learned what evidence looked like, what mattered in a case, and how a dispute was actually built.

Picture one of those first-years. A composite again, but any lawyer of a certain age will recognize her. It is her third week. She is in a windowless conference room with a screen and a stack of coding instructions, tagging emails: relevant, not relevant, privileged. It is the dullest work she has ever done.

And then, on a Thursday afternoon, she opens an email that does not fit. A date that is wrong. A name copied who should not have been. She walks it down the hall to a senior associate, who reads it twice and closes the door.

That afternoon she learned more about how a case is won than in a semester of law school. She could only learn it because she had read the four thousand emails that came before it.

The New York Times reported last year, in a passage a well-known legal blogger quoted with some sorrow, that junior paralegals and first-year associates who once cut their teeth on document review are now handing weeks of that work to AI tools that finish it in hours.

[SLOW]

The numbers are beginning to show it. A legal recruiting firm, BCG Attorney Search, reported this year that eighty-six percent of large firms plan to grow their overall associate ranks through twenty twenty-seven, but only thirty-five percent plan to grow their first-year classes.

Read that again. The firms want more lawyers. They do not want more beginners.

And they have another way to get the lawyers they need: hire them already trained from other firms. One industry study found that lateral hires, lawyers who move from one firm to another, made up more than half of the associates hired by the two hundred largest American firms in a recent year.

[PAUSE]

Here, too, the Bureau of Labor Statistics adds a quiet note. There were about four hundred and five thousand paralegals and legal assistants in twenty twenty-five, earning a median of about sixty-three thousand dollars. Over the next decade, the Bureau projects essentially no growth. Little or no change, over ten years, in a country whose population and economy will both grow.

The legal career site Vault put the core issue better than I can. The tasks AI has begun to absorb, it wrote, document review, first-pass drafting, legal research, were never just busywork. They were also how junior associates were trained.

[LET IT LAND]

Never just busywork. That phrase could be the subtitle of this entire speech.

A law firm that stops hiring first-years because a machine can review the documents will save money this year. Its partners will take home more. And in fifteen years, when those partners retire, it will look around for experienced senior associates to promote, and find that it trained none. It will try to hire them from other firms, and find that the other firms trained none either.

The pyramid does not survive without its base. It becomes a column, and then a pillar, and then a few aging people at the top of something that has nothing underneath.

## IX. The Junior Analyst

Our fourth occupation is the one that, more than any other, was built on the idea of the apprentice: the junior analyst. The young accountant at the large audit firm. The first-year analyst at the investment bank. The new consultant building slides at two in the morning.

These firms are famous for their pyramid shape. They hire very large classes of graduates every year. They work them extremely hard on routine tasks: checking numbers, reconciling accounts, building spreadsheets, formatting presentations, testing samples of transactions in an audit. Most of those graduates leave within a few years. A few rise to manager, fewer still to partner.

The whole model rests on that wide base. It is how the firms do the work, and it is how they find and form their future leaders.

Let me describe what that base actually learns, because from the outside it can look like nothing but drudgery.

A first-year auditor is sent to a client's office and handed a stack of invoices. Her job is to check that each one matches a payment, a contract, an entry in the books. It is tedious. She will do thousands of them. But by the end of her first busy season, she knows how a real company's money actually moves. She has seen what an ordinary invoice looks like, and so she has begun to notice the odd one: the round number, the vendor with no address, the payment that arrives a little too quickly. She has watched a senior colleague ask a client a question and seen the client's face change. She has learned, without anyone ever telling her in so many words, what it feels like when something is not right.

A first-year analyst at a bank spends her nights building financial models in a spreadsheet, line by line, and having them torn apart by an associate the next morning. She learns why the model was wrong. She learns which assumptions matter and which ones do not. Years later, when she is the one reviewing the model, she can spot the flaw in a glance, because she made that same mistake herself when she was twenty-two.

[SLOW]

Those are exactly the tasks, the matching, the reconciling, the first-draft model, the formatted slide, that AI systems now perform quickly and cheaply. And when a firm hands those tasks to the machine, it saves the hours. It also, unless it is very careful, throws away the busy season in which the young auditor learned to feel that something was wrong.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what is happening to the base.

Business Insider reported that PricewaterhouseCoopers in the United States planned to cut its graduate hiring by about a third by twenty twenty-eight. The internal documents it described pointed to AI, to work moved offshore, and to the firm's own reorganization.

In Britain, where the press has followed graduate hiring closely, KPMG's graduate intake fell from about fourteen hundred in twenty twenty-three to under a thousand the following year. Deloitte's fell from about seventeen hundred to about fourteen hundred.

[SLOW]

These witnesses deserve their caveats too. A slow economy plays a part. One of these firms said it expected to hold its graduate hiring roughly flat. Offshoring plays a part. The end of a pandemic-era hiring spree plays a part.

I accept all of that. But I ask you to look at what the firms are doing with their future, not just their present. When a firm with a historic, century-old pyramid model tells you that technology is reshaping how it works and that it will hire a third fewer graduates, it is telling you that it believes the base of the pyramid is no longer needed in the same numbers. That belief, held across an industry, is itself the evidence.

[PAUSE]

There is a phrase I have heard used for what may happen to these firms. The pyramid becomes a diamond. Fewer people at the bottom, a bulge of experienced people in the middle who direct the machines, and the partners at the top.

A diamond is a beautiful shape. It is also a shape that cannot reproduce itself. The bulge in the middle of a diamond came from the wide base of a pyramid. When the people in the middle retire, where does the next middle come from?

[LET IT LAND]

That is the question I want to pose, in the manner of the man from Illinois, to every executive committee in every firm in the country. Not as an accusation. As a question that deserves an answer.

Who, in your firm, will be senior in twenty forty?

Not who is senior now. You know who that is. Who will be senior when the people now in the middle have retired? Where are they now? What are they doing this year? What work, specifically, are they doing that will teach them the judgment you will need from them then?

If the honest answer is "we have not hired them," then what looks like efficiency is something else. Your firm is consuming its own future to improve its present.

[BUILD]

Four occupations. The coder, the voice on the line, the paralegal, the junior analyst. Four very different worlds. One with a degree in computer science, one with a high school diploma, one in a law office, one in an accounting firm.

And the same pattern in every one. The machine takes the codified, routine, checkable work at the bottom. The experienced worker, made more productive, holds her place. And the beginner, whose job it was to learn through that routine work, finds the door narrower than it has been in a generation.

That is the count. Now let us hear the other side.

## X. The Strongest Objection

We are about halfway through the evening, and the count is in. Now comes the test of it. I promised I would give the other side its strongest case, at full strength. A speaker who only answers the weakest form of the opposing argument has not earned your agreement. So let me make their case better than they usually make it themselves.

[PAUSE]

The first objection is this. The economy as a whole is fine.

The economists at the Yale Budget Lab, working with colleagues at the Brookings Institution, have been measuring how fast the mix of American occupations has been changing since the first chatbots appeared. Their finding, repeated as they have updated their work, is that there has been no discernible disruption of the broader labor market.

The occupational mix has been changing only a little faster than it changed during earlier waves of technology, like the arrival of the personal computer and the internet. Not markedly so, they say. Much of the shift they see was already underway before twenty twenty-two. And the length of time workers in highly exposed jobs spend unemployed has not risen.

If AI were really hollowing out white-collar work, the objection says, you would see it in the totals. And the totals are calm.

The second objection is history, and it is the one I opened with. Every technology has raised these fears, and every time the fears have been wrong. The economist James Bessen studied what happened to bank tellers after the automatic teller machine spread. In the average urban market, the number of tellers per branch fell from about twenty to about thirteen between nineteen eighty-eight and two thousand four. But because ATMs made branches cheaper to run, the number of urban branches rose by forty-three percent. Teller employment held up. The tellers stopped counting cash and started building relationships and selling services.

And the economist David Autor and his colleagues have shown that most of the work Americans do today is in job specialties that did not exist in nineteen forty. New technology creates new kinds of work. We cannot see it coming, but it comes.

The third objection is that I have the cause wrong. Young graduates are struggling, yes. But interest rates rose sharply in twenty twenty-two and twenty twenty-three, and the industries that hire the most young graduates, technology above all, are the ones that depend most on cheap money. Many companies hired wildly during the pandemic and then cut back. Some work has been offshored. And some executives, the objection says, are simply using AI as a fashionable excuse for cuts they wanted to make anyway. The Yale researchers themselves have warned that some firms may be hiding behind AI rather than admitting deeper causes. Challenger warns that AI attribution is ambiguous.

The fourth objection is the most hopeful, and I think the most interesting. It says that AI is actually the beginner's best friend. Erik Brynjolfsson, the same economist whose payroll study I have been citing, published an earlier study with Danielle Li and Lindsey Raymond of more than five thousand customer support agents who were given an AI assistant. On average, their productivity rose about fourteen percent. But for the novice and less skilled agents, it rose about thirty-four percent. The experienced agents barely benefited at all. The AI, in effect, took what the best agents knew and gave it to the newest ones.

If AI makes beginners nearly as good as veterans, the objection says, it should make beginners more valuable, not less.

[LET IT LAND]

That is the case. It is a strong case. I want you to feel its strength. Now let me answer it, point by point.

To the first objection: I agree. I said so at the beginning, and the Stanford study says so as its very first finding. There is no economy-wide displacement. But my argument was never about the totals. It was about the entrance. And here is what I find striking. The same Yale researchers, in their first report, flagged one small exception to their calm picture: the gap between the kinds of jobs held by recent college graduates and the kinds held by older ones had been widening a little faster in recent months. They were careful to say it might only be a slowing market, and that their samples were small. But consider the shape of it. Calm totals, with a gap opening between the old and the young. That is exactly what you would expect if the door at the bottom is narrowing while the rooms above it are full.

A labor market can be healthy in aggregate while it is failing a single generation. In fact, that is precisely the kind of failure that aggregates hide. If older workers are holding their jobs, and becoming more productive with new tools, the total looks fine. The damage is visible only when you ask who is not getting in.

[PAUSE]

To the second objection, the argument from history: I accept that new work will come. But look closely at the story of the bank tellers. Why did it end well? It ended well because the remaining human work, relationship banking, could still be learned on the job by a new teller. The ATM took the cash counting. It left a job a beginner could still enter and grow in. The ladder survived.

The question tonight is not whether new work will come. It is whether the new work will have a bottom rung. If the new jobs created by AI are jobs that require ten years of judgment to do, jobs directing and checking the machines, then they will be wonderful jobs for people who already have ten years of judgment. And they will be closed to the young, who have no way to acquire it.

And there is something else history teaches that the optimists sometimes skip over. Yes, the grandchildren of the hand weavers found work in the factories. But the hand weavers themselves, many of them, did not. When we say that technology always creates more jobs than it destroys, we are speaking in the language of decades. People do not live their working lives in decades. They live them one year at a time. A twenty-three-year-old does not have a generation to wait for the new jobs to be invented. She has rent due on the first.

We use the word Luddite today as an insult, a name for anyone foolish enough to fear a machine. But the original Luddites, the textile workers of the English Midlands who began smashing machinery around Nottingham in eighteen eleven, were not fools. They were skilled craftsmen who could see, correctly, that the new machines and the new factory system were about to make their hard-won skills worth far less. Parliament's answer was to make the breaking of machines a crime punishable by death. Over the long run, the economy that emerged was vastly richer. The Luddites were wrong about the century. They were right about their own lives.

I am not a Luddite. I do not want to smash anything. These machines are remarkable, and I believe they can do enormous good. But the Luddites do teach a lesson, and it has nothing to do with whether technology is good. A society which tells the people who bear the costs of a transition to simply wait for the long run is asking them to pay for a future they may never see. And this time, the people being asked to wait are not the old craftsmen at the end of their careers. They are the young, at the very beginning of theirs.

[SLOW]

To the third objection, about other causes: I agree that there are other causes, and I have tried to name them honestly as we went. But consider three things.

First, interest rates and overhiring and offshoring would not, on their own, explain why, inside the very same occupations, young workers fall behind while experienced workers hold steady. A rise in interest rates does not check your birth certificate.

The Stanford researchers tested exactly this. The most AI-exposed jobs are, on average, less sensitive to interest rates, not more. The gap persists when you exclude technology firms and computer jobs entirely. And it has kept widening long after interest rates stopped rising.

Second, even if some executives are exaggerating AI's role in today's cuts, they are not exaggerating their plans. When the head of Amazon tells his workforce that AI will reduce the corporate headcount, when an accounting firm says technology is reshaping how it works and it will hire a third fewer graduates, those are not excuses for the past. They are statements about the future. You do not need to settle what caused last year's layoffs to take seriously what the people doing the hiring say they will do next.

Third, and this is the point I most want you to hold, it does not matter much for my conclusion which cause is the biggest. Whether the door is narrowing because of AI, or because of interest rates, or because of a habit of running lean that firms learned during the pandemic, the young are still outside it.

And AI is the force that makes a narrow door easy to keep narrow. A firm that cut its graduate class in a downturn would once have had to rebuild it when the work came back, because there was no one else to do the routine work. Now there is something else to do the routine work. The downturn ends. The graduate class does not come back.

[PAUSE]

And to the fourth objection, the hopeful one, I say: yes. Yes. That is the most important finding in this whole debate, and it is the most hopeful thing I will say tonight.

If AI raises the productivity of novices by a third, then AI could be the greatest apprenticeship tool ever invented. It could take a beginner and give her, in her first month, the accumulated know-how of the best people who ever held her job. It could compress the years it takes to learn a craft.

But notice the condition. It can only do that for a beginner who has been hired.

The study of the customer support agents measured people who already had jobs. The AI made them better. That is a reason to hire beginners and give them these tools. It is not what the payroll data says firms are doing. The payroll data says firms are, in exposed jobs, hiring fewer beginners, and giving the tools to the people they already have.

[BUILD]

So the strongest objection, examined, turns into the strongest part of my argument. The technology could build a better ladder than we have ever had. Instead, in too many places, we are using it to remove the ladder and keep only the people already at the top.

That is not a fact about the technology. That is a choice. And choices can be made differently.

But before I say how, I have to show you what the wrong choice costs. Because the cost does not end when the hiring comes back.

## XI. The Scar

This matters even if, in five years, the market recovers. Even if new jobs arrive. Even if every one of these young people eventually finds work.

Because economists have studied what happens to people who enter the workforce at a bad time. And what they have found is that the bad time does not stay in the past. It leaves a scar.

[PAUSE]

The economist Lisa Kahn studied young men who graduated from college in the nineteen eighties and followed them for nearly two decades. She compared those who happened to graduate when the economy was strong with those who happened to graduate when it was weak. These were people with the same kind of degree, born only a few years apart. The only difference was luck: the year they walked across the stage.

She found that graduating into a weak economy had large, negative effects on wages, and that those effects persisted for the entire period she studied. Not one year. Not five years. The whole of the time she could follow them. Those who started in bad times ended up in lower-level occupations. Her own summary of the consequences was three words: large, negative, and persistent.

A team of economists, Philip Oreopoulos, Till von Wachter and Andrew Heisz, studied Canadian graduates with a large dataset linking universities, employers and employees. They found that graduating in a recession led to earnings losses that lasted about ten years. The young people started at worse employers, at lower pay, and slowly worked their way to better ones. And here is the part I want you to hear. The more advantaged graduates recovered faster. The less advantaged graduates could be permanently affected.

[LET IT LAND]

Think about what that means. The first rung is more than where you start. It is a launching point that shapes the arc of everything after it. Who you meet. What you learn. What it says on your résumé when you apply for the second job. Whether you believe, deep down, that you are a person the world has a use for.

And those studies were about recessions. Recessions end. Hiring comes back. The scar they describe came from a temporary narrowing of the door.

What we are describing tonight is not obviously temporary. If firms decide, durably, that they need fewer beginners because a machine can do the work beginners used to do, then the narrowing does not end when the business cycle turns. It becomes the new structure. A whole cohort, and then the next, and then the next, enters through a door that does not widen again.

[PAUSE]

And the scar falls hardest, as those studies found, on the people with the least to fall back on.

The graduate whose parents can pay her rent while she takes an unpaid internship will find a way in. The graduate whose father knows a partner at the firm will get the interview. The young person who can afford to go back for a master's degree, to wait out the bad years in a classroom, will do so.

But the first-generation college graduate who took on debt to get her degree, whose family has no connections and no cushion, who needs a paycheck in June? She takes whatever job she can find, which is often one that does not use her degree. She joins the forty-two percent. And every year she spends there, the experience gap grows.

And the young person with a high school diploma, who would have started on the phones and climbed? He may find nothing to start on at all.

Consider what the experience paradox does to Maya over time.

In her first year out of school, she is a recent graduate, and employers expect her to lack experience. Some will still consider her.

By her second year, after a year at the front desk of the dental office, she is no longer a fresh graduate. She is a person with a two-year-old degree and no relevant experience, and the question in the interview becomes: what have you been doing?

By her third year, the newest graduates have arrived behind her, younger and cheaper and with fresher credentials, and she is competing with them, too.

That is the mechanism of the scar. One bad year makes the next year harder, and the year after that harder still. The door does not merely stay closed. It moves farther away.

And the unpaid internship, the traditional answer to the experience problem, only deepens the divide. A young person who can afford to work for nothing for a summer, or a year, can buy the experience the market demands. A young person who needs to pay rent cannot. When paid first jobs become scarce, unpaid ones become the price of entry, and that price is paid in family money.

This is how a technology that could have been the great equalizer becomes an engine of inherited privilege. Not because anyone intends it. Because when the open door closes, the only doors left are the ones that were always opened by connection, by money, by family.

The apprenticeship, for all its faults, was one of the few institutions that could take a child with nothing and give her a trade. The entry-level job, in the modern economy, played the same role. It was the place where talent without connections could be discovered.

[PAUSE]

There is one more scar. This one is on all of us.

Return to the question I asked the firms: who will be senior in twenty forty?

Every profession runs on a pipeline. Today's senior surgeon was yesterday's resident. Today's managing partner was yesterday's first-year associate. Today's chief engineer was yesterday's junior developer fixing small bugs. If you stop the flow at the bottom of a pipeline, nothing seems to happen for a while. The people already in the pipeline keep moving. The output looks fine.

And then, about fifteen or twenty years later, it stops. The experienced people retire, and there is no one behind them.

[LET IT LAND]

We are, right now, deciding how many experienced professionals this country will have in the twenty-forties. We are deciding it by how many beginners we hire in the twenty-twenties. And if we hire too few, no amount of AI will fully make up for it, because the one thing these systems still need is people with deep judgment to direct them, check them, and take responsibility for what they do.

You cannot hire a twenty-year veteran in twenty forty-five who was never hired as a beginner in twenty twenty-five.

A society that stops training its young is not saving money. It is borrowing against its future, and the bill comes due all at once.

## XII. What Cannot Be Written Down

Before I turn to what should be done, I want to stay a little longer with the question of what, exactly, is lost when the first rung disappears. Because I think the optimists and the pessimists both sometimes miss it.

The optimist says: the routine tasks were dull, and good riddance. Why should a bright young person spend a year reviewing documents or fixing trivial bugs or answering the same five questions on the phone? Let the machine do it, and let the young person start on interesting work.

It is a fair question. And the answer is the most important idea in this whole speech.

[PAUSE]

There was a scientist and philosopher named Michael Polanyi who, in the middle of the last century, gave a name to something every craftsman already knew. He called it tacit knowledge. His shortest summary of it was that we know more than we can tell.

Think about riding a bicycle. You know how to do it. But try to write down, in words, exactly what you do with your weight and your hands and your balance at every moment, in a form that someone who had never ridden could read and then ride. You cannot. The knowledge is real. It is in you. But it cannot be fully written down. It can only be learned by doing, usually badly at first, with someone nearby to steady the seat.

Most of the valuable knowledge in any profession is like that.

[SLOW]

The senior lawyer who reads a contract and feels that something is wrong before she can say what. The veteran engineer who looks at a design and says, that will not scale, and is right, and cannot quite explain why. The experienced manager who hears the tone of a customer on the phone and knows, in three sentences, whether this is a person who wants a refund or a person who wants to be heard. The auditor who looks at a set of books and senses where to dig.

That is judgment. And judgment is mostly tacit. It is built up from thousands of small encounters with real cases. Most of those cases are routine. That is the point. You learn what normal looks like by seeing a thousand normal cases. And only then can you notice the one case in a thousand that is not normal.

The first-year associate reviewing ten thousand documents is not only producing a review. She is building, without knowing it, an inner sense of what an ordinary business email looks like, so that years later, when she sees one that is not ordinary, something in her stirs. The junior developer fixing small bugs is learning, without knowing it, the shape of how systems fail. The new agent on the phones answering the same question for the hundredth time is learning the hundred ways people ask it, and the one way of asking that means something else is wrong.

[LET IT LAND]

That is why the routine work was given to beginners. Not only because it was cheap. Because it was the curriculum. The boring cases are the textbook of judgment, and there is no other textbook.

Here is the problem with the optimist's hope that young people can skip the routine work and start on interesting work. The interesting work requires the judgment. And the judgment is built from the routine work. If you skip the routine, you do not arrive at the interesting work early. You arrive at it unprepared.

[PAUSE]

Now give the machines their due. Perhaps the tools themselves can become part of the curriculum. Perhaps a young lawyer, working beside an AI that reviews documents, and checking its work, and asking it why it flagged this and not that, can learn faster than her predecessors did by reading every page herself. Perhaps a junior developer, reading and correcting code the machine has written, can learn the shape of good code more quickly than by writing every line from scratch. I think that is possible. I think it may even be likely, if it is done well.

But notice again that every one of those hopeful pictures has a young person in it. A young person who has been hired, who is being paid, who has a mentor, and whose job is, in part, to learn.

The danger is not that machines will do the routine work. The danger is that we will let the machines do the routine work and not replace the learning that came with it. We will keep the output and throw away the education. We will get this year's documents reviewed and lose the next generation's judgment.

[BUILD]

There is a phrase economists use for things that are produced as a side effect of something else. They call them byproducts. For centuries, the training of the next generation was a byproduct of the routine work of the present. Nobody had to plan it. Nobody had to pay for it separately. It came free, bundled with the dull tasks that needed doing anyway.

Artificial intelligence is unbundling it. The dull tasks can now be done without a beginner. And so, for the first time, the training no longer comes free. If we want it, we will have to choose it. We will have to plan it and pay for it, deliberately, the way we already do for doctors.

That is the real meaning of the title of this speech. The last entry-level job is not the last job anyone will ever start at the bottom of. It is the last generation in which the first rung came for free, as a byproduct of the work. From here on, if there is a ladder, it will be because someone decided to build one.

## XIII. A Word to Those Who Hire

At Cooper Union, after the long count, the man from Illinois did something unexpected. He turned and spoke directly to the people on the other side of the question. He did not curse them. He reasoned with them. He asked them what, exactly, they wanted, and whether what they were doing would get it for them.

So let me now speak directly to the people who make hiring decisions. The executives, the partners, the managers, the people who sign off on the size of next year's graduate class.

[PAUSE]

I do not think you are villains. I want to say that plainly. You are doing what you have been trained to do and paid to do. You have a new tool that lets your experienced people do more. You have shareholders or partners who expect margins. You look at a beginner, who costs a salary and benefits and months of supervision before she produces much, and you look at a tool that costs a fraction of that and produces immediately. The arithmetic is not hard.

But I want to ask you to do a different arithmetic. A longer one.

You know, better than anyone, what your best people are worth. You know that the senior engineer who can look at a system and see where it will fail, the partner who can read a contract and sense the trap in it, the manager who can walk into a crisis and calm it, is worth far more than her salary. And you know where that person came from. She came from your graduate class fifteen years ago. She came from the bottom of your pyramid. Somebody hired her when she knew nothing, and gave her the dull work, and let her learn.

[SLOW]

If you stop hiring at the bottom, you are not just saving the cost of a beginner. You are declining to produce the very people whose judgment your firm will depend on when you are gone.

You may say: that is not my problem. I will hire experienced people from other firms when I need them. And for one firm, for a while, that may work. But I ask you to imagine every firm in your industry making the same decision. Then who trains anybody? Every firm is waiting for some other firm to bear the cost of training, so it can poach the result. And if every firm waits, no one is trained, and in fifteen years you are all bidding against each other for a shrinking pool of veterans, at prices you will not like.

Economists have a name for this. It is a collective action problem. Each of you, acting sensibly alone, produces a result that is bad for all of you together. It is the same logic as overfishing a lake. Each boat takes a little more. No single boat empties the lake. And then one season, there are no fish.

[PAUSE]

So here is what I would ask of you. Not as a regulator. As a fellow citizen who wants your firm to still be great in twenty forty.

First: keep hiring beginners. Decide on a number, as a matter of policy, not as whatever is left over after the budget is set. Treat your graduate class and your entry-level hiring the way you treat research and development. It is an investment in capability you will need later, not an expense to be cut when this quarter is tight.

Second: use the tools on the beginners. Remember the study of the customer support agents. The AI helped the newest agents most. It took what the best people knew and put it in the hands of the novices. That is a gift. Do not waste it by handing the tools only to people who need them least. Give a twenty-two-year-old an AI assistant and a good mentor and you may find that she becomes productive in months instead of years. Call that what it is: the best return on training any firm has ever had.

[BUILD]

Third: protect the learning work. Some routine work is worth having a person do, even when a machine could do it faster, because the person doing it is learning something you will need from her later. Airlines learned this. As autopilot systems got better, the industry found it had to make sure pilots still practiced flying by hand, because the day the automation fails is the day you need a human who knows how. Your profession has its own version of hand flying. Find it. Protect it. Give it to your beginners on purpose.

Fourth: reward the people who teach. In most firms, the senior person who spends an afternoon walking a junior colleague through her mistakes is, on paper, less productive that afternoon. If you measure only output, you will get less teaching. Pay for it. Promote for it. Make mentoring part of what it means to be senior.

Fifth: be honest about your job postings. If a job is entry level, do not ask for three to five years of experience. Every time you do, you send a young person at a kitchen table the message that there is no door at all. If you want only experienced people, say so. If you want to give beginners a chance, write a posting a beginner can answer.

[PAUSE]

And to the firms that build these tools, I say this. You have told us, some of you with real courage, that your technology may displace a great deal of entry-level work. You have warned us plainly. Thank you for that honesty. Now match it. You hire some of the most talented young people in the world. Show the rest of the economy what a ladder looks like in the age of AI. Hire beginners. Train them with your own tools. Publish what you learn about how to do it well. If anyone can prove that AI and apprenticeship belong together, it is you.

[LET IT LAND]

To all of you who hire, I ask the same question I asked before, and I ask you to sit with it. Who will be senior in twenty forty? Go back to your office on Monday and find out. Look at your hiring for the past three years by experience level. Look at how many beginners you brought in, compared to five years ago, compared to ten. And then ask whether that number is the number of future leaders your firm will need.

If the answer frightens you, you still have time to change it. The pipeline is long. What you do this year will not show up for a decade. That is exactly why it has to be done this year.

## XIV. A Word to Those Who Govern, and to the Rest of Us

Lincoln, after speaking to the other side, turned at last to his own. He told them what their duty was. So let me turn now to the public, and to those who act in its name.

I want to be clear about what follows. What I have said until now has been, as best I could make it, a count of the evidence. What I am about to say is my own judgment about what to do. Reasonable people can disagree with it. I offer it as proposals, not as facts.

[PAUSE]

The first duty of government here is the simplest. It is to see.

Right now, we measure this problem mostly by accident. We know what we know about young workers in AI-exposed jobs because a team at Stanford happened to get access to a private payroll company's data. We know what we know about graduate unemployment because the Federal Reserve Bank of New York decided to build a tracker. We know about AI-related layoffs because an outplacement firm in Chicago started counting them. Each of them has done a public service. But a country should not have to rely on luck to see what is happening to its young.

I would propose that our federal statistical agencies measure entry-level hiring directly, by age and by occupation, regularly and in public.

We already measure unemployment to a tenth of a percentage point every month. We should know, just as precisely, how many first jobs this economy is creating, in which fields, and whether that number is falling.

Imagine if, every month, alongside the unemployment rate, the evening news reported how many young Americans had started a first full-time job in the field they trained for. Imagine if every chief executive knew that number for her own industry. I suspect the conversation in this country would change quickly.

Remember the lesson of the payroll data. This problem is made of hires that never happen. Unless we look for them on purpose, we will not see them at all.

[SLOW]

The second duty is to remember that we have done this before.

I told you about medicine. As a nation, we decided that training doctors was too important to leave entirely to hospitals' bottom lines. And so the public pays for a large share of residency training: on the order of fifteen billion dollars a year in federal money. We did not leave it to each hospital to decide whether a resident was worth the cost this quarter. We decided that the next generation of physicians was a public good.

I would propose that we ask whether the same logic now applies to other professions. If the routine work that used to pay for the training of junior accountants, junior lawyers and junior engineers is being absorbed by machines, then the training no longer pays for itself inside the firm. Something has to pay for it. One answer is a public share of the cost, the way we do for doctors, in exchange for real, supervised, paid training of beginners. Not a classroom program. Real jobs, with real mentors, doing real work.

We already have a framework for this in the registered apprenticeship system, which for most of its history has served the building trades and manufacturing. I would propose expanding it, seriously and with money behind it, into white-collar work: software, accounting, law, finance, customer operations, health administration. Many countries have long traditions of apprenticeship in office and technical work. We can learn from them.

The third duty is to look at the incentives we have built into our tax code. Today, when a firm buys software, it can often write that investment off in generous ways. When a firm trains a beginner, it can deduct the wages as an ordinary expense, but it gets little special encouragement for the investment it is making in that person's future. I would propose that we look at treating the training of entry-level workers the way we have long treated research: as an investment the public wants more of, and is willing to reward. A firm that hires and trains beginners is doing research of a kind. It is discovering and developing talent that the whole economy will later use.

I would also propose a simpler rule of fairness. If a large firm reports to its shareholders that AI is allowing it to reduce its workforce, it should report to the public, in the same breath, what it is doing to train the next generation. Not a mandate on how many to hire. A requirement to say. Sunlight has a way of changing behavior.

[BUILD]

The fourth duty belongs to our schools and colleges. For a century, the bargain was: you teach them the theory, and the employers will teach them the practice on the job. If employers are no longer teaching the practice, colleges cannot pretend nothing has changed.

There is a model for this, too, and it is old. Since the early twentieth century, some American universities have run cooperative education programs, in which students alternate between semesters in the classroom and semesters in paid work, so that they graduate with real experience on their résumés.

I would propose that this become the norm rather than the exception, especially in the fields most exposed to AI. A computer science degree that does not include a year of supervised, real-world work is going to be harder and harder to use. A degree that does may be the answer to the form on Maya's kitchen table. Three to five years of experience? Here is one, earned while I studied.

The fifth duty is to the young Americans who do not have a four-year degree, who are, after all, the majority of them. Do not forget them in a debate that is mostly conducted by people with degrees. The call center, the front desk, the clerical job, the bank branch: these were ladders for people without a bachelor's degree. If those rungs disappear, we need to build others, in the trades, in health care, in public service, in the new work that the technology will create. And we need to make sure that the new work has a bottom rung that does not require a degree the young person cannot afford.

I would propose one more idea, and I offer it as the most ambitious. If the private market cannot, for a time, offer enough first jobs, the public can.

There is a great deal of work in this country that needs doing: in our schools, in our hospitals, in the care of the old, in the repair of our infrastructure, in the modernization of our own government offices.

A national program of paid, supervised first jobs in public service, a year or two in which a young person learns to work, to show up, to be responsible to others, would give a generation the experience that the private market is withholding. We have done versions of this before, in hard times. We could do it again.

[SLOW]

And now, finally, a word to the rest of us. Those of us who are already on the ladder.

If you are a mid-career professional, you are probably among the people for whom AI has been, so far, a gift. It makes you faster. It takes the dull parts of your job off your plate. You may have noticed that you no longer need the junior colleague you used to rely on.

I am asking you to notice what that means. Somebody gave you your first rung. Somebody took a chance on you when you knew nothing. Somebody sat with you while you made your first mistakes and did not fire you for them. That person was not being efficient. That person was being generous, or was working at a firm that understood its own future.

[LET IT LAND]

There is an old phrase from farming that I think about often. A farmer who is desperate, at the end of a hard winter, may be tempted to eat his seed corn: the grain he set aside to plant in the spring. It will feed his family for a few more weeks. And then there will be no harvest.

Every firm that cuts its beginners to make this year's numbers is eating seed corn. Every professional who stops teaching because the tool makes teaching unnecessary this quarter is eating seed corn. Every college that keeps its curriculum the same while the jobs its graduates used to take disappear is eating seed corn. None of these decisions is foolish on its own. Each of them makes sense for a season. Together, they leave a field with nothing planted in it.

[PAUSE]

Be that somebody now. Answer the email from the stranger who wants advice. Take the intern. Fight, inside your firm, for the graduate class. When your manager asks whether you really need a junior person now that you have the tool, tell the truth: no, I do not need one this year. But the firm will need her in fifteen years, and so will the country.

The ladder you climbed was not a law of nature. It was built by people who came before you, and kept up by people who made choices. If it is going to exist for the next generation, it will be because people like you chose to keep it standing.

## XV. To the Ones at the Bottom of the Ladder

I have spoken to the firms, to the government, and to those of us already established. I want to end by speaking to the people this speech is really about.

To the young.

To Maya, at the kitchen table with her one hundred and fortieth application. To the computer science graduate who did everything we told him to do and is driving for a delivery app while he waits for a callback that does not come. To the young man in the small town who would have started on the phones and climbed, and now cannot find the phones. To the paralegal who wanted to go to law school and is no longer sure what will be left for her when she gets out. To the accounting student watching the graduate classes shrink.

[PAUSE]

I want to tell you three things.

The first is this. It is not your fault.

I know it can feel like it is. When you send out a hundred applications and hear nothing, it is very hard not to conclude that something is wrong with you. When the form asks for experience you do not have, it is hard not to feel that you are already behind in a race that started before you got there.

But I have spent the last two hours counting, and you have heard what the count says. The Federal Reserve says it. The payroll records say it. The companies, in their own words, say it. The door has narrowed for people your age, in the jobs most exposed to these machines, while it has stayed open for people with experience.

That says nothing about your worth. It says something about the year you walked across the stage. Your parents did not face this at your age. You did. That is luck, and only luck.

The second thing is that you are needed. More than the market is telling you.

Every argument I have made tonight comes down to this. The economy will need people with deep judgment, in every field, for as long as anyone can foresee. The machines are powerful, and growing more powerful, but they still need people who can direct them, check them, catch their mistakes, and take responsibility for what they do.

Those people do not grow on trees. They grow from beginners. They grow from you.

The firms that are not hiring you today are going to need you in fifteen years. Some of them know it. Many have not done the arithmetic yet. When they do, I hope they remember that you were here, ready, the whole time.

And the third thing is this. Do not wait for permission to learn.

I know how that sounds. It sounds like the advice comfortable people give to struggling people: pull yourself up by your bootstraps, from someone standing on your fingers. I do not mean it that way. Everything I said to the firms and to the government still stands. They owe you a ladder, and I will keep saying so.

But while we fight for that, use what is in front of you. The same tools that are narrowing the door can also, in your own hands, be the most patient tutor anyone has ever had. Use them to learn, not just to produce. Ask them why. Make them explain.

Build real things, and show them to people. Find the people already doing the work you want to do, and ask them for an hour of their time. More of them will say yes than you expect. Some of them, I hope, were in this room tonight.

Benjamin Franklin was a runaway apprentice. He left Boston at seventeen with very little money and arrived in Philadelphia with little more than a trade and a habit of reading. Abraham Lincoln had perhaps a year of formal schooling, all told. He taught himself law from borrowed books in a frontier village.

Neither of them waited for the world to make room. But both of them, and this is the part of their stories we tell less often, had someone who gave them a first rung. A brother who took on an apprentice. An older lawyer who lent the books, and later took the young man on as his partner.

You deserve that, too. Every generation does.

Now let me close where I began.

I began by accepting the text of the people who disagree with me. Technology has always created more work than it destroyed. The economy as a whole is not in collapse. Some of the alarm has been overstated, and some firms blame machines for cuts that have other causes. New kinds of work we cannot yet imagine will surely come.

I accept all of it still.

And I have shown you, witness by witness, what that text leaves out.

It leaves out the graduates, whose unemployment now runs higher than that of the workforce as a whole.

It leaves out the four in ten who have work, but work that does not need their degrees.

It leaves out the payroll records, and the young in the most exposed jobs falling nearly a fifth behind their peers, through hires that never happened.

It leaves out the companies, saying in their own words that they will need fewer people and fewer beginners.

It leaves out the coder whose apprenticeship was automated, the voice on the line whose easy calls were taken, the first-year whose document review went to a machine, and the junior analyst whose graduate class is shrinking by a third.

It leaves out the ladder.

[PAUSE]

The great technologies of the past created more work than they destroyed, yes. But they did it in a world where the young could still get in. Where the routine work that remained could still be learned by a beginner. Where the training came free, bundled with the dull tasks of the day.

That world is ending. The world of work will go on. The world of the free first rung will not.

So a choice has arrived that no previous generation had to make in quite this way. We can let the machines take the bottom of every ladder, keep only the people already at the top, and enjoy the savings for a while. Or we can decide, on purpose, that every generation deserves a way in, and build it.

We have made that decision before. We made it for doctors. We made it when we built public schools, and land-grant colleges, and apprenticeship programs, and the laws that took children out of the mines and put them in classrooms.

Each time, someone said it was too expensive, or none of the public's business, or that the market would sort it out. And each time, we decided that the next generation was everyone's business.

We can decide that again.

[BUILD]

We can decide that a firm which uses these machines to grow its profits has a duty to grow its people, too.

We can decide that a government which measures unemployment to a tenth of a point can measure the first jobs that are never created.

We can decide that a college degree should come with experience built in, and that a young person without a degree should still find a rung within reach.

We can decide that those of us already on the ladder will reach down, and not just up.

If we make those choices, then this extraordinary technology, which could have been the thing that closed the door, can open it wider than it has ever been opened. A tutor in every pocket. A mentor's knowledge in every beginner's hands. A first year that teaches what used to take five.

That future is possible. It is not guaranteed. If we do nothing, the count I gave you tonight will keep getting worse, a little every year, quietly, through hires that never happen. Until one day we look up and find a generation that never got its first rung, and a country that has run out of people who know how to do the hard things.

[PAUSE]

All night I have been asking one question. I asked it of the firms, and I asked it of all of us. Who will be senior in twenty forty?

Let me answer it.

In twenty forty, Maya will be thirty-seven.

[LET IT LAND]

Thirty-seven is about the age of the senior associate who catches the email that does not fit. The engineer who looks at a design and knows it will not hold. The manager who hears three sentences on the phone and knows what the customer really needs. Maya could be any of those people. She has the mind for it and the will for it. All she lacks is the first year.

So the answer to the question is up to us. If someone hires her, and gives her the dull work and a patient teacher, Maya will be senior in twenty forty. If no one does, that chair will be empty, and the machines will have no one to answer to.

The man from Illinois ended his speech at Cooper Union with a charge to his listeners. He did not promise them it would be easy. He told them they had a duty, and that they should do it even when they could not be sure of the outcome.

His words were these: "Let us have faith that right makes might, and in that faith, let us, to the end, dare to do our duty as we understand it."

[SLOW]

I understand our duty this way.

Maya is going to click submit again tomorrow. She is going to keep trying. She will do her part.

Our part is to make sure that, one of these days, someone on the other side of that form decides to give her a chance. Not because software scored her well. Because a person, somewhere, remembered that someone once gave them a chance, too, and understood that the future of their firm, their profession and their country depends on doing the same.

Every one of us was a beginner once.

Let us not be the generation that pulled the ladder up behind us.

Let us be the generation that kept it standing.

Thank you.
